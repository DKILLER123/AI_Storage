Chapter Eighty-Two

The Saxophone Struck Up, and Baek Si-on Took the Table

September 6 9:00 pm Venice · the garden beside the Palazzo del Cinema The last carnival of the festival, and a brass horn that would not stay off the ham

The closing dinner was a little different from the opening one.

This, after the ceremony, was the last carnival of the whole festival.

Higher spec. More people. Stronger wine.

Four white tents had been put up in the garden, the interior lights dialled very low. The tables were crowded with prosecco, red wine, every kind of Italian spirit.

Closing dinner · the garden that did not eat

Four tents · a twenty-metre buffet · nobody serious about food

Prosecco — already open, already pouring

Aperol Spritz — one Italian producer, one whole bottle, half a glass into a winner’s cup

Parma ham — and tiramisu, and focaccia already torn in half

Grappa · Limoncello · the nameless spirits — the winners were the disaster zone

Tonight, everybody drinks

The buffet ran nearly twenty metres. Tonight almost no one was eating in earnest.

Everyone was drinking.

The winners were the disaster zone.

From the first second Baek Si-on walked into the garden, he was blocked.

First came the Golden Lion himself, Roy Andersson, strolling over with a glass of red, saying one sentence — “the young man should drink” — and clinking without waiting for an answer.

Then an Italian producer whose name Baek Si-on could not call.

The man came charging in with a whole bottle of Aperol Spritz, poured half a glass into his cup, and delivered a long stretch of congratulations in English with at least six “bravo”s nested inside it.

Then three Frenchmen. One critic. Two distributors.

The critic insisted on clinking in French.

Of the two distributors, one insisted on English, the other on Korean.

The three of them argued for a minute about which language would be more respectful to the winner, and finally decided to clink once in each.

Baek Jeong-hun’s side was worse.

The Lion of the Future, at a closing dinner, enjoyed a particular privilege:

Everyone wanted to drink with you, because “investing in a genius just getting started” was always a better deal than “investing in a master already famous.”

Someone was discussing the next film with Baek Jeong-hun.

Someone was hinting they had a development fund in hand.

Someone simply wanted to clink with a Korean director, so they could go home and tell their friends, “I drank in Venice with that Asian who took the Lion of the Future.”

Baek Jeong-hun’s glass was topped up at least eight times in ten minutes.

He did not refuse.

Every glass was a potential collaboration.

On the last night in Venice, no one said no to an opportunity.

✿ ✿ ✿

Baek Si-on was holding a glass of Italian spirit someone had stuffed into his hand, very seriously mapping a urinal-escape route toward the restroom, when it happened.

Under one of the white tents.

A show-off from some crew or other, brass saxophone in hand, jumped — with no regard for anyone — straight onto the twenty-metre table laid with top-grade ham and seafood.

Garden tent · the twenty-metre table

Parma ham to the left. Tiramisu to the right. A madman with a saxophone in between.

The intro of “Can’t Take My Eyes Off You” came out of the bell.

Three seconds of silence. Then a whistle. Then applause. Then the whole garden detonated.

The instant that melody left the horn, the entire garden’s attention was sucked toward it.

Every hand that had been clinking stopped.

Every mouth that had been making small talk closed.

A few hundred pairs of eyes turned, as one, to the madman standing between the Parma ham and the tiramisu, blowing a saxophone on the dining table.

The silence lasted about three seconds.

The first whistle went.

Then applause.

Then cheering.

Then the whole garden blew.

The crowd began to pour toward the table. Some swayed with the beat. Some lifted napkins and waved them over their heads.

A German director peeled off his suit jacket and threw it in the air. It nearly hit a waiter carrying glasses in the face.

The Venice Film Festival closing dinner, in the saxophone of a show-off, officially switched from “networking” to “party.”

To be honest.

Baek Si-on was sincerely grateful to this show-off who had jumped out of nowhere.

If this improvisation had not forcibly broken the social rhythm, he would probably, by now, have been poured into vomiting by those three Frenchmen.

He swayed twice with the beat.

The saxophone was genuinely good.

Full tone, steady breath. This hyung was probably not an amateur. Odds were he was some score player who had gotten high and come to join in.

He was still swaying.

A slap landed on his back.

Baek Si-on turned.

Baek Jeong-hun.

A wireless microphone in his hand.

No idea where he had gotten it.

Possibly from the PA system the tent kept on standby.

“Si-on.”

Baek Jeong-hun held the microphone out in front of him.

“You can sing, can’t you? Get up there and show them.”

Baek Si-on looked at the microphone.

Then at the expression on Baek Jeong-hun’s face.

This was genuine drunkenness.

With the alcohol up, that parental urge to show the whole world “our kid is first on earth” could no longer be held down.

Tonight he had to let these European old-money and elites with fat chequebooks in their pockets see.

The Baeks could not only act them into tears. Standing on a table and singing, they could still pin the whole garden to the floor.

“You sure?”

“Go. Quickly.”

In the corner of his eye, the three Frenchmen seemed to have agreed on something again, and were walking toward him with glasses, faces brightly red.

Of two harms, take the lighter.

Baek Si-on took the microphone.

Anyway he had already drunk.

Anyway he was leaving tomorrow.

Anyway the people here would, in all probability, never see him a second time in this life.

The show-off with the saxophone saw the genuine, newly minted Best Actor walking out with a microphone.

His eyes lit.

Extremely professionally, he shifted half a metre to the side, vacated the main position, and pointed at Baek Si-on with the bell of the horn.

Come.

Baek Si-on put one foot on a chair and the other across onto the tabletop.

Oxfords on a white tablecloth, beside focaccia torn in half and a prosecco glass that had gone over.

He stood straight.

From this height the whole garden was in view.

The warm light of the torches. The dark-gold light spilling from the tents. A few hundred faces tipped up.

The saxophonist was just blowing the tail of the verse. The melody was climbing. The chorus was about to come in.

Baek Si-on cleared his throat.

The mild alcohol was moving in his veins. There was a layer of warm looseness at the back of his throat, the kind of thing that reason usually kept pressed down, now softened in the drink.

He lifted the microphone.

Caught the breath-point where the melody entered the chorus:

“I love you baby, and if it’s quite all right —”

The voice was not the kind that charged the high register.

It was loose, with breath in it, that particular lazy texture that only comes after a mild drunk.

The saxophonist dropped his own volume and gave the melody over.

“I need you baby —”

The first cheer in the garden detonated.

“To warm the lonely night —”

The second cheer was three times the first.

“I love you baby —”

People started singing with him.

First a few scattered voices, then a sheet of them, then everyone under a whole tent coming in together.

“Trust in me when I say —”

Baek Si-on stood on the twenty-metre dining table, microphone in his right hand, left hand hanging naturally at his side.

As he sang, he scanned the crowd.

His gaze went left to right, over the people swaying with glasses, over the German director waving a napkin, over the brand PR filming on a phone.

Found her.

At the edge of the tent.

Choi Jin-ri was standing beside a support pole, clapping with the beat.

The corner of her mouth bent.

Her eyes bent.

Looking at the man on the table, standing between the torn bread and the fallen glass, throwing off hormones without any restraint.

Baek Si-on locked on her.

Put out the index finger of his left hand.

Through the torchlight.

Through a few hundred swaying heads.

Pointed, precisely, at the figure at the edge of the tent.

“Oh pretty baby —”

Choi Jin-ri’s two hands came together at her chest and did not come down.

He was pointing at her, in front of a few hundred people, and singing.

This was not the kind of point that sweeps generically across a fan section from a stage, fan-service style.

“Oh pretty baby —”

A layer of heat rose on Choi Jin-ri’s cheeks.

Not alcohol.

She had not drunk tonight.

She took her gaze off Baek Si-on and looked down at her own fingers.

Looked for about two seconds.

Could not help lifting it again.

Baek Si-on was still pointing at her.

Still singing.

“And let me love you baby, let me love you —”

The saxophone pulled a gorgeous slide on the last long note of the chorus, then the mouth left the reed and put up a thumb at Baek Si-on.

The whole garden’s chorus, cheering, whistling and applause mixed together, rolled over the tail of the saxophone, over the roar of the sea wind, over the sound of the Adriatic hitting the shore of the Lido.

Baek Si-on took the microphone down from his mouth.

Stood on the dining table, receiving the cheers and whistles coming in from every direction.

He tipped a small bow toward the crowd, then threw his gaze toward the tent pole where Choi Jin-ri had been standing.

No one there.

Baek Si-on did not go after her.

Because he had just jumped down from the table, his heel not yet steady, and the three Frenchmen were back.

“Mon ami! I had no idea at all! Not only do you act well, you sing so — comment dire — magnifique!”

“…”

Baek Si-on drank.

He had to.

Behind the three of them came a young Nordic director who had appeared from somewhere, and the show-off who had been blowing saxophone on the table.

The saxophonist wanted to clink.

They clinked.

The Nordic director wanted to clink.

They clinked.

Another forty minutes on.

Baek Si-on found that things were starting to come in double.

His body’s metabolising speed had completely failed to keep up with the speed at which the drinks were being poured.

This would not do.

Another round and he was afraid he would be hanging in a Venice emergency room.

He put the glass down.

Made two perfunctory sentences of nonsense with the person beside him, used the restroom as a pretext, and pulled out, decisively.

September 6 late Lido · the stone railing at the far end of the garden A dark-blue skirt, two discarded heels, and a conversation that would not stay light

He went around the edge of the main tent, followed the flagstone path, and kept walking toward the outermost rim of the garden.

The deafening saxophone and the mixed voices of every language were blocked behind layers of cypress hedge, fading into an extremely thin bed of noise.

At the very end of the garden.

A row of low stone railings appeared.

Beyond the rail, a descending flight of stone steps. The bottom of the steps ran straight onto the shoreline of the Lido.

Tonight’s moonlight had nothing in its way.

Cold white light on the Adriatic, laying the whole surface of the sea into a sheet of broken silver that rose and fell with the waves.

Someone was sitting on the stone rail.

A dark-blue skirt hanging down off the rough stone, moved, very slowly, by a night wind that carried salt.

Choi Jin-ri.

She sat there sideways.

Both hands braced on the stone under her, two pale legs hanging outside the rail, swinging, extremely slowly.

The expensive heels that had been prepared to match the haute-couture gown had been taken off, extremely casually, by their owner, and lay left and right on the flagstones under the rail.

She was barefoot like that, her gaze without focus on the distant sea covered in broken silver, spacing out.

Baek Si-on stopped three steps from her.

Stood quietly for a few seconds, then walked over and sat down on the empty edge of the rail.

The sound of cloth on stone startled Choi Jin-ri.

She turned her head, looked at this person beside her with his shirt collar slightly open, still carrying the smell of wine that had not yet gone, and smiled, lightly.

“You all right?”

“No.”

Baek Si-on braced both hands behind him on the rail and tipped his head up at the moon.

“Why did you hide?”

If it had only been embarrassment from that one point on the dining table, she should have gone to find Baek Eun-ah, or gone back to the hotel room to kick the quilt.

Not sat alone on a stone rail, barefoot, looking at the sea and spacing out.

“I didn’t hide. I just wanted to be quiet for a while.”

“Something on your mind?”

She looked down at her toes hanging outside the rail, and drew a slow circle in the air.

Baek Si-on did not push.

From the distant tent came a burst of laughter. Someone was shouting something. The saxophone started again.

Half a minute on.

Choi Jin-ri opened her mouth.

“Do you know how big this island is?”

“About twelve kilometres long. At the widest, less than a kilometre.”

“Mm. A very small island. But I spent ten days here.”

“In these ten days I stepped in the water on the beach, bought a mask on the island, got pulled onto a boat at the dock, smiled on a red carpet with a Hollywood actress… cried in a screening hall, said something at a press conference that I actually wanted to say.”

“Just now I was pointed at, in front of a few hundred people, by a man standing on a dining table, and sung a… song.”

“This is probably the ten days, since the trainee years, that I have lived most like a normal person.”

“No one photographed me to put online and curse. No one in the comments discussed what ‘out-of-line’ thing I did today. No one cropped my face into a meme to prove I was ‘mentally unwell.’”

“The day after tomorrow, the second the plane lands, everything will go back to what it was, and I will have to become that again.”

She did not specify what “that” was.

But Baek Si-on understood.

The person whose smile had to watch the occasion.

The person who had to run the wording three times in her head before she spoke.

The person who had to be careful even of the rhythm of her breathing, terrified someone would screenshot it and read it.

Choi Jin-ri’s toes started to swing again.

“I know this sounds precious. So many people want to be idols and can’t, and I became one and I’m sitting here saying I’m not happy.”

“It isn’t precious.”

Baek Si-on’s voice came from beside her.

Choi Jin-ri turned to look at him.

His gaze was on the distant sea. He was silent for a while.

“Have you thought about something?”

“What?”

“The unhappiness you just talked about — does the company know? Do your members know? Do the fans know?”

Choi Jin-ri paused.

“I…”

“Have you told them? Not through an interview. Not through social media. Not through a scrap of words someone screenshotted. Sitting down, face to face, from the beginning to the end, saying it.”

Choi Jin-ri opened her mouth.

Closed it again.

The answer was obviously no.

Baek Si-on took his gaze back from the sea and looked at her.

❝

Communication is a bridge between people. You took the bridge down.

— Baek Si-on, to Choi Jin-ri, the Lido rail

“You thought if you didn’t say it, it wouldn’t cause more controversy. If you didn’t explain, you wouldn’t be twisted. If you didn’t answer, you wouldn’t be attacked. Reality is the opposite.”

“This world has too many regrets that come from not knowing how to communicate.”

When he said that sentence, there was something in his tone Choi Jin-ri could not place.

As if he were talking about her.

And as if he were talking about something else.

“The company. The members. Even the fans. You have to try talking with them. Say what is in your head.”

He looked at Choi Jin-ri’s eyes.

“This is not being precious. This is your basic right as a person. What’s precious are the people who won’t even listen before they pass judgment. Not you.”

Night wind came off the sea and blew a few strands of hair from Choi Jin-ri’s shoulder onto the side of her face.

She did not lift a hand to brush them away.

Quiet for a long time.

Long enough that the saxophone in the distant tent changed songs.

“What if I say it, and they still don’t understand?”

“Then at least you tried. If you tried and still aren’t understood, that’s their problem. Your part is already done.”

“But if you don’t even try, you will never know the answer in this life. You will keep guessing. Guessing whether they dislike you. Guessing whether they did it on purpose. Guessing whether this world really cannot hold a Choi Jin-ri who is a little different from other people.”

“Guess to the end, and you will guess yourself into a dead end.”

“…”

A thin wetness gathered slowly at the bottom of Choi Jin-ri’s eyes.

It did not spill.

She sniffed.

Took her gaze back from the sea, turned her head, and looked at Baek Si-on beside her.

“You’re talking a lot today.”

Baek Si-on had, in fact, said a great deal.

Perhaps it was the alcohol.

And perhaps not entirely.

He did not answer that sentence of Choi Jin-ri’s.

Pushed himself up off the stone rail, and stood.

Turned, and walked.

The steps looked reasonably steady.

Choi Jin-ri sat on the rail, looking after his back.

One step. Two. Three.

On the fourth, that figure suddenly listed left, then the whole of his weight tipped forward.

His right foot tried to take a step and catch it.

Did not catch it.

Thud.

“Baek Si-on!”

Choi Jin-ri ran over barefoot. The soles of her feet hurt on the rough flagstones.

She did not care.

Baek Si-on’s posture at this moment was like someone starting an unstandard push-up and giving up halfway.

Choi Jin-ri crouched.

Both hands went out, caught his left shoulder and the side of his waist, and tried to roll him over.

Seven-tenths of her strength.

He did not move.

This person’s weight was much heavier than he looked.

The density of muscle accumulated in the gym, plus the weight of the frame itself — lying on the ground he was like a block of concrete poured in place.

Choi Jin-ri bit down, changed the angle, both hands under his right shoulder, knee against the side of his waist as a fulcrum.

Second try.

Full strength.

Baek Si-on’s body listed about thirty degrees, then collapsed back.

Choi Jin-ri caught a breath.

A Dior evening gown was obviously not designed for the working condition of “rolling an eighty-plus-kilo drunk on flagstones.”

She simply yanked the whole skirt up above her thighs, the entire calf out, and stopped caring.

Third try.

She dropped her centre of gravity, both hands through under his right shoulder, palms locked on his chest, knee jammed against the side of his waist, abdomen firing at the same time.

Baek Si-on’s body finally rolled over.

Face to the sky.

Choi Jin-ri’s two hands were still braced on either side of his chest, her whole body down over him, breathing in large gulps.

She looked down at his face.

Eyes closed.

Mouth slightly open.

A small wet patch on the stone by his cheek, sweat or drool, unknown.

Choi Jin-ri put her index and middle fingers together and held them under his nostrils.

Warm breath hit her fingertips, evenly.

Not a faint. Not a cardiac arrest. Just drunk, and asleep.

Choi Jin-ri’s shoulders dropped, hard.

She took her hand back from under his nose and pressed it to her own chest, feeling her heart still hitting her ribs.

About ten seconds.

The breathing evened a little.

Then she looked at this newly minted Venice Best Actor with what appeared to be drool at the corner of his mouth.

…

Try carrying him.

Choi Jin-ri crouched beside him, pulled his left arm over her shoulder, both arms around his waist, and drew a deep breath.

Ready.

One.

Two.

Three.

Her thigh muscles went to the limit. Her waist bent to the limit.

Baek Si-on’s upper body was pulled up about twenty centimetres.

But because she was not wearing shoes, her bare feet slipped on the flagstones.

The two of them nearly went down together.

Choi Jin-ri hurriedly put him down.

The back of Baek Si-on’s head knocked the stone.

“…”

She looked at his face.

Still asleep.

The expression was even a little more peaceful than before.

It should… be fine, right?

She looked back toward the rail. Her phone was still over there. She picked up her skirt and jogged.

Took the phone from her clutch, opened KakaoTalk, found Baek Eun-ah’s thread.

Choi Jin-ri gripped the phone and jogged back, skirt in hand.

Moonlight came down from above.

Soaked the whole of Baek Si-on in cold white light.

Choi Jin-ri stood beside him for a while.

Then sat down.

The Dior skirt spread on the rough flagstones. It would probably scuff. It would probably leave a mark.

She did not care.

She lifted Baek Si-on’s head, lightly, and put it on her knee, the way she had in that scene by the Han River a few months ago.

Chapter Thirty-Six

The Split, the Release, and the Light That Turned Red

July Morning LOEN Entertainment The contract, and the numbers that came out of it

Bae Jong-han laid the contract on the table in the small, glass-walled conference room and slid it across to Baek Si-on.

Three pages. LOEN's standard digital-distribution agreement, read line by line by two lawyers and one suspicious songwriters' representative. At the bottom, in the box where the revenue percentage went, someone had written a split in pencil that made Si-on look up.

“Thirty-seventy?”

“Company takes the thirty. You take the seventy.”

Bae Jong-han said it plainly, the way a man reads a weather report that has already gone his way. He was good at reading those.

“That's not standard.”

“It isn't. But this is a one-song release with no artist contract, no advance, and no distribution commitment from us beyond the platform. The publishing is thrice-partitioned when you count the writer's and the performer's halves, so the arithmetic doesn't favour us, and we both know it.”

“So why offer it?”

“Because the song's good,” Bae Jong-han said, “and because the person singing it is the one I was told runs on a debt collector's ledger. I don't suppose you'd tell me if I'm right.”

Si-on picked up the pen. He didn't say anything, but he didn't need to. The figure in pencil — the net that, after the composer's share and the performer's share had been carved out and the platform's cut skimmed off the top, left him with roughly forty-seven points of everything the song would ever earn — sat in front of him like a door he'd been looking for all summer.

He signed.

Digital distribution · single · one

Song: “Way Back Home” · lyricist Lee Ji-eun · producer Jung Jae-joon · vocal Baek Si-on

Revenue split: LOEN 30 : artist 70

Net to Baek Si-on, post playwright + performer + platform: ≈ 47 pts

No advance. No artist contract. One song, one door.

July 7 Noon COEX, the rollout stage The day the song went up

July seventh. The song went up on Melon at noon, and for the first three hours nothing much happened. A track with no artist name behind it, no M/V, no teaser photos, no anticipation — released quietly into the middle of the summer chart like a coin dropped into a wishing well no one else could see.

Si-on watched the numbers crawl from the sofa in Baek Jeong-hun's studio. The stream count ticked up the way a kettle warms: slowly, then a little less slowly, then not at all.

“This is the part where they wait,” Jae-joon said, not looking away from his own monitor. “A song doesn't move on the day. It moves after.”

“And if it doesn't?”

“After.”

Then, by late evening, something stirred. A user under the name of a long-time Uaena posted in a fan café thread a single screenshot, blown up and circled in red: the credits on the LOEN release page.

The thread went quiet for a beat, the way a crowd does before it makes up its mind. Then it caught.

July Evening MBC, the music-show stage The comeback stage everyone kept their eyes on

Across town that same week, f(x) was making its own noise — the kind with spotlights on it.

The comeback stage for Red Light had been hyped for a month. The song was a switch thrown hard: a darker, denser, more electronic turn than anyone expected from the group the company had once called an experiment, and the set they built for it — red haze, strobes in a slow pulse, five silhouettes coming in and out of the light — was designed to look like a building you weren't sure you were allowed to be inside.

Si-on wasn't there for the performance. He was on the sofa in Hapjeong-dong, and he watched it the way the rest of the country did, on a phone screen propped against a cup of iced coffee.

★ f(x) · Red Light · comeback stage

Red haze. A strobe that doesn't blink, it breathes. Five figures, then four.

The camera cuts to the line as the intro breaks — and there's a gap in it.

“Stop it out … the fire in the dark …”

The gap is where one silhouette should be. The one the media has been calling a slacker for three years.

The performance was clean. Sharp. Krystal's lines landed like they'd been filed to a point, Victoria held the centre with the calm that made the whole thing hold, and the two of them covered the middle with the kind of seamless professionalism the company had built on. Luna's voice took the top of the chorus and did not wobble.

It was, by any honest measure, a strong stage.

And it still ended the same way every f(x) stage ended that year: with the comment sections igniting under the word lazy, aimed at the one silhouette everyone had noticed missing, the one the camera kept almost finding, the one who'd built this song and then stood behind it while the others danced in the light.

Si-on scrolled for a long moment, thumb moving slowly, then switched the phone off and set it face-down.

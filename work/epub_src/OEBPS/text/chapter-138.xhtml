Chapter One Hundred Thirty Eight

The Whole World Makes Way for His Schedule

November 7 3:00 a.m. Seoul · Hapjeong 401 Two cans of iced americano, one studio lit for a man who does not need to be told how to be a king.

Three in the morning.

Hapjeong, past Hongdae.

When Baek Si-on pushed the door open, Jung Jae-joon was sitting at the computer, two cans of ready-made iced americano at his elbow.

Seeing him, Jae-joon lifted his chin.

“The prince has arrived.”

“Mm.”

“Which one first?”

“King.”

Jae-joon nodded and opened the session file directly.

He had grown numb to this kind of unscheduled overtime by now.

Small potatoes.

Baek Si-on walked into the booth and adjusted the microphone height.

Warming up.

Climbing from the low register upward, notch by notch, his vocal cords moving from a cold start into working temperature.

The session package from Scooter’s team had already been downloaded. Stems, demo, reference vocal, lyric annotations — everything sorted into folders, clean and legible.

This recording session really was simpler than the ones before it.

There was homework to copy.

The arrangement was already built.

The structure was already clear.

All he had to do was put his own voice into it.

Take one.

Jae-joon hit pause.

“Again.”

“What’s wrong with it?”

“You’re acting again.”

Baek Si-on: “……”

“That ‘ruling from the throne—’ line just now. You sang it like you were playing a man who rules from a throne.”

Jae-joon looked at him from behind the console.

“You are that man. No need to act him.”

Baek Si-on was quiet for two seconds.

“……Understood.”

Take two.

Take three.

Take four passed.

The chorus was stacked two layers deep. One layer of harmonic chopping added on the bridge — a treatment logic close to Bones, only leaner, because the song itself wanted arrogance, not decoration; there was no gap that needed filling with technique.

Just past four.

Every usable piece of master material for King had been recorded.

“Next.”

“Human.”

Baek Si-on rolled his neck once.

“Let’s go.”

Human went faster than King.

Because it shared a bloodline with Way Down We Go — the same low blues-rock root, and Baek Si-on had already sung Way Down We Go. The muscle memory was already in his throat.

Blues at the root.

Rock at the shell.

And in the middle, the stomping beat.

What it sang was: I’m only human.

Baek Si-on barely adjusted between the first two takes.

Take three passed.

Then he added the harmonies and a few tail notes on top.

By the time they were done, through the little window of the booth, the Hongdae sky had begun to lighten.

Seoul was not fully awake yet, but the traffic on the road was already thickening.

Baek Eun-ah checked the time and warned him at once:

“Oppa. Time to go.”

“Traffic?”

“Mm. Any later and it’s the morning rush. That’s an extra hour back to Jeonju.”

Jae-joon looked at the session list.

“What about Sign of the Times?”

Baek Si-on was quiet for a moment.

“Tonight.”

That song was not right for now.

Sign of the Times was the track on the whole Human album that demanded the most from the voice and from emotional control.

It was not a song you could overpower with presence.

Nor one you could carry on rhythm alone.

That grief — helpless, restrained, the hollow falling sensation — required the body to be quiet. It required a clean throat. And it required a head with no crown still on it from having just recorded King.

Jae-joon understood.

“Fine. Come back tonight.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Thanks for the work.”

Jae-joon waved a hand.

“Spare me. Go.”

In the old days, recording, Jae-joon would still have said he was only pressing a few keys. Now they were familiar enough that he had dropped the courtesy entirely.

✿ ✿ ✿

November 7 · that night After the wrap · again until near dawn Seoul · Hapjeong 401 · the light that never seems to go out Piano, water, and the kind of silence that records better than sound.

After the day’s shooting wrapped, Baek Si-on drove back to Seoul again.

The light in the studio at Hapjeong 401 stayed on for him until dawn, once more.

This time: Sign of the Times.

No jokes.

No wasted words.

Jae-joon dimmed the lights, leaving only one small lamp in front of the console.

When the piano introduction began, the booth went as quiet as a deep sea at night.

Baek Si-on closed his eyes.

This time, he did not think about Billboard.

Not about Spotify.

Not about King.

He thought about water.

About silence.

About the people who never got to hear an answer.

Take one finished. Jae-joon said nothing.

Take two — the tail notes.

Take three — the breaths.

Some songs needed a dozen takes before you found the door. Some songs told you the direction in three or four.

Sign of the Times was the second kind.

It was hard.

But tonight, Baek Si-on’s state happened to be exactly right.

At four in the morning, the last harmony was finished.

Jae-joon pressed save.

“Done.”

He said it.

“The whole album’s vocals — complete.”

Baek Eun-ah was on the sofa, so sleep-deprived her eyes had gone out of focus. Even so, the words snapped her head up.

“Really?”

Jae-joon packed the session files the way Scooter’s team had asked.

King.

Human.

Sign of the Times.

Added to the tracks already finished before them.

The vocal recording for the entire Human album — complete.

He opened the mail client. Attachment. The progress bar crept forward.

Sent.

Jae-joon leaned back in his chair and let out a long breath.

“Done. Over to the Americans to torture the mix engineers.”

Baek Eun-ah raised a hand with her eyes closed.

“Seconded.”

Baek Si-on picked up his coat.

“Thanks. Bill it under the studio contract.”

Jae-joon laughed.

“The boss talks more and more like a capitalist.”

“I’m just respecting labour.”

“That sounds even more like a line a capitalist would rehearse.”

“……”

Baek Si-on did not keep trading barbs with him.

He was, in fact, tired.

✿ ✿ ✿

On the road back to Jeonju, the rear cabin of the Grand Starex was dimmed almost to nothing.

Baek Si-on lay on the reclined airline seat, coat draped over him.

Outside the window, the motorway lights slid backward, one after another.

He had to admit it: this high-roof luxury nanny van from Hyundai was leagues ahead of the old second-hand Carnival.

Back in the Carnival days, whenever they hit a speed bump, Baek Eun-ah used to suspect her soul was going to bounce out through the roof.

Not anymore.

The fact that Baek Si-on was asleep within a minute of lying down — that was proof enough.

✿ ✿ ✿

November 8 From late morning, the sky turns difficult Jeonju · Gyeonggijeon Shrine · the Sado set A scene that eats light. A ceremony that cannot wait. And the world, this once, deciding to make way.

Around midday, the Jeonju sky started to misbehave.

The clean sunlight was slowly swallowed by a layer of thin cloud, and the shadows beneath the eaves of Gyeonggijeon shifted accordingly.

Today’s scene happened to need the light more than any other.

Light enlarged every trace of fatigue on an actor’s face — every suppressed grievance, and that particular contortion of a Crown Prince Sado who was nearly shattered but still had to hold the dignity of his rank.

By the original shooting plan, Lee Joon-ik had promised to release Baek Si-on by noon.

Today was the fifth Korean Popular Culture and Arts Awards.

This kind of prize was not like the year-end ceremonies.

At the Blue Dragon Awards, the Golden Discs, the MAMAs, being late or absent was at most a schedule conflict, and the press would write two lines about it and move on.

The Korean Popular Culture and Arts Awards was different.

Behind it stood the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism. A national seal of approval, at state level.

To put it plainly: it was the government’s face.

Whoever played the diva there would have the headline wrapped around his neck from the news pages all the way down to his ancestors’ graves by the next morning.

And yet, as they shot, things began to go wrong.

First, an extra’s hand shook as he served tea to Song Kang-ho’s Yeongjo. The porcelain cup struck the table with a clear ring.

That take was dead.

Again.

Then the cloud layer pressed down and the light flickered, and the director of photography frowned at the monitor — the exposure ratio between the first and second halves of the take jumped too clearly.

Another take dead.

Then Song Kang-ho shifted the landing point of one line.

Not the words. The pause.

This was the terrible thing about a master actor: he did not need to change much. Move a single pause by half a beat, and the whole weight of the scene had to be re-balanced.

Lee Joon-ik thought it was better.

Baek Si-on thought so too.

So — again.

And the most fatal cut of all was the one Baek Si-on inflicted on himself.

The take just now had been passable.

The assistant director was already reading the director’s face, ready to call the change-over.

Then Baek Si-on watched the replay and said one thing:

“I want to keep one that’s better.”

The assistant director glanced at the time, and his scalp began to tighten.

“Director, why don’t we just take this one and—”

Lee Joon-ik shook his head.

“He’s right. It’s a little off.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

What she hated most, right now, was the speed with which these people who took cinema seriously could always reach a consensus about art.

Art was satisfied.

Her blood pressure was not.

Time kept grinding forward.

Baek Eun-ah was close to taking off on the spot.

She stood beside the monitor — tablet in one hand, schedule in the other — eyes locked on the set, the toe of one shoe tapping the ground in a steady rhythm.

She did not dare call a halt.

She did not dare disturb Lee Joon-ik.

She certainly did not dare sidle up to Baek Si-on and whisper, “Oppa, we really should be going.”

Because she knew.

This was the scene where Crown Prince Sado’s emotional collapse began — the hinge of the entire film.

Break that, and there would be no picking it up again today.

But she also knew.

The ceremony hall was already running its sequence.

The host was memorising lines. The staff were checking the guest seats. Officials from the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism had already entered.

Baek Si-on’s name card sat dead centre of the first row.

Empty.

The broadcast director’s camera swept across that spot every ten seconds.

And then Baek Eun-ah remembered something.

She opened her contacts.

SK.

Yes. SK.

Other companies signed an ambassador, then shot commercials, bought media, ran campaigns.

Not SK.

SK looked at Baek Si-on the way a man looks at his own global-roaming business having cultivated itself into a spirit and grown a face at last.

And since the Lakers opener — when everyone else held back and they stood up for him — they had acted as if they wanted to stick the words “connecting the world” directly onto his forehead.

In that case.

It should not stop at copywriting.

Time to bring out the hardware.

Baek Eun-ah stepped aside and made a call, keeping her voice low.

Baek Eun-ah → SK Telecom managing director · Gyeonggijeon set · outgoing

“Hello — Managing director-nim. This is Baek Eun-ah, Agent Baek Si-on’s manager.”

“We’re at the Gyeonggijeon set in Jeonju right now, and today’s the Korean Popular Culture and Arts Awards—”

There was a silence on the other end for a moment. Then one question only:

“How fast do you need it?”

Baek Eun-ah looked up at the sky.

“As fast as possible.”

✿ ✿ ✿

Forty minutes later, a low rumble began to build over the set.

First in the distance.

Then closer.

Crew members preparing the next take looked up one by one.

The extras were confused.

“What’s that sound?”

“A plane?”

“Where’s it coming from?”

Lee Joon-ik raised his eyes from the monitor.

Song Kang-ho turned to look as well.

The next second —

A helicopter came circling overhead. Its fuselage was painted silver-white. Under its belly hung the vivid blue SK Telecom logo. On its tail, a large “T” rendered as a signal bar.

The wind-pressure arrived first.

Dust, leaves and prop cloths went flying in every direction.

A grip flattened himself over a reflector board, nearly lifted off his feet. The wardrobe team clung to their rack. The extras covered their heads while looking up, each face blanker than the last.

In the middle of the general chaos, Baek Si-on stood where he was, watching the SK helicopter hunting for a landing spot above the set.

He was silent for two seconds.

Then he turned to Baek Eun-ah.

“You did this.”

Baek Eun-ah pushed the hair the wind had blown across her face out of the way.

“Oppa.”

“Mm?”

“A state seal can’t be late.”

Baek Si-on said nothing.

He just looked at her.

Baek Eun-ah, growing a little defensive under the look, added:

“Also, SK said the brand value is achieving three-dimensional, multi-scenario linkage.”

The assistant director, overhearing, could not help but interrupt:

“Translate, please.”

Baek Eun-ah, expression unchanged:

“They want to be on the news too.”

“……”

The set went quiet for a moment.

Then someone failed to hold in a laugh.

That one laugh threw the switch: grips, wardrobe, props, extras — they all started laughing.

The only one who could not laugh was Park Ji-hun.

He looked up at the helicopter still circling overhead, then down at the complete Crown Prince Sado costume on Baek Si-on.

The headdress.

The wig.

The inner robe.

The outer robe.

The belt.

The shoes.

And a pile of small details he had spent over half an hour arranging just now.

Park Ji-hun went completely numb.

“Wait.”

He turned to Baek Eun-ah:

“I’m supposed to do his full costume-and-makeup change on that thing?”

Baek Eun-ah looked at him.

“Can you?”

Park Ji-hun looked up at the helicopter with the SK Telecom logo, then back at Baek Si-on.

“I can.”

Baek Eun-ah’s eyes lit up.

The next second, Park Ji-hun added:

“Provided he’s willing to appear naked over Seoul.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

Baek Si-on himself, by contrast, was perfectly calm.

“Can’t change there?”

Park Ji-hun was honest.

“Not unless that plane has a full dressing room on board, two assistants, a secured clothes rack, wind clips — and a mental environment in which I am not terrified of being killed by the rotors.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Understood.”

Baek Eun-ah was getting anxious.

“Then what do we do?”

She looked at her watch.

Leave any later and even a working helicopter would turn the official schedule into an accident.

At that moment, Song Kang-ho, who had been enjoying the show from the side, spoke up.

“Go in this, then.”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

Song Kang-ho pointed at the prince’s robes he was wearing.

“They’re giving you an actor’s award today, aren’t they? Then go wearing an actor’s clothes.”

Lee Joon-ik agreed:

“Propriety isn’t only a suit. If you walked into that hall like this, the people at the Ministry would probably be happier than we are.”

Baek Si-on had his doubts.

After all, an awards ceremony was a formal occasion.

If he showed up in costume and nobody framed it for him, it would be very easy to be criticised — for using an official ceremony to promote a film.

But Song Kang-ho and Lee Joon-ik had both spoken.

That changed everything.

One sentence from his seniors rewrote “the embarrassment of not having time to change” into “an actor receiving the salute of his era in the person of his character.”

That was the entertainment world for you.

The same act, with no one to vouch for it, was bad manners.

With someone to vouch for it, it was a beautiful story.

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Fine.”

Park Ji-hun switched into working mode at once.

“Then I’ll only touch up the makeup and tidy the headdress and hems.”

He looked at Baek Eun-ah.

“Give me three minutes.”

“Three minutes?”

“Can’t do less.”

Baek Eun-ah checked the time on her phone.

“I’ll give you two and a half.”

Park Ji-hun: “……”

Right now, he wanted to resign.

But then he remembered that Baek Si-on had just won two straight weeks of Billboard number ones, and that there might be more red carpets, more ceremonies and more money ahead — and quietly pushed the resignation letter back into the drawer of his imagination.

The greatest maturity of an adult was doing the arithmetic of the salary before losing his mind.

Over on the other side, the assistant director was already running for the parking lot, radio in hand.

“Grips! Clear the west side of the parking lot!”

“Move the prop truck first!”

“Extras, don’t go over to watch — rotor wash is dangerous!”

“Whoever owns that reflector, put it down! It’s about to fly to the Blue House!”

The set burst into motion at once.

The helicopter made one more circuit overhead, then sank toward the parking lot outside Gyeonggijeon.

The rotor wash churned the grey light under the cloud layer into disorder.

The crew held their hats, clutched equipment cases — a period-drama crew who had been swept, mid-shot, into a disaster film.

Baek Si-on stood still while Park Ji-hun pressed powder on him, muttering as he worked the sponge quickly across his nose and under his eyes:

“An actor reaching your level is a rare thing, you know.”

“Other people walk the red carpet.”

“You take a helicopter so the nation can stamp you.”

Baek Si-on watched the helicopter settling in the distance.

“I didn’t call it.”

“I know.”

Park Ji-hun glanced at Baek Eun-ah beside them.

“Your family. Everyone knows their role.”

Baek Eun-ah pretended not to hear.

She was confirming the ground transfer plan with the SK side.

“Yes — can we land somewhere around Yeouido?”

“No?”

“Then what’s the nearest spot?”

“Jamsil?”

“How long from Jamsil to the venue?”

“A police escort?”

At that last phrase, Baek Eun-ah finally let out a whole breath.

Good.

Now it was not only SK that wanted to be on the news.

The police were getting involved too.

This story was going to be unavoidable.

In the parking lot, the helicopter had set down steadily.

The door opened. A man in an SK uniform, headset on, stooped as he came to meet them.

The wind had not fully died. The roar of the rotors forced everyone to shout.

“Baek Si-on-ssi!”

“The route is approved!”

“We’ll compress the flight time from Jeonju to Seoul as much as we can!”

“Estimated arrival — forty-five minutes!”

Baek Eun-ah turned at once.

“Oppa! On board!”

Baek Si-on turned and bowed to Lee Joon-ik.

“Sorry for the trouble, everyone.”

Lee Joon-ik waved a hand.

“Spare me.”

He paused, then glanced up, almost against his will, at the still-thundering helicopter.

“Next time you want to keep a better take, tell me in advance whether you have air support.”

The parking lot dissolved into laughter.

Baek Si-on smiled a little too.

It was plain to see he was in a good mood today.

Baek Eun-ah had everything packed. She turned back to urge him:

“Oppa, hurry!”

“Coming.”

Baek Si-on turned and walked toward the helicopter.

Behind him, the old buildings of Gyeonggijeon, the extras, the cameras, the reflectors, the palace lanterns, and the leaves scattered across the ground by the wind — like a historical drama abruptly paused.

Ahead of him: the clean, violent efficiency of a modern commercial society, wearing the SK logo.

If Crown Prince Sado himself could see this from below the ground, he would probably conclude that the later generations had gone even madder than he ever did.

The S4 World Championship Opens

October 16 9:40 a.m. Kim & Chang conference room, Seoul A studio on paper. One name, two cousins, and a plan with a tax number.

Baek Si-on sat on one side of the long table, an iced americano in his hand.

Baek Eun-ah sat beside him, her tablet open in front of her next to a notebook, two printed copies of an incorporation plan, and a ballpoint pen whose cap she had already worried down to a row of dents.

Seo Eun-ju sat across from them, a thick folder on the table in front of her. On its cover, in neat print:

Kim & Chang · Seoul · private client desk

Baek Si-on — Personal Studio · Incorporation Plan

File ref BS-1016-01 · prepared by Seo Eun-ju · for signature this session

The entity

A one-man personal studio, owned outright. Every cheque the name earns — performance fees, royalties, likeness money — stops first at this door, and only then becomes the income of a man.

Ownership

Baek Si-on — one hundred percent. No backers, no silent partners, no board to charm. The cleanest capital table in Korean entertainment is a table set for one.

Business functions

Contracting and billing · music production · rights management · artist management · staffing · styling and studio costs · overseas licence receipts · day-to-day operations.

Why now

Income that outgrows the individual bracket needs a legal spine. The tax office reads a fast-rising artist the way a hawk reads a rabbit — and it is already flying.

One signature, and the money stops belonging to a celebrity and starts belonging to a company with an address, a stamp and an auditor.

Eun-ah looked at that title and felt something very strange. A few months ago they had been a fly-by-night crew. Baek Si-on took the jobs himself; she trailed him keeping the schedule; Park Ji-hun lugged the makeup case and doubled as the driver; even the van was the second-hand Kia Carnival she had bought. Now they were founding a personal studio. Turning the thing that had been held together by favours, ad-hoc scheduling and Excel spreadsheets into an entity that could be audited, inspected by the tax office, sign contracts, employ people and run itself.

Seo Eun-ju turned to the first page.

“In short.”

“Baek Si-on’s income structure this year has outgrown being handled purely as personal income.”

She wrote the income lines on the whiteboard.

“If all of this is received in a personal capacity, the tax pressure is heavy, and the expense side is a mess.”

“Styling costs, studio production fees, overseas travel, staff wages, vehicle costs, legal fees, promotional materials — a lot of it is still being moved through ad-hoc accounts.”

She looked at Baek Si-on.

“Fine in the short term. In the long term, it will break.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Mm.”

Seo Eun-ju had grown used to that reaction from him. She went on.

“So I recommend a personal studio. The studio becomes the corporate entity that takes on Baek Si-on’s performance activity, music production, rights management, staffing and day-to-day operations.”

Eun-ah bent her head and took notes.

Seo Eun-ju flipped to the next page.

“The ownership structure is simple. Baek Si-on holds one hundred percent.”

Eun-ah looked up.

“What about me?”

Seo Eun-ju regarded her.

“You’re an employee.”

Silence.

She had known that was the answer. Even so, hearing the words “you’re an employee” land on her, Eun-ah felt something complicated.

Baek Si-on glanced at her.

“Want shares?”

Eun-ah shook her head at once.

“No.”

She knew she wasn’t there yet. The studio was Baek Si-on’s personal vehicle; one hundred percent his was the cleanest arrangement. When she could genuinely carry a line of business herself, they would talk about other things. Taking shares now was not a reward. It was a liability.

Seo Eun-ju nodded.

“For personnel, we don’t need to be elaborate at the start.”

She wrote several lines on the whiteboard.

Seo Eun-ju looked at Baek Si-on as well.

Baek Si-on thought about it.

“Must accept irregular hours. No accident record. No bad habits. Signs an NDA. Pay can be on the high side…”

He paused.

“Just not too talkative.”

Eun-ah looked up.

“Oppa, that requirement sounds like you’re looking for a monk who happens to know how to drive.”

“Safety.”

Silence.

“What’s the company name?”

Seo Eun-ju turned to another page, pen ready.

“S.W Studio.”

“Short for you?”

“Right.”

Seo Eun-ju wrote the letters down. Eun-ah stared at them for a moment.

“Oppa, isn’t this name a bit too simple?”

“Easy to remember.”

Eun-ah: “…”.

Fine. The personal studio of a Venice Best Actor could probably be called “Baek Si-on’s Little Hut of Happiness” and nobody would raise an objection.

Seo Eun-ju did not join in the cousins’ little exchange; she quickly filled the name into the form. What followed was pure process. Application for incorporation. Corporate registration. Scope of business. Seal registration. Tax registration. Corporate bank account. Tax-and-accounting agency appointment. Template employment contracts. Artist income assignment contracts. “I’m in your debt.”

“No debt.”

Seo Eun-ju gave him a look.

“This is a paid service.”

“Mm.”

Eun-ah suddenly felt that the two of them, in a certain sense, suited each other very well.

At 11:20 the initial review of the documents was done. Eun-ah signed her name as handler on the last page; Baek Si-on signed the formation mandate. Seo Eun-ju swept the pages into the folder, stood, and shook each of their hands. Another appointment noted, another wall between an artist’s money and the audit of it built in neat paragraphs.

October 16 4:00 p.m. Studio 401, Hapjeong-dong · a temporary interview room Twenty-odd applicants for two kinds of silence.

That afternoon, Jung Jae-joon’s studio was commandeered as an interview venue. Park Ji-hun was drafted to play doorman, walking the applicants in one by one.

Driver interviews. Requirements: skilled driving, quiet, flexible hours, tight lips.

Security interviews. Requirements: sound body, fast reflexes, unremarkable, able to become a shadow.

S.W Studio · position requisitions · 16 October 2014

Position 01 · Driver — skilled driving · irregular hours mandatory · no accident record · no bad habits · signs an NDA · pay on the high side · not too talkative

Position 02 · Bodyguard — sound body · fast reflexes · unremarkable · able to become a shadow

Shared conditions — discretion absolute · employment and overtime to the labour standard · contracts executed on studio registration

The monk who happens to know how to drive was always going to be a short list of one.

More than twenty people came through. Most were retired athletes, former special-forces operators, or career bodyguards who had left security firms. By the end, the initial shortlist was settled.

The driver was surnamed Jo. He had worked as an entertainment manager before, and knew Seoul’s map cold.

Two bodyguards were kept. One, surnamed Kang, was a retired judoka with the unmistakable cauliflower ears of a lifetime on the mat. One, surnamed Han, was a discharged operator of the 707th Special Mission Group. Background checks were run by the security firm and by Seo Eun-ju’s side at the same time. The NDAs, employment contracts, pay structure and overtime compensation would all be signed formally once the studio’s corporate registration was complete.

October 19 10:00 a.m. Sangam World Cup Stadium · dress rehearsal Sixty-six thousand empty seats, and the sound of a war drum with no one yet to aim at.

Seoul. Sangam World Cup Stadium.

Ten in the morning, and everyone ran the full rehearsal in makeup and costume. There were no spectators in the bowl yet, but sixty-six thousand open seats were already enough to produce a strange kind of illusion.

In front of the main stage, more than forty Korean traditional drummers had split into a red team and a blue team, standing at either wing of the stage. The moment they began to play, the low frequencies spread outward along the turf and the empty stands hurled the sound back, building an echo almost like a battlefield. Baek Si-on stood on the lift, and in his in-ear monitor Daniel’s voice ran the countdown. The drumming quickened. The opening drum pattern of Legend locked in. The lift rose; Si-on stepped onto the platform. The first line came out, and the sound engineer immediately raised a hand to signal the mains were clear.

The eight bars before the chorus. Bangtan cut in from behind the drummers. Red Velvet’s half-time set was also walked through once. Lights, blocking, broadcast positions, host scripts, player walkouts, sponsor-logo exposure — everything was pressed against the timeline.

The drum corps ran the battle twice — once for the cameras, once for the echo. The bowl kept every decibel and handed it back doubled.

By 12:20 the rehearsal was over and everyone went into standby.

October 19 1:40 p.m. Backstage corridor, Sangam World Cup Stadium A machine already lit, a moment from firing.

By 1:40 the backstage corridor had come alive. Crew wheeled equipment cases past at a clip. Sponsor representatives with lanyards were being led toward the VIP section by the executive director. Journalists were held behind the line at the designated press area. Casters, team managers, coaches, interpreters, makeup artists and choreographers shuttled along the narrow passages, and the sound of headsets, footsteps, English, Korean and Chinese all ran together. The whole backstage was a machine that had been ignited and was about to go off.

Baek Si-on came out of the dressing room. Eun-ah made to follow, and he waved a hand.

“I’m going over to Bangtan.”

“Want me along?”

“No need.”

He walked to the door of the next holding room and knocked. The door opened — and all seven men inside stood at once, almost in the same movement.

“Sunbaenim!”

Same angle. Same volume.

Baek Si-on did not let them hold the bow long.

“Dance well out there in a bit.”

“Yes!”

Seven voices, together.

Baek Si-on nodded once.

Then he said:

“After the performance, two million won each.”

The room went still for one second. Kim Nam-joon blinked. Kim Seok-jin’s mouth opened a little. Kim Tae-hyung looked as if he had not heard correctly. Jeon Jung-kook recovered first, his eyes simply lighting up.

Baek Si-on went on:

“Collect it from Agent Baek.”

With that he turned and left, leaving seven paralysed young men behind him in the doorway.

Three seconds later the holding room detonated.

October 19 1:55 p.m. Beneath the stage · the lift platform Ten. Nine. Eight. The sea, on the other side of the deck.

Under the stage, in the lift area, Baek Si-on stood on the narrow mechanical platform. Above the trapdoor came the muffled drone of the stadium — like a sea, beat after beat washing against the stage structure. On the big screen the highlight reel was playing; every kill, every teamfight, every cut to a player brought a fresh surge of screaming from the crowd.

Daniel’s voice came through the in-ear.

“Standby. Thirty seconds.”

Outside, the highlight reel entered its final stretch. On the screen, the logos of the two finalists. The stands began to chant in rhythm. Korean. Chinese. English. All mixed into a single roar that no language could still separate.

The great Korean drum was struck. More than forty traditional drummers, red team and blue team at either wing of the stage, mallets raised high. A second strike. A third. The drumming climbed from low to high, slow to fast, for a full ninety seconds. The rhythm tightened and tightened; red and blue played against each other like opposing lines, the sound colliding from both flanks until it all poured into the centre of the stage.

Daniel’s voice rode the countdown in the in-ear.

“Lift going up in five… four… three…”

On the stage, the traditional drumming began to accelerate wildly, denser, more urgent. At the exact instant the drumming hit its summit:

“Two… one… go!”

The lift surged into motion. Baek Si-on rose slowly through the centre of the stage. Beside him, the lead drummer, after a final strike, handed over the mallets in one flowing gesture. Baek Si-on took them with one hand. Every camera cut to him at once. Black leather jacket. Silver chain. Studded boots. Mallets in hand.

Baek Si-on read the whole thing and, privately, did the arithmetic of irony. He remembered exactly how Eun-ah had left SM — a marginalized trainee, staring down a training-debt bill that was anything but small, and the debt had been settled the day he had set a cash bag on a table, physically, with a sound. Now she represented S.W Studio and was sending ultimatums to her former employer as the paying side of the table. The sensation of that reversal was probably difficult to put into words.

On her side of the screen, Eun-ah laughed like a shiba inu discovering a promotion. The joy of borrowing a tiger’s shadow was, apparently, better for the body than a bonus. A former trainee who had once walked SM’s corridors looking at the floor now watched the PR department report schedules to her with both hands. This was what power smelled like.

She had become thorough. If nobody pre-loaded a respectable excuse, the day a high-vote act failed to show, the studio would be fried as manipulators in the first hour. That they were, in fact, manipulating was beside the point. The public-facing language had to be laid in advance, like gravel under a road.

Park Ji-hun, who had spent the entire exchange stepping back to study the color under his star’s eyes and adding one more stroke of shadow, finally put down the puff. In the mirror sat a man with a grey face, something heavy pressed down behind the eyes, and lips paled by powder.

Baek Si-on locked the screen and set the phone face-down on the table. Park Ji-hun looked for a moment at the face he had just darkened with his own hands.

The rice chest took two and a half days to shoot.

Short to say. Hard to sit through: the whole crew went quiet for the duration. These scenes carried no complex blocking. For most of it, Baek Si-on was alone inside a narrow wooden chest, building the Crown Prince out of sound, breath, fingers, shoulders, and body reactions that grew fragment by fragment — rage, then pleading, then collapse, then numbness, then the stage beyond numbness where a person stops holding the shape of a person at all — laying it down layer by layer, take by take.

The difficulty of such scenes is not the suffering. Suffering is easy. Shout, weep, thrash against the boards, and the audience will do the accounting themselves.

The difficulty is that you may not stay at maximum. A human being driven toward the edge is furious before he is broken; he refuses to believe; he imagines rescue; he goes suddenly, terribly calm; he breaks again. It is the staircase that kills, not the fall.

Lee Joon-ik wanted every step of the staircase.

So for two and a half days the set stayed mostly silent. When the final take ended, Lee Joon-ik stared at the monitor for a long time before he said:

“That’s it.”

Nobody cheered. First came the collective exhale. The applause arrived afterward, slowly, the way applause arrives for something people are not sure should be applauded.

It meant the heaviest, most airless material of Sado had been collected. Next the production would move to Hwaseong Haenggung in Suwon — where the pickup shots waiting for them were, ironically, the gentlest stretches of the Crown Prince’s life. Painting. Reading. A child’s birth. Archery. The moments that still got to be called ordinary.

Cinema is occasionally very cruel in its scheduling. First it makes an actor die inside a box. Then it asks him to go back and play the man before the breaking.

At two in the afternoon, the Jeonju set began packing itself up. Props inventory — palace gates, light stands, folding screens, the wooden chest, the old scrolls. Wardrobe checked costume numbers against hangers. The assistant director paced the floor with the shooting schedule, reciting load-in times for Hwaseong Haenggung like a litany.

This was when Baek Eun-ah arrived.

She had not slept a full night in a bed for two days. In a certain sense, the cousins had been serving parallel sentences: he was locked in a rice chest; she was locked in contracts. The difference was that his sentence came with rice.

The concert’s hardest negotiation, she had assumed, would be City Hall. It turned out City Hall was the easy part. The genuinely delicate work had been balancing five conglomerates — each contributing, none dominating, nobody allowed to turn the plaza into a chaebol trade fair with a stage in it.

But that was finished. She was here with a stack of files to collect.

She wove past crew members carrying chest panels and reached his chair. He sat while Park Ji-hun removed the day’s makeup.

“Cousin.”

“Mm.”

She set the folder on the folding table.

“The venue is locked. Jamsil Olympic Stadium.”

Baek Si-on’s eyes came up.

“December 18 — Thursday. Seven in the evening. Capacity released: fifty-eight to sixty-two thousand.”

Park Ji-hun’s hand stopped for half a second, then resumed.

Jamsil Olympic Stadium. In Korea this is not a venue an artist simply wants and gets. A commercial show at that scale normally spends most of a month just grinding through police, fire, and traffic approvals; a month or two more on stage design, tender, and construction; add security, ticketing, and logistics, and three months is the honest floor.

But Baek Si-on was not a standard artist. He had declined Seoul Plaza precisely to avoid the appearance of borrowing the nation’s symbolic heart — and in doing so he had handed the city a better asset: a sixty-thousand-person free cultural event inside Seoul’s own stadium, drop-kicked into the mayor’s achievement column. Every stamp came back green. When Baek Eun-ah submitted the application stack, she experienced, for the first time in her life, being chased down a corridor by officials demanding she let them stamp her documents. The sensation, she reported, was more stimulating than three iced americanos.

Baek Si-on picked up the shooting schedule from the table and ran his eye down it. At Lee Joon-ik’s latest pace, the Hwaseong pickups would finish his portion in half a month — and then his Sado would wrap. Whatever the film did after that belonged to other people: the grown Jeongjo, the posthumous rehabilitation of his father, the settling of accounts with the old order Yeongjo had left behind. Another power game entirely. None of it needed the man who had already died in the box.

Which was absurd. This had begun its life as a man paying his own money to thank his fans. One lap around Korea’s broadcasters and conglomerates later, the free concert had been forcibly converted into a positive-cash-flow project. Capitalism was ridiculous.

Baek Eun-ah enjoyed this particular flavor of ridiculous — the kind that made the studio’s balance sheet prettier — more than she was prepared to admit in writing.

Baek Si-on studied the bid sheet, and decided.

“Sell to tvN.”

“Understood.”

tvN’s number was not only the most sincere. The parent company had just signed him as the global face of bibigo — the contract already into its formal stage — and now the broadcaster inside the same group was taking the free concert’s Asia rights. One hand investing in food going global; the other investing in culture going the same direction. Separate ledgers, one narrative.

He closed the bid sheet and handed it back.

“Eun-ah.”

“Mm?”

“Talk to the Seoul Metro. Have them extend operating hours two extra hours on the lines near the venue. I’ll cover every employee’s night premiums and the full cost of the extension.”

She blinked. The thought had occurred to her, but she hadn’t expected it this early. Six thousand problems at a concert, and none of them is entry. Entry can be metered, staggered, drip-fed through ticket gates all afternoon. Exit cannot. Sixty thousand people leaving a stadium in the same twenty minutes turns every subway platform, bus stop, taxi rank and access road within two kilometers into one pot of porridge — and if snow or a cold snap joins, the next morning’s headlines write themselves while fans fight over the last trains in the wind.

“And if they refuse?”

“Raise the number.”

“To what?”

“Until they say yes, or until they can prove to me it is physically impossible.”

He thought for a second, and added:

“Also — charter two hundred long-distance coaches. Route them by home region, and send the out-of-town fans home safe after the show.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

She looked at her cousin and, for a moment, had nothing on file. Buying up public transit with cash was unreasonable to a degree that should require a permit — and it was also the only mechanism that actually solved a sixty-thousand-person midnight exit. A crowd that size flowing into a paralyzed grid is not an inconvenience; it is a crush hazard. He was spending money to protect his fans, and, incidentally, doing the Seoul Metropolitan Government and the police a favor they hadn’t asked for and couldn’t bill for. The city had already banked one windfall of “mayor values culture.” Now it would bank a second of “no traffic complaints.” Double benefit, delivered to the door.

She logged both items in her memo, then asked:

“Light sticks? Hand warmers? Do we standardize those too?”

“Mm.”

“Prepare sixty thousand of each.”

Neither item was expensive. A light stick costs what an advertising giveaway costs. Hand warmers cost pocket change at wholesale. That was not the point. The point was the vote: sixty thousand people were going to spend a night cheering, and not every act they voted for could stand on that stage. Some fraction of the crowd would carry a small, private disappointment through the gates. You cannot un-disappoint people. But a person who paid nothing for the ticket, and is handed a light stick, a hand warmer, a hot drink, a route home, and a bus at the end of it — that person’s disappointment loses most of its teeth on the way out.

Eun-ah opened a fresh sheet on the tablet and started running it like a siege.

She finished the list and laughed at herself. Concert ticket prices in this industry had climbed for years — VIP floors starting at a month’s rent — while the after-service ran on fan-organized support stations and whatever the agency could be shamed into. Now this: free tickets, sixty thousand light sticks, an extended subway, two hundred buses home. From here on, every concert in the country would be graded against a comparison table its customers hadn’t asked for and couldn’t unlearn. Once that expectation hardened, the industry’s cost structure would be dragged upward by the neck. Refuse to lift, and get flayed. Lift, and bleed.

Baek Si-on heard the entire thesis out with no change of expression.

“Their pain is not my department.”

He picked up the script beside the chair and opened it a page.

“I make my money. I spend my money. If anyone feels wronged, they’re welcome to petition City Hall to ban me from holding concerts.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

Superb. Entirely without reason. And entirely him. He had never been cut from the standard parts of Korea’s idol-industrial assembly line, and he did not care in the slightest whose rice bowl the difference shattered.

By four in the afternoon the Jeonju set was more or less bare. The last costume truck swallowed its cargo and slammed its rear doors. The assistant director checked the schedule one final time and waved the column out.

MAMA. The Mnet Asian Music Awards, an institution with a very specific position in the Korean music industry: it has spent years selling the concept of a pan-Asian celebration, while every insider knows the machinery underneath is a meticulously portioned meat-sharing exercise. Awards are allocated by strategy first and results second. Kwon Ji-yong has spent years needling it from his own stages, and the audience loves him for it. Whether MAMA is hurt by this is unknown; the ratings certainly are not.

So does MAMA carry weight? Depends on who is asking. For a singer with an AMAs double and a Billboard siege behind him, it is an attendance card. For everyone else in the room, its broadcast footprint still matters enormously. And there was the real problem: if he did not go, then a “pan-Asian” grand ceremony would be missing the most successful Korean act of the year, and the awkwardness would be a story of its own.

tvN had just paid ₩4 billion for the Asia rights. CJ had just signed him for bibigo. Two very real sums of money. Favors do not run on one-way accounting: he had eaten their bid sheet, signed their endorsement, taken their rights price — and now the year’s biggest domestic stage asked him to show up, sing once, and collect two trophies that were already his by results. That much face was due.

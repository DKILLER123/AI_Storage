Chapter One Hundred Forty Seven

Saffron Steeps in Water, Not in Vinegar

November 24 · 9:07 a.m. A rare morning with no schedule, watched from a sofa Seoul · Seongbuk-gu · Choi Jin-ri’s apartment For the first time since he debuted, she can watch one of his broadcasts from the very beginning — officially, completely, in the posture of a mature adult.

No schedule this morning, so Choi Jin-ri had slept until she woke — a genuine, unassisted, natural wake-up, at nine-thirty. Bathroom. Face washed. Teeth brushed. Hair gathered up with zero ceremony. The person in the mirror still carried the softness of somebody freshly unconscious, eyes a touch puffy. It was fine. The magazine shoot in the afternoon came with professionals whose entire job was rescue.

Out of the bathroom, she wandered to the kitchen and boiled a kettle. A few strands of saffron went into the cup.

the morning ritual · saffron, again off duty

The look Hair in a loose morning knot, an oversized off-white knit cardigan over soft pink lounge shorts, slouchy grey socks — a person with nowhere to be until two.
The cup A few saffron strands, hot water, both hands around the glass. Not for the health benefits. The steeping just happens to require thinking about a certain someone, and the day’s mood improves inexplicably from there.
The setup Phone propped on the coffee table, AMAs live stream loaded, sofa cushion freshly claimed.

Saffron, steamed; stream, loaded; posture, mature adult.

The most comfortable broadcast of his career — for her, at least. No hiding in a backstage this time, no manager shooing her toward makeup. She sipped.

She took one sip of the saffron tea.

Still awful.

The live signal connected. The show began.

✿ ✿ ✿

Red carpet. The moment Baek Si-on stepped out of one of four all-black state sedans, she nearly inhaled her own tea.

Last time it was a helicopter. This time, a motorcade. How many more levels did this man’s transport have left to unlock?

Complaints aside — when the full look came on screen, she still couldn’t stop herself murmuring:

“…He does look unfairly good.”

She immediately took another sip of water.

Confirmed: saffron water suits the constitution. It does not suit concealing a racing heart.

Then she watched Ariana Grande climb out of the same car, and the two of them walk arm in arm toward the press wall.

Choi Jin-ri told herself: it’s work. It’s a carpet. Co-ed arrivals are normal. Although the carpet’s composition would also survive the arm being held a little looser.

After the carpet, the ceremony proper. When he took New Artist of the Year, she sat bolt upright.

“He got it.”

Her eyes lit. She watched him thank everyone who voted, then tell the fans who’d stayed up all night to please go get some sleep, and she lost the fight with the laugh.

“Doesn’t sleep himself. Polices everybody else’s bedtime…”

Other winners said thank you, dream, honour, journey. This one prescribed naps.

Then the King stage. She watched with the cup forgotten in her hand — she could not have said when she put it down. She was beginning to understand why the Americans ate it up. This wasn’t a song-and-dance number. This was the word king manufactured directly into a picture. And the man on that throne was the same man who had told her, with total sincerity, that he had never personally eaten the camel-milk chocolate he’d once handed her as a gift.

The gap was enormous. Enormous enough that the person on stage felt miles away, while the person she knew felt like a secret. A small, private, delicious piece of insider knowledge — she knew a Baek Si-on nobody in that arena knew. The pettiness of it delighted her.

By the time he took Favorite Pop/Rock Male Artist as well, her mood could be summarized in four characters: fully satisfied. He was glowing in Los Angeles, and she was watching him glow from Seoul. Everything was perfect.

Right up until —

Taylor won. She watched Taylor spring up, hug Selena and kiss her cheek, hug Sam Smith and kiss his cheek too — and then Taylor turned her head, and locked eyes with him on the screen.

On screen, he visibly stepped backward, half a step.

A flicker of satisfaction rose in Choi Jin-ri’s chest. Good. Some upbringing intact.

The next second, Taylor had closed the distance, wrapped him, and kissed his cheek.

The arena screaming nearly blew her phone’s speaker.

“……”

She stared at the right side of his face for three full seconds. Then she exited the live stream, with great gravity.

The living room went very quiet. The phone sat on the home screen. The saffron water was still steaming faintly on the table.

She looked at the cup for a few seconds.

Conclusion: saffron steeps in water. It does not steep in vinegar.

She picked the phone back up. Opened Twitter. The trend was already climbing.

Trending: Taylor kisses Baek Si-on.

“……”

Expressionless, she scrolled past it. Then, with the calm of a surgeon, she switched to her burn account and searched for a dedicated Taylor-hate archive.

Done, she laid the phone face-down on the sofa and leaned all the way back, looking at the ceiling.

Excellent. She had degraded, within one broadcast, from mature adult watching the AMAs in a composed manner to petty girl liking Taylor’s worst moments from a sock puppet.

Pathetic. She was exactly this pathetic. So what? Who decreed that a woman may not vent through a sock puppet after watching the man she cares about get cheek-kissed by another woman? Who made that rule? The United Nations?

She sniffed, reached over, and flipped the phone back over. The screen lit. KakaoTalk. Top of the list, still: Baek Si-on.

The same night · deep into it The long ad break, where nobody in the building actually stops Los Angeles · the Nokia Theatre · the artists’ floor The broadcast cuts to commercials; the theater keeps living — warm-up bits, re-lit cameras, and one actress doing damage assessment two seats down.

When Taylor came back from collecting her award, the applause hadn’t fully settled. She sat down beside him and returned the silk square — now carrying tears and a trace of makeup, no longer strictly within haute-couture regulations.

“Thank you.”

Baek Si-on took it. Looked at it. Folded it. Returned it to his breast pocket. The ivory silk would never again lie as flat as it had left the atelier. Gucci’s soul, tonight, had suffered the streaming era’s gentlest workplace injury.

The ceremony hit its ad break. The television broadcast cut away, but the Nokia Theatre never actually stopped: the host kept warming the room, crews re-aimed cameras, touch-up artists threaded the front rows, and the singers, actors and label executives finally had gaps to talk quietly, drink water, fix lipstick.

Taylor glanced sideways at him and discovered something: he had stopped engaging with her. A minute ago he was still answering in full sentences; now she got the occasional nod.

She blinked.

“You’re not angry about one ceremonial cheek kiss, are you?”

He watched the Samsung phone ad cycling on stage.

“No.”

“Then why did you go quiet?”

“I’m always like this.”

She smiled.

“You weren’t, earlier.”

Two seconds of silence.

“I just don’t like things that slip out of my control.”

She looked at him — and then answered, perfectly naturally:

“So you like being the one who makes the first move?”

“……”

How had this conversation swerved onto this road? And swerved so fluently?

Taylor, for her part, showed zero awareness of how dangerous her own sentence had been. She tilted her head, dropped her voice lower:

“Or — you could kiss it back.”

He regarded her, expressionless.

“I have no interest in ending up as the villain of one of your songs.”

She froze for a beat — then laughed out loud.

“I don’t write a song about everyone.”

“Your hit rate is not low.”

“So you’re afraid of me?”

“I’m afraid of trouble.”

“That hurts more than being feared would.”

Before she could add to that, the stage lights cut to something new. To the TV audience: commercials. To a live arena that could not be asked to sit on its hands: an interstitial performance. Centre stage lit up, and two Asian men bounded out with a dance crew.

The music hit — and Baek Si-on’s expression registered a brief stall.

the ad-break act · two men, one dance crew, a basket of prop fruit · the Nokia Theatre

Taylor couldn’t parse a word of it, but the melody reeled her attention in within seconds.

“What song is this?”

“A Chinese song.”

“You know it?”

“Korea has a cover version.”

She studied the two bouncing men.

“What’s it about?”

He considered, then translated the original lyric as faithfully as the language allowed:

“‘You are my little apple.’”

She looked at him. Blinked once. And said, with tremendous sincerity:

“Baek — don’t you think that’s a rather sudden pickup line?”

“……”

He replayed his own sentence mentally. Fair. In Western iconography the apple was the forbidden fruit of Eden, and stripped of its context, you are my little apple was, technically, vulnerable to misreading. Particularly when the misreader was an American pop star who had kissed his cheek twenty minutes ago and was currently watching him with a smile.

“It’s a lyric.”

“Ohhh—”

She drew the sound out long, arranging her face into sudden enlightenment. The smile hiding inside that face ran at least three layers past enlightened.

She was winding up to tease him further when the act, finishing, came down off the stage with the dance crew — and the audience stirred, because the crew was coming down the aisle with an actual basket of apples, handing them out to the front rows.

“……”

“……”

Both of them received one. Big. Round. Faithful to the song’s spiritual core.

Taylor looked at the apple in her hand and gave up on composure entirely.

“So what are we supposed to do now?”

He watched the broadcast camera swinging their way.

“Hold them up.”

She caught on instantly. Both of them lifted the apples, on cue, and the director took the shot with relish: on the big screen, side by side, the man who had just finished a throne coronation and the woman who had just taken the first-ever Dick Clark Award — together, raising apples. The absurdity was complete. The house roared with laughter and cheering.

The Korean stream lost its mind.

The act wound down to scattered — polite but nowhere near warm — applause. Then came an award invented specifically for the East-Asian market: International Artist of the Year, presented to a Mandarin-language singer named Zhang Jie. He gave a short English thank-you, then cleared his throat and sang a few bars of Michael Jackson a cappella — a pure display of instrument.

The room’s reaction flipped at once. People stood. Whistles. One loud “whoa.” The applause ran long.

Baek Si-on, watching from the floor, found the segment genuinely interesting. Two singers from the same region, two American stages. One had chilled the room at maximum volume. One had won it back with nothing but an open throat. The industry could be very plain sometimes: bring apples if you like, bring a dance crew if you like — but whether anyone stays depends on what’s still there when the microphone opens.

The ceremony rolled on. Trophies out, acts on, lights up and down. By the time the host declared the 2015 show year closed, Los Angeles had gone fully dark outside and the Nokia’s marquee burned like a second, unsleeping daylight.

Baek Si-on won nothing further, which came as a relief. Tonight was already plenty: New Artist, Favorite Pop/Rock Male — any more and American netizens would genuinely have started to suspect that Korean fandom had quietly acquired the AMAs’ servers.

The closing music came up. The floor rose section by section; staff funnelled zones toward the exits; cameras caught the last dissolving shots of the night. Standing, Taylor looked once at the silk square tucked back into his breast pocket.

“After tonight, your Gucci friend may hate me.”

He glanced down.

“He hates many people.”

She laughed.

“Then it’s fair.”

She handed her trophy to an assistant, then turned back to him.

“There’s a little party at my place later. Coming?”

He looked at her, and did not answer immediately. He wasn’t sure what species of invitation this was. If it were just the two of them, that was too private. If it was an induction into her circle, that was too fast. More fundamentally: he held a plain, deep-seated distrust of American parties. Smoke. Alcohol. Little pills appearing from nowhere. Character-actor strangers at three in the morning and your face on a gossip site’s front page by breakfast. Too many variables. Too little control.

“My album drops at midnight. Next time.”

She studied him for two seconds, and took it without any visible disappointment. She understood: he was at the steepest part of the climb — a midnight release, platform push, chart pressure, media interviews, the gravity of that stage, all stacked on the same night. Private time was a luxury for people in that state; her own 1989 cycle hadn’t exactly allowed spontaneous disappearances either.

But she didn’t let the invitation die empty-handed. She borrowed a marker from a staffer, wrote a string of digits across the apple, and handed it over.

Convert the fans’ passion into cash flow while it’s hot — and, said unkindly, most companies see the fandom as a chive patch: cut one crop, another grows right back.

And now, fresh off two AMAs, off the throne stage, with Twitter still burning and the album dropping at midnight — he intended to lose money on a free show?

This was not failing to harvest the chives. This was fertilizing the chives, and building them a shed while he was at it.

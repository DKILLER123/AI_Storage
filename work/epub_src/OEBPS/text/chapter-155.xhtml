Chapter One Hundred Fifty Five

Cindy Is Not Reasonable

December 3 · 8:31 p.m. After the opening · back in the artist seats · ceremony in progress Hong Kong · AsiaWorld-Expo · the artist seating block He came back out changed and ready to watch an awards show. What was waiting for him instead was a dispute about chairs — which, at every ceremony on Earth, has never once been about chairs.

Changed and ready, Baek Si-on came back out from the backstage side and headed for the artist seats.

Before he got there, he saw Lee Ji-eun standing at the mouth of the aisle, talking to a member of the event staff.

Beside her stood six young women, wearing the specific expression of people who have been standing somewhere they were not supposed to be standing for slightly too long.

Baek Si-on approached, stopped at the edge, and listened for a while.

“My row has empty seats right next to it. Can’t they just sit there?”

“IU-ssi, those seats are reserved for guests performing later in the show.”

“Then let them sit for now and move when the guests arrive—”

“It’s not a seating issue.”

Lee Ji-eun went quiet.

Baek Si-on understood.

It was not a seating issue. It was a face-sheet issue. Or, more precisely, it was the organizers wanting the traffic while wanting to keep their hands clean — the operational posture of a certain kind of host, everywhere, always.

The troll incident was nearly two years cold.

By any reasonable calendar, T-ara should not have been spending an awards ceremony unable to sit down without a clearance form. But the troll who had walked out of the group never stopped feeding: every few months, another tearful selfie, another ambiguous paragraph of quiet suffering, posted with the diligence of a person terrified that the Korean public might, catastrophically, forget.

So T-ara’s domestic reputation stayed pinned face-down in the mud.

Over in Greater China, meanwhile, their traffic was enormous. The organizers had been starving for exactly that — overseas ratings and discussion volume — and had flown the six of them in on purpose. Having flown them in, the organizers did not want to offend the domestic pile-on crowd either. The solution: no proper seats at all. Six girls, marooned at the edge of the artist block like people serving a sentence.

the chair rescue · one sentence long

Baek Si-on walked over. The staffer saw him and his face changed shape entirely.

“Baek Si-on-ssi.”

Baek Si-on looked at the empty row beside his own seat.

“Have them sit next to me.”

The staffer blinked.

“Uh…”

“Is there a problem?”

“No — I’ll go arrange it.”

The man turned and left. Fast. People in that line of work can read a menu and price a guest in one glance: ten minutes ago, with Lee Ji-eun, he had a whole vocabulary available — protocol, reservations, not a seating issue. One sentence from Baek Si-on, and the vocabulary evaporated down to “I’ll go arrange it.” Not because a conscience had sprouted. Because Baek Si-on’s current weight could convert not-a-seating-issue into a-problem-we-are-solving-immediately, at a word, without friction.

Within minutes, staff arrived carrying chairs and set them in around Baek Si-on’s row. The six of T-ara finally stepped off the aisle.

the chair delivery · six bows

The leader, Qri, bowed first.

“Thank you, Baek Si-on-ssi.”

Eunjung bowed after her.

“Really — thank you.”

Hyomin, Jiyeon, Boram, and Soyeon followed down the line, each with her own bow. Baek Si-on waved it off.

“Don’t misunderstand.”

“I just didn’t want to watch guests of my concert being made uncomfortable.”

He tipped his head toward Lee Ji-eun.

“Thank her.”

Six pairs of eyes followed his gesture. Jiyeon reached out and took Lee Ji-eun’s hand and squeezed it hard. Lee Ji-eun squeezed back and patted the back of her hand twice.

“Sit down, all of you. No more standing.”

She murmured a few more reassurances and herded them into their seats quickly, before the cameras and the director could develop an interest in the story.

Only when all six were settled did Lee Ji-eun tilt her head toward Baek Si-on and lean in slightly.

“Thank you.”

Baek Si-on watched the broadcast feed on the big screen.

“Next time, argue louder.”

“…What?”

“The way you looked just now was practically aegyo.”

“……”

Lee Ji-eun’s expression went through three jurisdictions in one second.

“Which artist on this planet dares to YELL at the host broadcaster?!”

Especially at a meat-sharing exercise of this caliber. The host’s executives were sitting in the backstage command row; start a real fight, and tomorrow the corrective-thinking press would drown her by lunch. Standing up as far as she had was already volunteering for collateral damage.

“I’m starting to doubt you can play Cindy at all.”

Lee Ji-eun needed a few seconds.

Cindy. The top idol in Producer — temper in the stratosphere, a face like a glacier, someone who would show a broadcast director her back mid-sentence and chew her manager into the carpet without raising her voice past freezing.

Then she got it.

He wasn’t mocking her for fighting too gently. He was teaching her. Next time something like this happened: bill the anger to the role. Method-acting expenses, fully deductible.

The excuse was rotten.

It was also, unfortunately, airtight. Too deep in character. Who could fault it?

“I was being reasonable out there,” she tried, one more time.

“Cindy is not reasonable.”

“……”

Lee Ji-eun said, quietly:

“Then next time I’ll be fiercer.”

“Not fiercer. The point is to make people understand your temper isn’t you losing control — it’s that they haven’t earned a reasonable conversation.”

“……”

The sentence was rotten with layers.

She stared at him for two full seconds and arrived at a diagnosis: Writer Park Ji-eun’s Cindy was going to be used, personally and repeatedly, as an instrument of torture against her.

“Do you enjoy watching me make enemies?”

“I enjoy watching you not swallow things.”

Too direct.

Lee Ji-eun stalled. Whatever she had loaded for the counter came off the rails, and what came out instead was very small:

“Oh.”

Baek Si-on looked back at the stage.

“But you should practice first.”

“Practice what?”

“The cold face.”

“……”

She endured. She did not succeed.

“I can already do it.”

Baek Si-on said nothing.

“I’m merely a polite person in daily life.”

“Mm.”

“What is that mm doing?”

“Interpret it yourself.”

Lee Ji-eun inhaled, long and slow.

Excellent. The Cindy state was arriving ahead of schedule. At minimum, the urge to murder a manager was fully charged.

…

After the opening stage, the host took over and ran the standard MAMA liturgy: a movement about how great Asian music was, how brilliant the Wave was, how tonight belonged to everyone who loved music — beautiful sentences, correct emotions, and, set next to the meat-sharing machinery that would follow, mildly dissociative.

Best New Artist. Best Dance. Best Performance. The awards went out one by one; fandom sections screamed on cue and clapped politely off it.

Baek Si-on sat in the middle of the artist block, applauding at intervals, expression level. The six of T-ara, beside him, sat looser than they had all night — no longer hot-potatoes rotisserieing at the end of the aisle.

Jiyeon was the first to lose patience with sitting still. She watched Lee Ji-eun get up and head backstage to prep her stage, then sneaked a look at Baek Si-on. After a while, she migrated.

Every eonni saw it happen.

the six-chair peanut gallery · minute one of the operation

Soyeon, without turning her head, at conversation volume:

“Jiyeon-ah.”

Jiyeon developed sudden hearing loss and sat down in Lee Ji-eun’s vacated chair anyway, leaning in, voice pitched for one listener:

“Baek Si-on-ssi.”

“Mm.”

“Are you and our Ji-eun unnie close?”

“Not as close as you are.”

Jiyeon: “……” Between dismissal and refusal, she privately scored it as refusal, smiled the politest smile in her inventory, and migrated back.

Qri leaned over:

“What did you two talk about?”

“Nothing.”

A pause. Then the addendum:

“He doesn’t seem to like talking.”

Eunjung glanced at the man’s profile two seats down.

“He doesn’t seem to like talking to anyone.”

“He talked to Ji-eun unnie for ages, though.” Hyomin, very quietly.

Six women went silent in a single synchronized beat. Then Boram coughed once, like a woman closing a drawer.

“Stage. Let’s watch the stage.”

The stage lights went down, and Lee Ji-eun took the arena.

A medley, ten minutes corner to corner: from ‘Meet Me on Friday’ into ‘Sogyeokdong’, out through ‘Fly, Little Chick’. The fan sections of AsiaWorld-Expo went off like a struck match. A solid share of the lightsticks down on the floor had come tonight specifically for her.

the ten-minute medley

IU · MAMA 2014 · ‘Meet Me on Friday’ → ‘Sogyeokdong’ → ‘Fly, Little Chick’

The stage that dragged the broadcast rating back up to its peak —

And her stagecraft, for the record, earned every decibel it got.

The interesting part was the graph: the medley hauled MAMA’s live rating back up the cliff — and, accurately measured, hauled it back to exactly the peak Baek Si-on’s opening had set. The night had two suns, and the graph knew it.

The stage ended. Applause and screaming held for a long time. She bowed and came off.

Before long the host was back up.

“Next, we present — Most Popular Singer!”

Lee Ji-eun had barely made it back to her seat and sat down when her own name came out of the speakers.

“IU! Congratulations!”

The hall applauded warmly. The fan sections, obviously, screamed the roof loose.

And at the exact moment she heard her name, Lee Ji-eun laughed from pure indignation.

Most Popular Singer. Not Artist of the Year. Not even Best Female Singer. An award that sounded like it had been copied out of a template library five minutes before printing. And under the iron logic of MAMA’s meat-sharing, taking this one meant putting the grand prizes officially out of reach.

If that had been all, she could have swallowed it.

The problem was the weeks of winks from the organizers’ side — said more than once, in more than one channel: come, and the big one is in play. She had believed it. She had performed a full ten-minute medley on the strength of that belief.

And they turned around and handed her this?

Wearing the professional smile, she climbed the steps one at a time, took the trophy, and stood at the microphone:

“Thank you, truly.”

“Honestly, I’ve always felt I have so much left to learn, so receiving this Most Popular Singer award — I’m genuinely overwhelmed.”

“I don’t even know if I deserve this heavy, heavy honor. Thank you to the organizers for preparing it for me, specially.”

“……”

The fans heard a humble queen and screamed louder. Every word of the second sentence sailed past the entire building at a low altitude and nobody flagged it.

She came off the stage. The smile came off with her, the moment she sat down.

Sour face. Fully deployed.

Extremely Cindy.

Right on cue, Ma-ryong took the stage.

This was MAMA’s preserved tradition, the dish that had to be served: Ma-ryong rapping at the organizers about the meat-sharing, the organizers hearing nothing, the crowd cheering itself hoarse. Everyone got what they came for.

And indeed: the first half stayed inside the lines. Then the rhythm flipped, and he simply — changed the words. The rewritten bars mocked the meat allocation with the subtlety of a fire alarm, and the artists who had just lived through tonight’s allocation wore a gallery of expressions while he did it.

Baek Si-on listened with half an ear and glanced sideways at the sour face beside him.

“Not happy?”

Lee Ji-eun watched the stage.

“Should Cindy be happy?”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Good state. Keep it.”

“You’re welcome.”

She turned her head.

“Are you also going to say I should thank MAMA for the role-immersion opportunity?”

“I could.”

“……”

She almost laughed for real. She held it — because she was still angry, and holding it was the whole performance.

“Will this mood of yours affect your attendance at my concert?” Baek Si-on asked.

Lee Ji-eun’s face went flat.

“No idea.”

Baek Si-on thought for a second. Then he swept the staff section down on the floor with his eyes, located Baek Eun-ah, and raised one hand. A small wave.

She startled, pointed at herself: me? He nodded. She hunched down out of camera sight-lines and jogged over, crouching beside his seat.

“What’s wrong?”

“Tell the tvN president.”

She snapped into work mode instantly.

“Mm.”

“One of my concert guests may not attend — or may attend reduced — due to unfair award allocation by the host broadcaster, Mnet.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

She looked at Lee Ji-eun. Lee Ji-eun looked at him. Baek Si-on continued, in the same tone:

“If she cancels or reduces, the licensed content of the broadcast package tvN purchased changes materially.”

“That risk originates in a CJ-internal event’s arrangements, and should not be carried by S.W Studio.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

She understood every word. Understanding the words and watching her cousin convert a MAMA meat-sharing scandal, live, into licensed-content risk attributable to a CJ-internal event were two different organs of the body. This man was simply too good at this.

Beside him, Lee Ji-eun had started biting the inside of her cheek. She wanted to laugh. She could not laugh. She was in Cindy state.

“Got all that?” Baek Si-on asked.

Baek Eun-ah looked at Ji-eun, then at her cousin, and nodded.

“Got it. Contacting them now.”

She jogged back off, hunched.

Lee Ji-eun watched her go, and said quietly:

“I never said I’d cancel.”

Baek Si-on kept his eyes on the stage.

“Which is why I said may.”

“So I’m your pretext?”

“I’m giving you a reason not to swallow it.”

Down on the stage, the rewritten bars kept coming. The cheering kept arriving in waves. Mnet’s director cut fast and desperate, trying to gift-wrap the evening’s controlled derailment as “the free spirit of hip-hop.”

Lee Ji-eun said, very softly:

“You are frighteningly good at repackaging private feelings as commercial risk.”

“It’s called professionalism.”

The laugh escaped. Short. But it escaped.

Six seats down, T-ara had entered full melon-eating formation. Excruciatingly restrained, though — they had been fished out of the standing-isolation zone barely an hour ago. One does not behave like a row of front-line melon-patch sentries while still holding a debt of chairs.

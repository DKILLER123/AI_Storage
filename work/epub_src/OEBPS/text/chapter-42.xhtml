Chapter Forty-Two

Start Deciding What to Wear to Venice

July Night Choi Jin-ri's apartment The last of the meal, and the sentence that landed

As Si-on watched Jin-ri start to eat, the pieces he'd set aside for himself seemed to change direction.

Jin-ri looked down at her own plate — at the tower of meat that had kept growing on it — and opened her mouth to say something. But Si-on had already turned back to the grill.

She swallowed the "that's enough" that was already sitting in her mouth, and dutifully picked up a piece and ate it.

“The director submitted the film to Venice. We'll know by the end of the month.”

Jin-ri's chewing slowed.

She lifted her head and looked at the person across the table who was laying a fresh strip down on the grill.

This was the second thing he'd said to her all night.

The first had been, where's the kitchen.

The second was the Venice Film Festival.

That was quite a leap.

“……Really?”

She swallowed what was in her mouth.

“I hope it gets in.”

Si-on flipped the chicken thigh. The fat met the hot plate and hissed.

“You should be thinking about which dress to wear.”

Jin-ri froze.

Not just her.

Goo Hara blinked. The soju glass in her hand stopped halfway to her mouth.

The Venice Film Festival?

A gown?

She knew both words on their own. Put together, in the living room of an apartment in Bukchon that smelled of barbecue, they were just slightly out of range of her ability to process them.

“Cousin, you're a little too sure of yourself.”

Eun-ah was chewing on a ring of green chilli and spoke around it, indistinctly.

Her father, Baek Jeong-hun, had been making films for years, and the biggest stage he'd ever reached was the Busan International Film Festival.

Venice.

One of the Big Three.

In her heart, she thought her cousin was pouring sugar water over Jin-ri's head.

But she didn't say it, because the green chilli in her mouth was too hot, too hot to get a full sentence out.

Si-on didn't explain.

He only pressed the fresh meat down with the chopsticks, making sure every piece lay flat against the plate, and the sound of fat seeping out started again.

He didn't explain because it wasn't that he couldn't — it was that bringing up this particular thing in this particular room would just sound arrogant. He knew his uncle.

July Night The film, from the first take to the final cut Why this one would land in Venice

From the script phase right through to the export of the rough cut, he'd been there for every scene. He'd seen every frame of it.

What was this film about?

On the surface: the tragedy of violence inside a broken family. A bluebottle — a green bottle fly — buzzing around a wound.

But that wasn't what Baek Jeong-hun really wanted to talk about.

What he wanted to talk about was violence itself.

Violence is contagious. It circulates. It passes down from one generation to the next the way a virus does. The grandfather hits the grandmother. And so the father learns to hit the mother. And the son, growing up in the middle of it, will one day raise a fist against his own wife in turn.

That cycle doesn't need malice to drive it.

It only needs silence.

It only needs every bystander, every time a fist falls, to choose to close the door, to draw the curtains, to pretend they heard nothing at all.

The film · Baek Jeong-hun's intent · the argument beneath it

Two families. No lectures. No grand statements.

Violence is inherited — not by blood, but by silence. Every bystander who closes the door becomes its carrier.

Small: one family's wound. Large: the logic can be laid over anything — war, class, hatred between peoples.

Baek Jeong-hun didn't say any of those big words in the film.

He only shot two families.

But anyone who walks out of the cinema is left, despite themselves, thinking about the larger things. This is the exact move the juries of the three European festivals eat up. Cannes, Berlin, and Venice each have their own taste, but they share one unspoken rule: they like pain.

Green Bottle Fly was that kind of film.

That it would get in, in Si-on's view, was less a hope than a certainty.

July Night The low table Three-quarters of the table, then the clean-up

The three girls' combined battle power accounted for, in total, about a third of the meat.

The remaining two-thirds went to Si-on.

Jin-ri sat beside the table, knees hugged to her chest, watching him. A memory surfaced — that night, the nameless little shop in Yeonnam-dong, Si-on across the table, a whole table of food in front of him, head down, eating with an unself-consciousness so total it was almost rude.

Back then she'd had no appetite. But she'd watched, and watched, and then, without meaning to, eaten a whole bowl.

It was the same now.

Twenty minutes ago she'd thought her stomach was locked shut, that nothing would go in. And then she'd sat down and watched him grill, flip, serve — and watched, and the mountain of meat on her own plate had emptied without her noticing.

After the meal, the four of them cleaned up together.

Hara washed the dishes. Eun-ah wiped the table. Si-on turned off the portable stove, pulled the gas canister, and scrubbed the grill plate. Jin-ri crouched on the floor holding a garbage bag, picking up bones and garlic peel.

The smoke in the living room had mostly thinned. The window was open, and the night wind came in carrying the cicadas and the faint music from some shop you couldn't locate.

When it was done, Si-on glanced at his phone.

Ten-forty.

“It's late.”

He slid the phone back into his pocket and stood.

“We'll head off.”

Eun-ah waved a hand.

“Unnie, we'll come see you again in a few days.”

Jin-ri nodded.

Si-on changed his shoes, straightened up, and put a hand on the door handle.

Then he stopped.

Turned.

“Message me if anything comes up.”

The exact same words as the day filming wrapped. That time Jin-ri hadn't answered — not because she hadn't wanted to, but because she hadn't had time to react before he'd gone.

This time she did.

She nodded once.

The door closed. The footsteps and voices in the corridor faded, the elevator made its "ding," and then there was no sound at all.

July Night Downstairs, the street The lit window, and the light going out

Downstairs, Si-on pushed the entrance door open and stepped out into the night air.

The wind at the end of July still carried the day's leftover heat. It hit him neither cold nor warm but just right.

He'd gone a few steps before he stopped.

Eun-ah walked on two paces before she noticed her cousin wasn't following, and turned to look at him.

The window of Jin-ri's apartment was lit.

The curtain had been pulled open a crack, and a single narrow band of light was coming out of it.

He looked at it for about five seconds.

The band of light in the crack vanished. The curtain was drawn from the inside.

Si-on took his gaze off it and kept walking.

“Cousin.”

“Mm.”

“What were you looking at?”

Si-on didn't answer.

The July night wind poured in from the mouth of the alley and rustled the leaves of a ginkgo tree by the roadside.

Eun-ah waited about ten seconds.

For a question like that, ten seconds was already a long time.

“Forget it.”

She clasped her hands behind her back, lifted her head to look at the small patch of sky above the streetlight, washed pale by light pollution.

“You wouldn't tell me anyway.”

This time Si-on tilted his head slightly.

“Know what?”

Eun-ah looked at him and grinned.

“Know that you won't answer.”

Si-on's step slowed for a beat.

Eun-ah hopped a couple of steps ahead, turned back, screwed up her face at him, then turned again and kept hopping off down the street.

✿ ✿ ✿

July Night Upstairs, the living room The girl at the window, and the excuse

Upstairs, Goo Hara came out of the kitchen wiping her hands on a towel, and saw that Jin-ri wasn't on the sofa.

She was standing at the floor-to-ceiling window of the balcony. The curtain was held open a slit by one hand — about five centimetres wide, just enough for one eye to look down.

Hara said nothing.

She leaned against the kitchen doorframe, arms folded, and watched quietly for a while.

Then Jin-ri, as if she'd been burned by something, yanked the curtain shut and turned around.

The two of them locked eyes across the living room.

Hara's expression was calm.

Calm — with a smile that was carrying a little too much calm in it.

“What were you looking at?”

“There's a stray dog down there, I think.”

Jin-ri walked back to the sofa and sat, reaching for the glass of water on the table.

“Oh. A stray dog.”

Hara walked over and sat down next to her.

“That dog's pretty tall, isn't it.”

Jin-ri: “……”

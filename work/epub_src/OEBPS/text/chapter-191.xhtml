Chapter One Hundred Ninety One

The Top-Luxury Sea King Meets the Nation’s First Love

Late February · the settlement, then the sky, then Milan One hundred eleven and a half million dollars on one projector · an afternoon in which a mountain of money became a slaughterhouse · forty million that never got to glow · a four-brand ambush at Malpensa won by twenty minutes of backrest · and a front-row seat with no backrest at all SB Projects, Los Angeles → Milan Malpensa → a hotel suite → the Fendi show The tour’s money is counted, carved, and distributed before it can melt; Milan receives him with four logos and one escort war; and the front row of an Italian runway introduces the Nation’s first love to the man the luxury houses call their sea king.

Los Angeles. The SB Projects conference room.

The tour had been over barely a week, and Scooter had already pressed Baek Si-on into a financial settlement meeting. On the table: coffee, documents, two laptops, and a master statement that could stop a middle-class family’s heart at ten paces.

Seo Eun-ju had flown in from Seoul the night before, jet lag still unresolved, black suit already worn to the millimeter. Baek Eun-ah knew the moment she saw her that this was no ordinary reckoning. Seo Eun-ju appeared in person only when the money had grown large enough to start causing trouble.

The American accountant switched on the projector. Baek Eun-ah read the first line of numbers and briefly lost the ability to speak.

Human Live 2015: First Light Tour · round one · the golden-mountain page

GROSS, ROUND ONE: $111,500,000

North America · 32 shows, box office — $73,000,000

Middle East · 4 shows, guarantees + sponsorship + VIP boxes + backend — $11,000,000

Merchandise, net — $7,500,000

Brand sponsorship + city subsidies — $11,000,000

Broadcast + content rights — $9,000,000

Laid end to end, these no longer resemble a singer’s tour statement. They resemble the quarterly report of a mid-sized enterprise.

Baek Eun-ah stared at the screen for a few seconds, and spoke with alarming serenity:

“Cousin. You’re not an artist anymore.”

Baek Si-on sat at the far end of the long table, a glass of water at hand.

“Then what am I?”

“A mid-sized enterprise that walks.”

Scooter understood roughly half of that, judged from the tone that it was a compliment, and drank his coffee with satisfaction.

Baek Si-on was unmoved by his new title.

“How much is left?”

In the adult world, happiness begins with that sentence. It also ends with it.

Baek Eun-ah turned the page.

Human Live 2015: First Light Tour · round one · the slaughterhouse page

Venues, ticketing, local promotion, security, cleaning — $20,000,000

Stage, lighting, sound, LED, freight, Middle East special visuals — $14,000,000

Band, dancers, tech crew, tour managers, physio, day labor — $7,000,000

Transport, hotels, catering, logistics — $6,500,000

Guest collaborations, rights clearance, rigging adaptation — $2,500,000

Legal, insurance, visas, union compliance, audit — $5,000,000

OPERATING COSTS, TOTAL — $55,000,000

North America partnership split · 20% — $11,300,000 (line item: Scooter Braun)

Tax reserve, per S.W Studio policy · 30% — corporate, state, cross-border, future-liability coverage

Cash landing in the S.W Studio account at the end of it all: about $31,000,000. The page before this was a mountain of gold. This page is where the knives come out.

The income had looked like a gold mountain one page ago. The next page was an abattoir. Baek Eun-ah decided she wasn’t reading a statement at all — she was watching a circle of small, elegant knives divide Baek Si-on’s wallet with exquisite manners.

Scooter finally spoke.

“Tours are expensive. That’s the business.”

Baek Eun-ah gave him one cold look.

“You’re more expensive.”

Because the very next line was his name. Scooter did not have one gram of guilt about it.

“I’m worth the price.”

That was, annoyingly, also true. Without him, those thirty-odd North American dates do not get laid down at that speed. It hurt, and it was earned — both at once.

Then came tax. Corporate income tax. State tax. Cross-border exposure. Future back-payment risk. The reserve Seo Eun-ju demanded: thirty percent. Her reasoning: you do not gamble with the American tax system.

Knife by knife by knife, the tour’s spendable remainder — the cash actually landing in the S.W Studio account — came to roughly thirty-one million dollars.

The number was absurd. More absurd was Baek Eun-ah catching her own first reaction: not euphoria. Compliance filing anxiety.

People are rarely matured by sudden epiphanies. They are grown, under protest, by circumstance.

Baek Si-on tapped the table.

“Music income.”

Baek Eun-ah surfaced. ‘Legend’: October’s global streaming, downloads, MV advertising, radio-related settlements — confirmed pre-tax at a little over $3.8 million. After platform cuts, distribution, the Jung Jae-joon split, North American promotion, and tax holding: $2.4 million left on the company’s books. ‘Human’: global sales confirmed at 1.6 million copies. Physical, digital, vinyl, collector editions, streaming-equivalent revenue all counted — then channels, production, writing, distribution, and tax deducted — $6.8 million left.

Baek Eun-ah’s brow came down.

“One point six million albums sold, and that’s what survives?”

Scooter spread his hands.

“Welcome to the music industry.”

Seo Eun-ju put it more directly:

“Which is why masters, publishing, and long-term splits are what matter. Sales are the noise. Cash recovery is a different sport.”

Baek Eun-ah summed it. Tour: thirty-one million and change. ‘Legend’: $2.4 million. ‘Human’: $6.8 million.

Notes · Baek Eun-ah, margins of the master statement

Not counted: equity. BodyArmor’s future value. A certain car and a certain guitar, gifts, Middle East. The Korean endorsements already signed.

New spendable cash, company total: just about forty million dollars.

It landed forty seconds ago. It has not been allowed to glow. It is already carved: tax reserve, future costs, production budget, bonus pool.

Conclusion: when you were poor, you at least fantasized freely. Now the money is real, and the money is busier than the people.

Baek Si-on asked exactly one question about the whole sum:

“Can I spend it?”

“You can spend it.”

“Top up the tax reserve first.”

Baek Eun-ah drew a long breath.

“Cousin, you are genuinely gifted at ruining the atmosphere of sudden wealth.”

He ignored her.

“Set aside the European fashion weeks, the next Asian leg, and production costs for the new songs.”

She noted several lines on the sheet. Then heard him continue:

“Jung Jae-joon gets a two-hundred-thousand-dollar bonus.”

Her pen stopped.

“He’ll cry.”

“Tell him not to cry. Tell him to keep working.”

Very cold. Very realistic.

“The touring crew gets one too.”

He kept going:

“Dancers, band, core tech, stage directors, tour managers — split by role. Everyone on the front line gets at least one month’s salary as a bonus. Core leads, thirty to fifty thousand each. The few who had it worst get more.”

Scooter whistled.

“You’re buying loyalty.”

Baek Si-on finally looked up.

“It’s called settling up.”

“They carried thirty-six shows. I’m paying out the respect that was owed.”

Baek Eun-ah looked down and wrote, beside the table: TOUR BONUS POOL. She hesitated a moment, then filled in three million dollars. Baek Si-on’s eyes passed over the number.

“Enough?”

Baek Eun-ah was suddenly a little afraid on behalf of those three million dollars.

“Enough. Very enough.”

“Then pay it.”

Seo Eun-ju added at once:

“Through the company account. Declared, compliant.”

Baek Si-on acknowledged it. Baek Eun-ah looked at the sheet one more time — forty million dollars had landed, been denied its moment of glowing, and been dissected into tax reserve, future costs, production budget, and bonus pool, all inside one afternoon. She found herself missing being poor. Poor people at least got to fantasize in freedom.

Before the meeting broke up, Scooter raised his coffee cup.

“Congratulations, multimillionaire.”

Baek Si-on clinked it with his water glass.

❧ ❧ ❧

The settlement had ended not long ago; the forty million hadn’t even finished cooling in Baek Eun-ah’s head, and Baek Si-on was already being stuffed onto a flight to Italy. Such is the life of an international star: the money has just finished being counted, and the body is already in the sky.

The plane came down at Milan Malpensa. Baek Eun-ah had assumed a single business van would collect them. She walked out of the VIP channel and saw a row of cars. Gucci. Fendi. Armani. Prada. Separate brand PR units, each standing beside its vehicle, each maintaining a perfectly dignified distance from the others — so dignified that every face said the same thing: why are the rest of you still here?

Milan Malpensa · the arrival bid sheet · four logos, one sidewalk

GUCCI — hotel prepared and waiting.

ARMANI — dinner timing can move for you.

PRADA — styling team on standby, around the clock.

FENDI — larger back seat. Nearest hotel to the venue. And: “at least twenty extra minutes of rest,” addressed not to the star but to the manager.

Winning bid: FENDI, on a technical knockout. The air outside the VIP channel turned expensive and awkward simultaneously.

Baek Eun-ah slowed, dragging her suitcase.

“Cousin.”

“Mm.”

“Is this an airport pickup, or a chaebol motorcade screen test for ‘The Heirs’?”

Baek Si-on scanned the row.

“Pickup.”

“It doesn’t look like one.”

The PR teams converged. Gucci announced the hotel was ready. Armani said the dinner could move. Prada said the styling team stood prepared. And then — the Fendi unit’s young female publicist turned, of all people, toward Baek Eun-ah.

“Manager Baek — long flight. You’ve worked hard.”

Baek Eun-ah blinked. The publicist kept smiling.

“Our car has the larger back seat, and our hotel is the closest to the show. You’ll have a full schedule to handle after landing — if you go on ahead, you get at least twenty extra minutes of rest.”

Baek Eun-ah was silent for two seconds. No brand story. No century of craft. No spirit of Italy. But twenty extra minutes of rest. Nothing in the entire airport was more moving than that. She looked at the black Fendi van, then turned to Baek Si-on.

“Cousin. That car looks more comfortable.”

Baek Si-on walked toward it.

“That one, then.”

Gucci: “……” Armani: “……” Prada: “……”

The houses had assumed the key to this war was Baek Si-on. Only now did they understand the breach was the young woman beside him. And Baek Eun-ah, settling into the back seat, had no idea she had just been promoted inside Milan’s fashion ecosystem — from “Baek Si-on’s manager” to “the woman you must please first if you want Baek Si-on.”

No brand story. No century of craft. No spirit of Italy. Twenty minutes of backrest. The room went to the only bid written in a language humans actually feel.

— Malpensa, on how escort wars are lost

❧ ❧ ❧

The hotel really was close to the venue. So close that Baek Si-on had barely entered the suite — had not yet managed to drink any water — when the Fendi styling team rolled in two racks of clothes. Coats. Suits. Knitwear. Leather accessories. Shoes. Sunglasses. And several runway pieces that no functioning human being wears to live their life.

Watching the operation unfold, Baek Eun-ah finally understood the promised “extra time.” Extra time, nothing. They simply wanted him disassembled and re-dressed as early as possible.

One hour later, Baek Si-on was stuffed into a car again, bound for the Fendi show — and “stuffed” is the precise word. Between entering the suite and sitting down in the vehicle, almost nothing in the process belonged to autonomous human will. The Fendi styling team worked like a trained surgical unit: in, strip, re-dress, fix the hair, check the cuffs, apply scent, inspect the shoes. The single right he negotiated for himself was half a bottle of water. Then he was shipped.

Milan’s lanes were much narrower than Los Angeles’. The buildings were older. What slid past the window was not giant billboards and palm trees but stone walls, arches, corner cafés, and Italian faces that had long ago stopped being impressed by fashion week. In the car, Baek Eun-ah kept auditing the next slot aloud.

“Fendi ends, then straight to Gucci. You’ve got forty minutes between. If a brand doesn’t grab you for a surprise interview, theoretically that’s enough to change looks.”

Baek Si-on watched the street.

“Theoretically?”

Baek Eun-ah didn’t look up.

“It means it isn’t.”

“……”

By the time the car stopped outside the Fendi show, the flashbulbs had already pooled into a white rain. Baek Si-on pushed the door open. The shutters thickened instantly. The Fendi PR unit exhaled, visibly — at least this look would not have been put on in vain before the eyes of Park Ji-hoon, which judged worlds for a living.

Baek Eun-ah followed, about to hurry him inside — and heard Korean media murmuring beside her.

“Daebak…”

“That face is actually insane.”

“Did you get it? Tell me you got it?”

She couldn’t help looking back. Very good. The first three seconds of Baek Si-on exiting a car in Milan would presumably keep Korean forums arguing for a full day.

❧ ❧ ❧

Bae Suzy was already seated in the front row. Today’s fit was a pale Fendi suit — clean lines, a waist taken in exactly enough — hair loose behind her shoulders, makeup so light it seemed barely attempted, which was precisely why everyone looked twice.

Staff walked Baek Si-on to the front row. His seat was beside Bae Suzy.

The front row of Milan fashion week contains a very particular cruelty. It signifies status. It also tortures the lumbar spine. The seats are narrow and low; there are no armrests and no backrest. The moment he sat down, Baek Si-on finally understood why so many fashion people watch shows with faces like marble. It is not because they are elevated. It is because they are uncomfortable.

Bae Suzy watched him adjust his posture, and laughed, softly.

“Baek Si-on sunbaenim.”

She curved her eyes into crescents.

“We finally meet.”

Baek Si-on turned his head.

“Have we met before?”

“On television. Many times.”

She paused.

“Doesn’t that count?”

“Then you’ve met a great many people.”

Her smile deepened.

“But not every one of them made me remember.”

Baek Si-on declined to catch the ball. He said, evenly:

“Bae Suzy-ssi. The honor is mine.”

She didn’t find it awkward in the least. In this industry, a person who can be steered by one pretty sentence is a boring person. This refusal-to-take and refusal-to-dodge at least proved he was not someone a camera and a compliment could push around.

The show lights fell. Music came up. Both of them turned to the runway. Fendi’s season ran to clean, hard lines and cold tones; fur and architectural outerwear looked expensive under the lights, and deliberately hard to approach. Baek Si-on watched with real attention. Not because Italian fashion had suddenly conquered his heart — but because he knew Park Ji-hoon would ask him later, “Which piece can we take apart?” and he intended to have answers prepared.

The front row was tight. Bae Suzy sat properly, but their shoulder lines inevitably ran close. Her scent was faint — white woods with something clean and powdery under it — and every time she leaned over to speak at half-volume, that unreasonable little wisp of it drifted his way.

A dark long coat crossed the runway. She tilted her head, voice lowered:

“That one would look better on you, sunbae, than it does on the model.”

Baek Si-on didn’t turn.

“Do you work for Fendi?”

“No.”

She smiled.

“So I can say true things.”

He glanced at her. She kept her show-watching posture, profile flattered to perfection by the cold runway light, as if the sentence had been a passing remark about a coat.

Baek Si-on returned his eyes to the runway.

“Then you should say fewer of them.”

“Why?”

“True statements tend to be expensive.”

Bae Suzy held still for one second — and then laughed out loud. It was a light laugh, swallowed quickly by the show music.

❧ ❧ ❧

When the Fendi show ended, the crowd began moving outward. The moment front-row guests stood, photographers and brand staff converged. Fendi’s PR had clearly prepared this in advance.

“Mr. Baek — Suzy — may we get one of you together?”

Bae Suzy looked at Baek Si-on with flawless politeness, as if requesting permission. He didn’t refuse. They were led to the backdrop outside the show space. She took her place at his side, tilted her head a degree, and set her shoulder line and face angle where lenses love them. He stood alongside, expression still flat. Flat to exactly the right depth.

One of them sharp. One of them soft. One like a knife under cold white light. One like a spring that cameras favor. The media’s fingers nearly struck sparks off the shutters.

Afterward, Bae Suzy accepted her coat from an assistant, put it on slowly, and asked in a what-casual tone:

“Your next one is Gucci, sunbae?”

“Mm.”

“That’s a busy day.”

“Are you not busy?”

“I’m better at slacking.”

She laughed, beautifully and without any burden on it. Then she glanced toward the staff consolidating the car line, and her voice dropped a register:

“There’s a small Fendi after party tonight. Not many people. Easier than the formal dinner.”

She turned to him.

“If your evening isn’t fully assigned yet — come sit with us a while.”

The sentence was engineered to be safe. It was also not only safe. Because outside a Milan show space, a actress who has just finished a two-shot with a man, asking whether he might drop by a party that is “not so formal,” is very hard to file under itinerary coordination.

Baek Si-on looked at her, and had not yet answered —

when a new commotion rose on the other side of the exit. Gucci staff were escorting a party in from the passage. At its center walked a woman in sunglasses and a dark Gucci look, Jung Han-teuk at her elbow, murmuring the interview order. She was listening. Her steps slowed as her eyes found the two people at the backdrop — and stopped.

Lee Ji-eun took off her sunglasses. Her gaze landed on Baek Si-on first. Then moved to Bae Suzy, beside him. Bae Suzy saw her too. Across the not-yet-settled flashbulbs outside a Milan show space, the two women’s eyes met.

One had just extended an invitation.

One had caught its final syllable.

Chapter Two Hundred Seventeen

The End of the Itinerary Is Choi Jin-ri

June 2

Morning — 7:12

New York · a hotel room, one thin line of light through the curtains

New York mornings began with the backup beeper of a garbage truck.

The hotel curtains were excellent at their job — and leaked one thin line of light anyway.

When Baek Si-on opened his eyes, the phone screen read 7:12.

He lay there for about thirty seconds.

Then he picked up the phone.

Found the number traded at last night’s party. Rihanna’s.

At the call button, seeing the hour, he hesitated a moment.

And dialed anyway.

Six rings.

The seventh was picked up.

First came a long stretch of breathing, and then a voice so hoarse it had lost its gender entirely:

“Whatever bastard you are, waking me up at this hour…”

A pause of two seconds.

“…you’d better have an apocalypse-grade reason.”

“It’s Baek Si-on.”

Two seconds of silence on the other end.

“…Oh.”

One more second.

“What?”

“That thing you said last night,” Baek Si-on said.

“I said a lot of things last night.”

“The cosmetics brand thing.”

Rihanna went quiet again for a while.

“What time is it?”

“Quarter past seven.”

“I’ve slept two hours.”

“Sorry.”

“If you were actually sorry, you wouldn’t have made this call.”

Baek Si-on did not dispute this.

Rihanna sighed into the line.

“Fine. Ask.”

“A cosmetics brand — where’s the actual handhold in all of this?”

Rihanna organized her answer in her head.

“You only have to control four points: the brand IP. The creative direction of the product. The core R&D concept. And then marketing and promotion.”

“What about production?” Baek Si-on asked.

“The best cosmetics contract manufacturers in the world aren’t in Europe and aren’t in China. They’re in Korea.”

Baek Si-on pinned the phone between shoulder and ear and freed his hands to unscrew a water bottle.

“And channels?”

“Warehousing, logistics, retail-end negotiation, the online-offline distribution network — there are no shortcuts in any of it.”

“Understood.”

“One more thing.”

“What?”

“Don’t let your agency touch this business. The brand is yours, and the equity structure is clean from day one.”

“You sound like you’ve been burned.”

“I haven’t been. But I’ve watched too many people be.”

A very long yawn traveled down the line.

“Okay, I’m going back to sleep.”

“Thank you.”

“Don’t mention it. But the next time you call me at seven in the morning, I’m blocking your number.”

“I’ve noted it.”

“You’d better have actually noted it.”

The call ended.

Baek Si-on set the phone on the nightstand.

The line of light through the curtains was a little wider than before.

This gets done the moment I’m back in Korea.

❧ ❧ ❧

About forty minutes later.

Three knocks at the door.

Baek Si-on opened it.

Baek Eun-ah stood in the doorway hugging a folder.

“‘La La Land’ came through.”

She handed the folder over.

“They signed the bring-your-own-investment package.”

“The production agreed to push the shoot back one month.”

“Rehearsal schedule, shooting window, and the male lead’s being Asian — all re-adjusted.”

Baek Si-on took the file and flipped through it.

Inside: a thick sheaf of contracts.

In English.

Every page tagged in pencil at the bottom right, marking where a signature belonged.

“What about Emma’s side?”

“No problem. Her schedule was always looser than yours — a month’s delay barely touches her.”

“Mm.”

Baek Si-on sat down at the desk and picked up the hotel’s pen.

One page at a time.

One signature at a time.

“Cousin.”

“Mm?”

“Our company account is down to ten million dollars.”

Baek Si-on’s pen didn’t stop.

“And then.”

Baek Eun-ah swallowed.

“You also owe KB Bank four thousand two hundred billion won.”

Baek Si-on turned a page.

“So?”

Baek Eun-ah’s expression oscillated between “professional” and “collapse” for about two seconds.

“So, cousin — you need to make money now.”

On the last page, he finished signing, set the pen down, and closed the folder.

“Got it. Once these two films are shot.”

He pushed the folder back across the desk.

Baek Eun-ah took it and checked it carefully from the first page, twice.

Everything in order, she turned toward the door with the contracts in her arms.

At the door, she stopped.

“Cousin.”

“Mm?”

“That ‘ten million dollars left’ I said — it isn’t entirely accurate.”

Baek Si-on waited.

“Minus this month’s operating costs, team salaries, and reserved taxes, the usable cash is about eight point seven million.”

“……”

“I just felt you should know the precise number.”

“Fine. Noted.”

“Then I’m gone.”

Baek Eun-ah left.

As the door swung shut, Baek Si-on heard a sigh travel down the corridor.

It was, presumably, the sound ten million dollars makes sliding past Baek Eun-ah’s heart.

❧ ❧ ❧

The room went quiet.

Baek Si-on sat back down at the desk and opened the laptop.

The inbox refreshed. One new mail at the top.

Sender: Damien.

He opened it. The body was short. The gist: a signature is a signature — but before the shoot, you will complete the following preparation.

Below hung a column of attachments.

Baek Si-on scrolled. One after another.

The 53.7 gigabytes, as audited

Basic jazz-piano fingering tutorial videos, parts one through twelve. Tap-dance step breakdowns, beginner through intermediate. A documentary on the history of Los Angeles jazz — three episodes, ninety minutes each. Character-psychology reference films, four of them. A must-listen album list, seventeen albums. A must-read book list, five books.

Plus several compressed archives whose file names alone read like revenge on society.

Total size: 53.7 GB. Clearly, Damien had compromised the schedule for capital’s sake — and surrendered not one drop on the professional and artistic requirements.

A very peculiar feeling came over Baek Si-on.

He had just signed away ten million dollars.

And the return shipment had already arrived: 53.7 gigabytes of homework.

He clicked “Download All.”

The progress bar started running. Estimated time: four hours, twenty-seven minutes.

Dame’s syllabus · 53.7 GB · due before the first day of shooting

01 — Jazz piano fingering, lessons 1–12. 12 parts

02 — Tap steps, broken down, beginner → intermediate. 1 compilation

03 — LA jazz history documentary. 3 × 90 min

04 — Character-psychology reference films. 4 films

05 — Must-listen albums. 17 albums

06 — Must-read books. 5 books

07 — Archives named like an act of vengeance. several

With the contracts seen to, Baek Eun-ah hurried back to the hotel to help Baek Si-on pack.

She was going back too.

Charlie’s daily operations, schedule management, and liaison work were handed over — along with a fourteen-page operations manual, stuffed into the hands of a newly hired assistant.

As for why going back was non-negotiable…

The reason Baek Eun-ah gave Baek Si-on was: to keep an eye on him, and stop the bleeding of money.

But in actual fact —

❝

Near Baek Si-on, she could act spoiled, complain freely, and coast a little in front of her cousin. At Charlie’s office, she only got to be a professional manager. Twenty-four hours a day of holding her posture. It was exhausting.

— the real reason, filed under personal

Then, the moment the ticket was booked, Under Armour called.

The content was simple: as a brand endorser, would he attend a game at the Golden State Warriors’ home floor.

Plainly put, a brand appearance. No essential difference from cutting a ribbon at a mall opening — except the mall had been swapped for Oracle Arena.

Baek Si-on looked at Baek Eun-ah.

Baek Eun-ah looked at the contract clause posting Under Armour’s 4%-a-year royalty on collaboration sales.

Then she refunded the Incheon flight she had just booked.

❧ ❧ ❧

Game night

Tip-off to final horn

Oracle Arena, Oakland · second row, courtside

Baek Si-on walked into the tunnel in a dark-grey hoodie provided by Under Armour.

The hoodie was from the Curry collaboration line.

Under Armour’s people had been cunning about this: seat one endorser courtside in a second endorser’s collaboration drop, and every angle shoots itself.

At the end of the tunnel, an Under Armour staffer was already waiting.

“Baek — this way, please.”

He was led to the second row at courtside.

The seat was excellent — so excellent that after he sat down, he discovered a lady’s handbag resting on the empty seat to his left.

And then the handbag’s owner came in from the side tunnel.

Ayesha Curry.

Her look said less came to watch a game and more came from a school pickup run.

“Hi!”

Ayesha’s face lit up at the sight of him.

“You’re Baek!”

“Hello.”

“Steph told me you’re the coolest Asian guy he’s ever met.”

“…Thank you.”

Baek Si-on couldn’t determine whether that counted as a compliment.

But Ayesha’s smile was sincere, with no second layer to it. She was simply one of those people who shipped with warmth pre-installed.

Ayesha Curry

Stephen Curry’s wife · the Warriors’ home front

School-pickup energy · genuine warmth · one song request, planted

Mistook cool for a compliment and meant every word. Clapped loudest when the mascot staged its ambush, judged a championship shimmy against Kobe’s cold blood, and walked away with a promise attached: his own song, before the Finals.

Footsteps and the thud of basketballs came from the players’ tunnel. Warm-ups had begun.

Steph Curry jogged out, glanced toward the family section first — and finding Baek Si-on seated next to his wife, froze for half a second, then split into a grin and aimed a finger-gun at him from across the floor.

Baek Si-on raised a hand and returned it.

A few Warriors fans nearby saw the exchange and began whispering to each other.

Once the game began, Baek Si-on watched quietly from his seat.

His viewing style differed completely from Ayesha’s: she tensed at every Curry touch, frowned at every hit he absorbed, leapt up clapping at every make.

Baek Si-on’s expression barely moved all night.

The broadcast director did not care. At the first dead ball of the first quarter, the big screen cut to him.

Baek Si-on’s face filled the arena screen, and the stands erupted in cheers and whistles.

He waved at the lens.

The expression was perfectly regulated: nothing exaggerated, nothing cold — landed exactly on the balance point between “friendly” and “handsome.”

Before the first quarter even ended, the camera cut back a second time, and the cheer was louder than the first.

Ayesha laughed beside him.

“You’re more popular than Steph.”

“That’s not possible.”

“Really — listen how loud they’re yelling.”

“That’s because your husband hasn’t started shooting threes yet.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Halftime. The scoreboard read Warriors by nine over the Grizzlies.

The house lights went warm; the cheer team performed at center court; the Warriors’ mascot, Thunder, ran all over the floor with a T-shirt cannon on his shoulder.

And when the dance number ended, Thunder happened to be standing right in front of Baek Si-on, plush arm pointing at center court.

Before he could refuse, he had already been hauled up out of his seat.

Ayesha clapped with delight at his side.

The arena sound system chose that moment to detonate.

The DJ’s voice crashed down from the overhead speakers:

“LADIES AND GENTLEMEN! We have a VERY special guest sitting courtside tonight!”

The big screen cut to footage of Baek Si-on being dragged to the edge of the floor by the mascot.

Screams and whistles burst through the building.

The DJ kept stacking:

“‘See You Again’ is in rotation on every radio station in America right now — and this man is standing on our court!”

Baek Si-on raised a hand.

The applause grew louder.

The DJ held a beat — and his tone shifted into full agent-of-chaos register:

“So I’ve got ONE question for you, Baek!”

“You’re an Under Armour athlete. Steph’s an Under Armour athlete.”

“You wear the same shoes — so can you shoot the same ball?”

The whole arena roared with laughter.

Baek Si-on stood beyond the three-point line and looked up at the rim.

He hesitated, a little.

“Come on!”

The DJ stirred the pot from overhead.

“One shot! Make it, and beer’s on me for the whole building! Miss it, and you owe everyone a line of ‘See You Again’!”

The crowd began chanting in unison:

“Shoot it!”

“Shoot it!”

“Shoot it!”

Thunder took a ball from a ball kid and stuffed it into Baek Si-on’s hands.

Baek Si-on looked down at the Curry-collab hoodie he was wearing, then at the far rim.

Then he adjusted his stance.

Two hands on the ball, knees bent.

The release itself was, honestly, not an ugly sight.

The ball drew its arc — and flew away in a manner that honored Curry’s spirit while betraying Curry’s technique, nearly clipping the photographer under the backboard.

An air ball.

The building exploded laughing.

The DJ pounced instantly:

“Good news: your shooting form is prettier than ninety percent of our fans’. Bad news: you still have to sing.”

Baek Si-on stood at center court.

Twenty thousand people watching him.

He took the mic from the staffer, cleared his throat, and sang the full chorus of ‘See You Again’.

The applause came down like an avalanche.

He handed the mic back and walked to his seat.

“Your shot really did look good,” Ayesha said, grinning.

“Thank you. I just didn’t want the DJ going bankrupt tonight.”

❧ ❧ ❧

The first half wasn’t exactly close, but it hadn’t broken open either.

What broke the game open was the third quarter.

Once the Warriors’ tempo jumped, the Grizzlies stopped keeping up.

And Curry’s touch began heating toward scalding.

One screen. One three.

Next possession. Up the floor, almost without adjusting — another deep one.

By the time the ball left his hand near the logo, people in the building were already on their feet early.

Swish.

In again.

The arena’s roof panels seemed to shudder under the weight of the roar.

Curry, retreating on defense, shook his head — and gave both shoulders two little shimmy-shakes to the rhythm, mouth crooked, the expression parked somewhere between proud and punchable.

Ayesha couldn’t help covering her face with both hands.

“Goodness.”

“That move of his is genuinely so silly.”

Baek Si-on didn’t chime in.

Though privately he did think that, measured against Kobe blowing on the barrel after a bucket, or Allen Iverson stepping over Tyronn Lue after dropping him flat, the shimmy carried a little less menace.

“Maybe it’s because the in-arena background noise is too messy.”

Ayesha turned her head.

“What?”

“When he does it, the crowd is yelling, the commentators are yelling, the speakers are playing some random BGM.”

Baek Si-on watched Curry defending on the floor.

“If it had its own dedicated track — a theme locked to the rhythm of that shoulder shake — the effect would be far better. Like the entrance score the first time a character walks on screen in a film.”

Ayesha’s eyes lit up. She rotated her whole body toward him, one knee nearly landing on his leg.

“Then could you write him one?”

Before Baek Si-on could answer, she was already rolling.

“You know Steph has always been a little jealous of Kobe.”

“Jealous of what?”

“‘Legend’! Every time Kobe came out of the tunnel, the whole building played it. That presence — unreal.”

She paused for a beat.

“And now the man who wrote ‘Legend’ is sitting right next to me.”

Baek Si-on studied her expression.

Half joking.

Half serious.

Accurately: the serious fraction was compounding fast.

He was still assembling a polite and vague reply when a voice cut in beside them.

“That is a fantastic idea.”

Under Armour’s head of North America marketing had, at some point, materialized at their row.

“Baek — if you wrote Steph a song of his own… that’s a dream collaboration! Under Armour will support it in full!”

Baek Si-on listened to the whole speech with a level face.

“All marketing costs on us.”

The executive kept stacking.

“Promotion channels, streaming placement — Under Armour covers everything. All you’d need to do is make the song.”

Ayesha nodded hard beside them.

Baek Si-on looked left, then right.

Ayesha: eyes full of expectation.

The Under Armour executive: eyes full of dollars.

On the floor, Curry stood at the free-throw line.

It had come to this: the brand was asking him for something that benefited both sides, and the endorser’s wife had made her request in person, in good faith.

The cost of declining was higher than the cost of agreeing.

“I’ll think about it,” Baek Si-on said.

Ayesha’s smile climbed another notch.

The executive was already typing into his phone.

A message to headquarters, probably.

❧ ❧ ❧

The final horn sounded.

Warriors 101–86, a rout of the Grizzlies, and the opener of the Western Conference semifinal series in the bag.

The players filed toward the tunnel.

Curry ran over and hugged Ayesha first. His sweat transferred wholesale; she smacked his back, muttering about the stink, and did not let go.

Curry released his wife, turned to Baek Si-on, and put out a hand.

The two of them clapped palms.

“Brother! Thanks for coming!”

“Good game.”

Curry grinned:

“My shot is so much truer when Ayesha’s in the building.”

Ayesha gave him a little push at that.

“I told him something.”

“What thing?”

“To write you a song.”

Curry’s face went through a magnificent sequence of changes.

“Seriously?”

He grabbed Baek Si-on’s arm:

“Brother — is this song going to have my name on it?”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

A man who had just dropped eight threes in front of twenty thousand people was wearing the exact face of a grade-schooler who had been promised a theme park.

“Maybe.”

Curry’s grin stretched from the corners of his mouth all the way to his ears.

“Then when do I get to hear it?”

“Before the Finals.”

Curry blinked — and then laughed louder still.

“You said it!”

“I said it.”

He let go and jogged two steps toward the tunnel, then turned back around.

“If it’s worse than Kobe’s ‘Legend,’ I’m telling the entire country.”

“Then you’d better make enough threes to deserve it.”

❧ ❧ ❧

With the Under Armour event over, the American leg of the itinerary had reached its final pages.

Curry’s song remained at the verbal-promise stage.

No hurry. The Finals hadn’t started. He planned to grind it out slowly, back in Korea.

As for ‘La La Land’ —

That was no longer a grind-it-out-slowly kind of problem.

Fifty-some gigabytes of jazz piano, tap breakdowns, character reference films, and LA jazz history documentaries were pressing down on his laptop with great specificity.

June 5

Dawn

Incheon International Airport · arrivals

The flight landed.

Baek Eun-ah walked out behind Baek Si-on, eyes on the laptop bag over his shoulder.

Ten million dollars invested, and so far it had brought Baek Si-on back zero glory from Hollywood.

What it had brought back first was a pile of jazz-piano fingering tutorials, tap-dance breakdowns, and LA jazz history documentaries in compressed form.

If investments could be returned, Baek Eun-ah wanted to ask Damien Chazelle whether he accepted seven-day no-questions-asked refunds.

Arrivals hall.

The crowd on the left detonated the instant the party walked out of the corridor.

Screaming. Flashbulbs. Phones held higher than heads.

Even with expectations set, the density of the sound wall exceeded projections.

“Ahh—!”

“Oppa—!”

“Baek Si-on—!”

Two bodyguards front and back, Baek Eun-ah at the flank. Airport security had already strung the barrier lines along both sides of the corridor.

Mask on, cap pressed low — and completely irrelevant to anyone’s ability to recognize him.

As he walked, his peripheral vision swept the crowd pacing him from behind the barrier.

And found three familiar faces.

Baek Si-on moved two steps closer to the rail.

The bodyguards glanced back at him. They did not intervene.

“You look thinner,” Baek Si-on said to the fansite master called Warm Light.

The camera in her hands nearly escaped.

Her mouth opened twice before a sentence squeezed out:

“…Oppa. That video on Twitter — you playing piano, and the way you and Emma Stone looked at each other — is that for a movie?”

Baek Si-on raised his right index finger to his lips.

No admission.

No denial.

And the gesture itself was the most premium form of confirmation there was.

Someone else shouted at the top of their lungs:

“Oppa, when’s the next concert—”

Mid-stride, Baek Si-on tilted his head and answered:

“Probably October.”

Another wave of screaming.

“Where!”

“World tour.”

“Will there be a Seoul stop!”

“There will.”

The word landed — and the decibel level of the entire arrivals hall headed straight for the fire-alarm threshold.

Out of the terminal, the van already idled at the pickup lane.

The party loaded in.

Before climbing in himself, Baek Si-on turned and waved to the fans who still hadn’t dispersed.

“Get home safe. Early night.”

The last wave of screaming chased them out.

Five minutes into the drive.

One bend later, fully clear of the terminal’s sightlines —

The driver suddenly slowed.

Then stopped the car.

Baek Eun-ah looked up:

“What is it?”

The driver didn’t answer her.

He looked at Baek Si-on through the rearview mirror instead.

“Boss. Miss Choi is waiting up ahead.”

Baek Eun-ah blinked.

She looked forward.

About twenty meters ahead of the van, a Porsche 911 stood at the roadside.

“…Jin-ri unnie?” Her voice came out half a register higher than before.

The driver nodded.

And suddenly she remembered.

A few nights ago, at dawn.

The message Scooter had sent her.

Message · Scooter Braun

Scooter

Who’s Baek Si-on’s girlfriend in Korea?

At the time she’d assumed Scooter was drunk.

Or fishing.

Either way she hadn’t taken it seriously at all — fired back a one-word “no one,” and tossed the phone aside.

Now, every piece of the puzzle clicked into place at once.

Baek Eun-ah turned her head, slowly, toward the man unbuckling his seatbelt.

“Cousin.”

“Mm.”

“You and Jin-ri unnie… are really dating?”

“Mm.”

Three seconds of silence.

“…Why didn’t you tell me?”

Baek Si-on glanced at her.

“You didn’t ask.”

“……”

Baek Eun-ah chewed on those three words twice.

You didn’t ask.

Wonderful.

She had followed this man from buying lottery tickets in Hongdae to a Best Actor cup in Venice, from a Billboard champion to a North American tour.

And she was the last person alive to learn that her own cousin was in a relationship.

Baek Si-on already had the door handle in hand.

“Don’t follow.”

The door shut.

Through the glass, Baek Eun-ah watched his back cross the gap between the two cars, toward the Porsche.

The Pacific crossing, as audited from the back seat

Exhibit A: she had flown back across an ocean. Primary stated duty — monitoring unsound expenditure.

Exhibit B: the actual function performed today — witnessing, with her own eyes, that the itinerary’s final stop was a borrowed Porsche and a woman in sunglasses.

Conclusion, confirmed by direct observation: she was, in the most technical sense, completely superfluous.

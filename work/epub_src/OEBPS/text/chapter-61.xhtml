Chapter Sixty-One

The Angel Round on the Treadmill

July 29 Later that day Yeonnam-dong · a gym Two treadmills, one bad day, and the seed of a company

The two treadmills in the corner of the gym were grinding away.

Incline mode.

Baek Si-on let go of the handles, keeping an even breath, his steps striking steady rhythm on the belt as it climbed.

On the treadmill beside him, Son Nam-won was clutching the heart-rate grips with both hands, his shoulders pumping up and down with every stride, the towel around his neck already soaked through.

“Really… didn’t see that coming.”

Son Nam-won dragged in a huge lungful, the words chopped to pieces by the belt beneath him.

“That… that’s the result.”

Baek Si-on didn’t look at him.

Truth be told, when that article had gone up this morning he’d been a little hot about it.

The release was too soft.

Back at that Cheongdam-dong izakaya, he’d been explicit with Son Nam-won: use the press’s strengths and build Choi Jin-ri into a “victim.”

But Son Nam-won had lived so long in the shadow of the big three that he’d never dared to finish the job.

What ran was a headline that read "or absent for personal reasons."

The body was even more restrained — it didn’t come within arm’s reach of words like “blocking” or “suppressing.”

Forget a victim narrative. The phrasing was so polite it might as well have been doing PR for SM.

If SM’s public-relations office had been thick-skinned and hard-nosed enough that day, they could easily have walked down that stair — issued a statement that the artist was genuinely unwell and regretted her absence.

And then everything Baek Si-on had built would have been wasted.

It was only Kim Young-min’s guilt that saved it.

He wasn’t willing to be saddled with the “obstruction of Korean cinema” label on the eve of an international festival like Venice, so he’d rushed out a crawling statement of “full support for her going to Venice.”

The goal had been reached.

Baek Si-on reached out and tapped the display, dropping the incline by a notch.

He turned his head and glanced at Son Nam-won, wheezing like a broken bellows beside him.

A man in his mid-forties, thirteen years in the business, pressured by the big three over an article that wasn’t even that hard-hitting, tossed out of the office that very afternoon carrying a cardboard box, now at a treadmill in Yeonnam-dong questioning his life.

The words he’d wanted to say suddenly wouldn’t come.

“What are you planning to do next?”

“Haven’t… thought yet.”

Son Nam-won freed one hand to wipe the sweat from his chin, breathing like a leaky bagpipe.

“The big three have spoken… it’s not just the portal sites. Even a newspaper’s entertainment page… probably no one will take me now.”

Baek Si-on pressed the red stop button on the treadmill.

The belt slowed from fast to slow, the hum dropped, and finally it stopped.

He swung off, holding the handrails, wiped his face with the towel, and turned to look at Son Nam-won, still struggling to keep his legs turning on the belt.

“If you still want to keep working in this industry.”

Son Nam-won gripped the handrails and twisted his head around to look at him.

“I have a bit of a connection with Director Lim at Dispatch. I can introduce you.”

Son Nam-won’s stride faltered.

His left foot missed the belt by half a step, and he pitched forward. If Baek Si-on hadn’t shot a hand out in time, he’d most likely have ended the day in the pose of “a forty-something man catapulted off a treadmill.”

Baek Si-on dragged him off the belt.

“Take your time.”

He tilted his chin toward the weights area.

“I’m going to train.”

And he was gone.

Son Nam-won propped his hands on his knees, bent over, and wheezed for a good two minutes.

Then he walked over to the rest area and sat down.

From here he could see the weights area.

Baek Si-on was lying under the bench-press rack, big plates racked on each side of the bar, the lines of his arms standing out sharply on every up-and-down rep.

Son Nam-won watched him push a set.

Watched him push another.

His mind was turning.

Dispatch.

Lim Geun-ho.

If Baek Si-on really could get him into Dispatch, that was a stable road.

Dispatch was the biggest entertainment-exclusive news supplier in Korea. No matter how hard the big three clamped down, they couldn’t embargo Dispatch.

But what could he do there?

Run errands for Lim Geun-ho? Start over from a junior beat writer?

Hard to slide from luxury back to bare living.

He’d sat in the editor-in-chief’s chair so long. Now, pointing a telephoto lens at underground garages to tail idols — he couldn’t do it.

He watched Baek Si-on finish a set and sit up to drink.

Sweat ran down the buzz cut; the back of the T-shirt was a wet patch.

Son Nam-won stood and walked over.

Baek Si-on was between sets, hands on his knees, watching himself in the mirror as he caught his breath.

“Mr. Baek.”

“Mm?”

“What would you think of me going independent?”

Baek Si-on turned and looked at Son Nam-won’s soft, middle-aged face.

Going independent.

A jobless man blacklisted by the big three, effectively socially dead in the media world — going independent?

Who would feed him news sources?

Who would publish his releases?

“…You’re not pinning your hopes on me, are you?”

“I am.”

Son Nam-won didn’t dance around it.

Who’ll rise, who’ll fall, which story will blow up, which one will go quiet.

That was his bread and butter.

Now he’d pointed it at Baek Si-on.

Baek Si-on said nothing, and lay back under the bench-press bar.

Both hands took the bar, grip at shoulder width, the heels of his palms pressing against the knurling.

Drive.

The bar went up, smooth and steady.

He remembered the old, brutal maxim: the high ground of public opinion belongs to whoever takes it. If you don’t seize it, your enemy will.

Son Nam-won had just said “going independent.”

A blacklisted former editor-in-chief, with the experience, with the news instincts — short only on money and a backer.

And he, Baek Si-on, had the money, the heat, and lacked a voice to speak for him at the crucial moment.

Two people’s wants. Exactly a lock and a key.

Ten reps.

Baek Si-on pressed the bar back onto the rack and sat up.

“Do you need an angel investor?”

Son Nam-won had been leaning against a piece of equipment, arms folded, still working out how to steer the conversation toward a “long-term meal ticket.”

At those words, his hands slid down from his chest.

“Are you serious?” Son Nam-won’s voice came out unsteady.

“Name a price.”

Starting a business wasn’t something Son Nam-won had never thought about.

Back at OSEN, after every scolding from the CEO, he’d ride a midnight taxi and run the numbers on "how much I’d need to go out on my own."

He’d done it plenty of times.

The number was already a fixture.

“Rent plus security deposit, twenty million won. A proper outlet needs at least three full-time staff, so labor costs are fifteen million a month. Outsourcing the website, ten million. Second-hand equipment, bare minimum twenty million. Then six months of operating capital to stay afloat.”

“All told, about two hundred million won.”

Two hundred million.

Not a big number by startup standards.

A halfway decent apartment in Gangnam cost more than that.

Baek Si-on ran through his balance in his head.

The World Cup winnings had been spent out left and right. What remained on the card came to just about two hundred million.

“I put in a hundred and fifty million for fifty-one percent. You put in the remaining fifty million for forty-nine percent.”

Son Nam-won’s expression froze.

A hundred and fifty million.

Fifty-one percent.

Baek Si-on wasn’t doing charity; he wanted control of the company.

But that wasn’t the point.

The point was the second half of the sentence.

“Fifty million… I put up?”

Fifty million won — it wasn’t as if he didn’t have it.

The statutory severance from OSEN, plus the payout for unspent annual leave, came to exactly fifty-one million won net.

But that was the one thing still incontrovertibly his after being blacklisted by the big three and shoved out of the editor’s chair.

It was his floor.

If he never turned things around, that fifty million could keep him going in Seoul for years.

And now Baek Si-on wanted him to pull that floor out from under himself.

“That won’t work?” Baek Si-on looked at him.

“What about me supplying the skill and the channels, and you fully funding it?”

“No.”

Baek Si-on stood, walked to the bar rack, and began loading plates onto either end.

Speaking with his back to Son Nam-won:

“Put your own money in and you’ll fight for it. Spend somebody else’s and losing it won’t hurt.”

Son Nam-won stood motionless.

He’d been an editor-in-chief, had owned an office, managed more than a dozen people at his peak.

And now, in the weights area of a gym in Yeonnam-dong, watching a twenty-two-year-old load plates onto a bar, seriously weighing whether to stake his entire net worth.

Baek Si-on lay back under the bar, both hands gripping it.

“Made up your mind?”

Son Nam-won drew a long breath.

Very long.

Long enough to press the leftover treadmill breathlessness out of his lungs entirely.

“I have.”

Baek Si-on pushed the bar up.

“Welcome aboard.”

Chapter One Hundred Eighty Two

Rarer Than the Aurora, You

January 2 · afternoon flight into a long polar night One scarf surrendered at the airstair · one trash can of great bearing · one snow war in a forest quiet for centuries · one necklace that will not wilt · three centimeters, two, one Churchill, Manitoba — the Polar Bear Capital → a snowfield at the edge of the pines → two folding chairs under the first green Science said the aurora owes nobody anything; a lawn oracle said otherwise; a physics lecture interrupted a kiss and was then, thoroughly, avenged; and at minus thirty, the coldest place in North America hosted the least cold conversation of the year.

The moment Choi Jin-ri came down the airstair, the north wind of Canada finished introducing itself, with no courtesy whatsoever.

She understood at once why Professor Yoon Hye-ja had called the freezing cold too punishing. That hadn’t been fussiness. That had been fifty-plus years of accumulated life wisdom, sincerely advising the world.

Science is cruel. The temperature is crueler.

Baek Si-on came down behind her, took one look at the half of her face that had already gone red, pulled off his own scarf, and wound it around her face.

“There’s still time to regret this.”

“No regrets.”

Her voice came out from behind the wool, muffled but firm.

“Your mouth is frozen stiff and you’re still this stubborn.”

“That’s called willpower.”

She tugged the scarf up a little higher, leaving just her eyes showing.

The aurora trip was not, in fact, a party of two: Baek Si-on’s two bodyguards were along as well. Canada is vast and thinly peopled, with more wild animals than humans. Baek Eun-ah had spoken her piece about keeping their aunt company with such towering righteousness that it completely buried her own grief at being excluded from the aurora. As for Choi Jin-ri, she had no objections at all — anything short of thirty people with cameras circling them was acceptable.

Their guide was called Pierre — a huge, burly white man with a rust-colored beard.

“Welcome to the Polar Bear Capital.”

While he stuffed their luggage into the back of the snow-tracked SUV, Pierre delivered the safety briefing.

“Here, a polar bear is what we call lethal risk. People who treat one like a tourist attraction mostly end up on the news.”

“If you see one: do not approach, do not scream, and above all do not raise your phone to take a picture for social media.”

The two bodyguards went visibly alert. They had come from Seoul, where the risks they were trained for were telephoto lenses and strangers who got too close. The polar bear variable sat entirely outside their professional curriculum.

Pierre led them onto the vehicle and headed for the wilderness observation point. They hadn’t even cleared the airport zone when, at the edge of a white slope in the distance, a pale, somewhat bloated shape appeared.

“Eleven o’clock. Suspected target.”

Pierre unhurriedly raised his binoculars, looked, lowered them.

Churchill · threat assessment · eleven o’clock direction

Suspected polar bear: white, bloated, motionless, threatening.

Field verdict: “That’s a trash can.”

Addendum from the guide: the local trash cans are a hardy people. The non-hardy ones have all been demolished by bears.

Choi Jin-ri got her head down and her shoulders shook with held-in laughter.

Baek Si-on contemplated the snow-covered trash can in the distance and said, with perfect calm:

“It has great bearing.”

Pierre nodded.

“Ours are all tough. It’s selective pressure.”

Choi Jin-ri stopped laughing at once.

Churchill is a small town. Low houses sit scattered thin across the white earth; half-buried wooden fences line the road; beyond them stands dark conifer forest, and beyond that, a bay frozen into stillness. The sky hangs low, clouds pressed against the horizon, the gray-blue and the snow-white meeting without any clear border.

Choi Jin-ri pressed her forehead to the window. The glass was cold. She couldn’t bear to look away. It had been a very long time since she had looked forward to one single thing this simply.

The drive took nearly an hour. The observation point, when they reached it, received a field professional: Pierre stopped the car and began allocating tasks with tidy authority — the two bodyguards sent to pitch the aurora tent in the lee of the wind, himself unloading the stove and fire kit. The bodyguards, entirely helpless before the concept of a polar bear, proved unstoppable at the labor of tent poles.

Choi Jin-ri watched for a while and discovered she could not help at all. The instant she reached out to steady a stretch of tent fabric, Pierre gave her the look of a man watching a kindergartner wander toward a running chain saw.

“Ma’am — it’s safer if you stand back a bit.”

She obediently retreated. Two steps later, she decided she had perhaps been too obedient.

She looked down at the snow at her feet.

White. Clean. Extremely suitable for crime.

She crouched, and with her gloved hands rolled a snowball. Rounded it. Packed it. Looked up. Target: Baek Si-on — likewise standing idle, for identical lack of polar survival skills.

The snowball left her hand at a beautiful angle.

Thump. Dead center on his shoulder, the powder bursting off the black down jacket in a little white fog.

He looked at his shoulder. Then up at her.

“Do you know what assaulting me means back in Korea?”

Choi Jin-ri hid the second ball — which she had, of course, already rolled in secret — behind her back and tilted her head.

“But this is Canada.”

“Fair point.”

The words were barely out when the second ball struck him square in the chest, bursting even bigger.

Crime accomplished, she turned and ran. What she forgot is that a snowfield is not a Seoul practice-room floor. Ankle-deep snow made every step heavy; running felt like being enthusiastically detained by the earth itself, footprints deep and shallow, trajectory veering wildly.

Baek Si-on watched her staggering flight, bent down without hurry. Scoop, one-handed. Pack. Aim. Release. The ball drew an arc of thoroughly wicked quality across the air and hit the back of her head with precision.

She stopped dead, spun around holding her head.

“You—!”

“You threw first.”

He stated fact, unburdened by anything at all.

Choi Jin-ri exhaled a plume of white breath, and the competitive instinct in her eyes caught fully alight. She bent, both hands now, scooping snow at double speed. Seeing that, he finally deigned to move properly — sidestepping her volley and collecting fresh ammunition as he went.

And so the edge of a pine forest that had probably sat in silence for centuries received the noisiest half hour of its entire tenure.

❧ ❧ ❧

The tent went up. Pierre worked clean — the camp heater was already set, and two folding chairs stood side by side, aimed at the best patch of sky.

Baek Si-on crouched at the stove, lighting the fire. Flame licked up from the starter block, caught the wood, and settled into small crackling. He put the kettle on.

Choi Jin-ri, in thick gloves, watched for two seconds and decided she ought to contribute. She reached for the cups. Dropped one. Bent to retrieve it; her hat slid down over her eyes. Pushed the hat up; her other hand knocked the supply bag — and tissues, chocolate, and heat packs clattered out across the snow all at once.

Choi Jin-ri: “……”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

“Sit.”

“I can help.”

“You already helped.”

“Helped with what?”

“Drop-testing the camp supplies.”

She remained unconvinced. In the end she did successfully hand over two paper cups, and he accepted them with one word of high praise:

“Useful.”

She was instantly satisfied. Sitting back down in her folding chair, she concluded that she had personally contributed at least one percent of camp construction. That one percent mattered. Without paper cups, hot water loses its dignity.

They sat. The flame jumped in the stove, and the north wind combed the pines until they whispered.

“Cold?”

“A little.”

“How much is a little?”

She pulled her chin down into the scarf.

“If you don’t ask, I can still stand it.”

“Then I won’t ask.”

He fished a heat pack from his pocket, smacked it against his palm, and pushed it into her hands. She took it, peeled the packaging open, looked up at him.

“Aren’t you cold?”

“I just took seven snowballs from you.”

he said.

“My circulation is excellent.”

Choi Jin-ri: “……”

Of those seven, she was confident at most three had actually landed; where he got the other four she could not imagine. She did not argue. He had, after all, just handed her a heat pack.

❧ ❧ ❧

The sky went on darkening. A deep blue spread upward from the horizon, deepening with height, near-ink at the very top. The stars were bright — bright the way things are when someone has pinned them into the ceiling one at a time.

Pierre and the bodyguards built their own fire some distance off, murmuring; the wind carried the words away almost immediately. Between the two folding chairs, quiet remained.

She watched the sky and said nothing. Neither did he.

The kettle boiled; he poured two paper cups and brought one back for her.

“Thank you.”

She cradled it in both hands and blew gently on the steam. The water was boiled. The sky, meanwhile, was still performing exactly nothing.

Another hour passed. Stars still stars. Deep blue still deep blue. Not the first suggestion of a green curtain anywhere.

Her hands tightened slightly around the cup. She began tallying a very unprofitable ledger in her head: Baek Si-on had cleared his whole schedule for today, and the return on it was sitting in the north wind drinking plain hot water in front of an empty sky. She was afraid her wish had been too willful.

“Honestly, it’s fine if we don’t see it…”

“It isn’t fine.”

He cut across her.

She blinked.

“Huh?”

He was looking out at the dark.

“If it’s something you wanted to see, then not seeing it — of course that isn’t fine.”

Choi Jin-ri suddenly couldn’t say anything at all.

She had always believed that when a wish fails, you hurry to smile and say it’s fine — so no one feels put upon, and you don’t look pitiful. But that one sentence had come down, gently, on the hand she was habitually pulling back. Turned out you were allowed to admit the disappointment. Wanting a thing and not getting it was permitted to hurt.

She sniffed — the cold, or something else — and then took from her pocket a small object wrapped with great care in tissue.

Inside was the four-leaf clover. Slightly flattened now, but the leaves mostly whole. She unfolded the tissue and held it up in front of Baek Si-on.

“So I brought this.”

He looked down at the little clover with its slightly aggrieved leaves. She explained, very seriously:

“What science can’t guarantee — the four-leaf clover gets the final say.”

He did not dispute a single clause of this metaphysics. Instead he reached into the inner pocket of his down jacket, felt around, drew something out, and held it up for her to see.

A thin chain. Silver. At its hang, a very small four-leaf clover — natural malachite, gold-edged, translucent as though backlit.

Choi Jin-ri focused on it and went still.

“How do you have this?”

“I couldn’t find a real one. So I bought the kind that doesn’t wilt.”

That had been, roughly, a two-in-the-morning sort of thing.

Whether one album and two crowns would actually be cashed in, Baek Si-on had not been able to say for certain. ‘Uptown Funk’ was no ordinary opponent — a monster-level song that will be written into the history of pop. Different eras, different rules, different ways of listening; comparisons across the decades always carried variables. He hadn’t tried to suppress the uncertainty. It had simply evicted him from sleep.

And lying there, he had thought of Choi Jin-ri crouched on the lawn that day, flipping grass. So he had gone downstairs.

Back garden, two in the morning. A security guard on his flashlight rounds looked up and found a guest crouched on the lawn, phone torch aimed into the grass. The guard came over.

“Sir — you appear to be looking for something?”

“Luck.”

The guard was silent. That answer did not fit in a patrol report. He consulted his memory: the gardener had mentioned, that daytime, an Asian girl who spent a very long shift crouched on this lawn, hunting four-leaf clovers. Carefully, he tried:

“You’re… looking for a four-leaf clover?”

“You know where some are?”

The guard chose his words with diplomatic gentleness.

“A true four-leaf clover turns up in maybe one of ten thousand, sir — and found, it wilts fast in outdoor cold.”

“Van Cleef & Arpels does keep a four-leaf clover jewelry line. If it would help, I can call.”

Baek Si-on stood on the lawn, briefly stunned. Right. Why exactly was he digging in the dirt?

Four Seasons Beverly Hills · the overnight log entry that was never filed

2:07 a.m. — VIP guest located crouching on rear lawn, phone torch deployed. Stated objective: “Luck.”

2:20 a.m. — concierge line to Van Cleef & Arpels opened.

3:00 a.m. — delivery signed for at the porte-cochère.

Classification: the night the room rate finally justified itself.

Holding the chain now, Baek Si-on explained none of this. Choi Jin-ri didn’t press.

“Yours is the more expensive one.”

“Mm.”

“But mine was harder to find.”

He looked at her.

“Yes.”

He looped the chain once around a finger, said nothing further, and offered it to her. She didn’t take it.

“Put it on me.”

He paused one full second. She had already turned around, pulling the scarf down a little to bare the back of her neck. He unfolded the chain, lowered his head, and fastened the clasp. She felt the temperature of his fingertips — hotter, somehow, than the heat pack in her hands.

Turned back around, she looked down at the small clover resting at her chest. Green. Luminous.

“Pretty.”

she said, quietly. Then she tipped her head back to the sky. Still deep blue. Still nothing. But the anxiety had, without announcement, loosened its grip.

Baek Si-on sat back down and stirred the fire. The quiet closed over them again.

After who knows how long — she was nearly asleep — he said, suddenly:

“Jin-ri.”

“Mm?”

“The four-leaf clover gets the final say.”

She opened her eyes.

At the rim of the horizon, a pale green curtain of light was surfacing, slow as breath. At first, only a line. Then the line began to spread and churn, widening, brightening — a river flowing without a sound, rising off the end of the snowfield into the sky.

Choi Jin-ri came up out of the chair like a spring.

“It’s HERE!”

She grabbed Baek Si-on’s hand and shook it hard through two layers of glove, a kid who had just pulled the best present out from under the tree.

“I’m seeing it! It really showed up!”

“Mm.”

“It’s the four-leaf clover!”

“Mm. The four-leaf clover is very impressive.”

Head tipped back, eyes unblinking, she watched the green spread. It wasn’t loud like fireworks, wasn’t fierce like stage light — but the whole night, without being asked, stepped aside and made room.

Baek Si-on turned his head and looked at her instead. The aurora ran through her eyes, and the eyes that had always been bright burned brighter still. He found himself thinking that the canceled schedule, and whatever Scooter’s blood pressure was doing in Los Angeles right now, might both have been worth it.

She registered the weight of his gaze and turned, slowly.

“Aren’t you going to watch the aurora? Why are you looking at me?”

“It already showed up.”

“And?”

“And now I should be looking at the rarer thing.”

Her heartbeat skipped one beat, then restarted at a frequency no cardiologist would endorse. He meant her — right? He was saying she was rarer than the aurora — right?

— between two folding chairs, Churchill, minus thirty degrees

So. The mood was built. Which meant the next step…

She peeked at him. He was still watching her. Whether the aurora’s tenderness was contagious or not, even that usually over-calm face of his looked, tonight, almost soft.

She gave herself roughly three seconds of civil war. Then, with the air of someone crossing a point of no return, she reached up and pulled the heavy scarf down off her face. The cold hit her cheeks instantly; there was no longer time to care.

Choi Jin-ri closed her eyes. Tilted forward, slightly. Let her lips drift closer.

Three centimeters. Two. One.

Contact.

Not the softness she had projected. Hard. Thick. Slightly rough.

Her eyes opened. Her lips were pressed against Baek Si-on’s glove.

“At this temperature, the surface moisture on lips freezes.”

“Do you want to fly home tomorrow with two split, well-done sausages for a mouth?”

Choi Jin-ri: “……”

Her expression completed a full circuit in three seconds: anticipation, to bewilderment, to disbelief, to a sincere ambition to bury him in the snow. She had marshaled her entire courage and received, in return, a physics lecture on moisture condensation and the formation of sausage-mouth?

Did this man even have a heart? A polar bear understood atmosphere better than he did!

Her fist closed. She hauled back to swing it—

—and Baek Si-on moved the hand that had been in front of her face.

“What I mean is—”

Her fist hung midair.

He looked down at her.

“A thing like this — a girl shouldn’t have to be the one who goes first.”

And he lowered his head and kissed her.

The physics lecture in her head was smashed to powder. Her hand hovered in the air with nowhere on earth to be, and finally settled for gripping, very lightly, the hem of his coat.

The aurora flowed overhead, without a sound. The kettle gurgled beside the stove, breathing steam. In the distance, Pierre and the two bodyguards stood with their backs to all of it, facing the opposite direction with the highest professional ethics.

❝

Minus thirty degrees. The lips did not freeze. They did not stick together. He lied.

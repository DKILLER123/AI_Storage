Chapter Eighty-Nine

The Best Actor’s Versailles Demystification

The next day 13:00 · then 14:15 Seokgwan-dong · K-Arts · Lecture Hall 1 Four hundred in a room for two. Don’t copy me.

The next day.

One in the afternoon.

Seongbuk-gu.

Korea National University of Arts.

Seokgwan-dong campus.

In the drama school’s highest-grade Lecture Hall 1, the high-power air conditioner that on ordinary days was enough to make the first-row students pull up blankets had, at this hour, fully declared itself paralysed.

There were simply too many people.

A space that could take two hundred had now been stuffed, hard, with nearly four hundred.

The empty floor at the front was packed with first- and second-years sitting cross-legged. On the sloped stair-aisles at both sides you could not even find a crack to put a foot.

Oxygen in the whole space was extremely thin.

A back-row corner.

A acting-department boy in black-framed glasses was tugging his collar to fan himself.

In the tail of his eye.

A woman in a black baseball cap slid in sideways through the door crack.

The brim was pressed very low, but it was useless. Press it to the bridge of the nose and you would still know her.

He hurried to elbow the classmate beside him.

“Look. The star sunbae’s here!”

The friend turned the way he was pointing. Eyes went a full circle wider.

“Kim Go-eun? Isn’t she supposed to be on the Chinatown set right now? She ran back too?”

Kim Go-eun

Actress · K-Arts acting · class of 2010

Real-name counterpart · Eungyo’s rookie sweep · supposed to be on Coin Locker Girl

A black cap pulled to the nose, and still everyone knew her. She had already taken the newcomer prizes. She still asked a fierce woman director for a day off, to see how a twenty-two-year-old statue breathed.

“Do you even have to think?”

Black-frames looked at him with contempt.

“This is the only occasion in the country where you can privately see a living twenty-two-year-old Venice Best Actor. Never mind asking the set for leave — even skipping work and paying the penalty, plenty of people would come to see how this statue breathes.”

“…Fair.”

At the same time.

Third row, mid-rake, to the right.

Park So-dam, who had come extremely early and successfully grabbed a proper wooden seat, saw Kim Go-eun struggling to move in.

Park So-dam

Actress · K-Arts · class of 2010

Real-name counterpart · a Veteran cameo still wet on the résumé

Same year as Go-eun. Go-eun had already swept the newcomer prizes with Eungyo. She was still bouncing off commercial auditions, turning in independent shorts that paid in dust. In this hall the class friendship still held. She had saved half a seat.

In this cruelly competitive top school, as classmates who had entered the same year — the class of 2010 — Kim Go-eun was already the absolute weather, the one who had swept the newcomer prizes with Eungyo.

She herself was still bouncing off commercial-film auditions, able only to turn in independent shorts poor enough to shed crumbs.

The class gap existed.

But at school, the revolutionary friendship of the same year still held reasonably firm.

Park So-dam lifted a hand at once and waved twice in the air.

Kim Go-eun saw it.

She squeezed along a crack narrow enough to be almost a line, and sat, barely, on the edge of the half-seat Park So-dam had occupied with her life.

“Oh my god…”

Kim Go-eun took off the cap, raked the short hair sweat had glued to her forehead, and smiled at the classmate and best friend beside her.

“Don’t you have a call too? Why did you come here to take a sauna?”

Park So-dam rolled her eyes, speechless.

“I’m in Director Ryoo Seung-wan’s Veteran. All in, I don’t even have three minutes of face. One more of me, one less of me — nobody cares. You, though. That fierce woman director actually let her big female lead come back to school?”

Kim Go-eun propped her chin on the back of her hand, gaze going over the dense backs of heads in front, watching the still-empty lectern.

“A scene of this grade, of course you have to have the face to ask for leave and come back~”

“…”

The two of them were still chirping.

The side door at the front of the hall was pushed open.

All talk dropped to zero in an instant.

Dean Choi walked in, both hands on the lectern, gaze sharp, sweeping once around the airtight human net below.

Dean Choi

Dean · School of Drama · K-Arts

OC · tortoise-shell glasses · a vanity that did its own typing

Silvering hair combed back, a charcoal blazer over a black turtleneck, the smile of a man who has invited a trophy into a school and would like the school to remember who opened the door. He speaks in complete sentences. The students have learned to wait for the full stop.

“Today’s special lecture, I don’t think I need to do much introduction.”

“These past two weeks, from first year to fourth, our drama department has shown an extremely serious disgust for study. You’ve started to disdain movement class. You’ve started to dodge diction class. You think the set in the textbook is too rigid. You think if you rely on instinct and going mad you can act.”

Four hundred people below, not a crow, not a sparrow.

“So today, I have personally invited the principal culprit who started this crisis of faith in the academic school.”

“Please welcome the seventy-first Venice International Film Festival’s Best Actor—”

“Baek Si-on.”

He turned sideways, put out a hand toward the side door in a “please.”

The side door was pushed open from outside again.

Baek Si-on walked in.

Black crew-neck T-shirt, black trousers, black sneakers.

No business formal on top of him, and no high-grade hair and makeup.

But this young man in the most ordinary clothes, on the first step into the hall—

Every heaven’s-favourite of the professional school in the room stood at once.

Thunderous applause tore around the rake.

Si-on stood beside the lectern, waited ten seconds in complete ease, lifted one hand, pressed down.

Everyone sat back in order.

K-Arts · Lecture Hall 1 · special lecture

Hello. My name is Baek Si-on.

I never went to university. I was a dancing idol for a few years, a private for two, and after discharge I started acting.

The above is my entire academic background.

Dean Choi just said I am the principal culprit who started your crisis of faith in the academic school.

That hat is a bit large. But since I came, I’ll wear it and talk.

A lot of you in the middle think that because I took Best Actor in Europe with The Green Fly, that proves the most painful immersive Method — the kind that pulls the soul completely out — is the highest law of acting. You think fundamentals are the shackles that bind a genius.

Of course, that the Method can give birth to extremely great performances — I don’t deny. After all I now have the qualification to stand here and say so.

But there is one real situation I have not mentioned in any interview. Today I plan to share it with you for free.

This film’s only full-equity producer and investor is my own mother. The producer, writer, and director is my own uncle.

What does that mean?

It means that on that set, I was a privileged class with absolute firing rights and capital to be capricious.

I did not need to worry about going over budget. I did not need to worry about being replaced. Because that unit was there to play with me alone.

You may feel this is showing off — that only people whose families have property have the qualification to be a pure Method actor.

More or less that is the meaning.

The Method needs time. It needs a large amount of time that cannot be compressed, with no interference. And time, in this industry, is the most expensive resource.

First. Say you graduate, and finally wait for a good role. The director wants a gambler. You decide to use the Method, and soak in a casino for half a year. You really go. Eat and drink with those people, lose money with them, smoke at a table at four in the morning until your hands shake. But three days before cameras. A newcomer prettier than you, more gifted than you, with a whole capital side hard-selling them behind, drops into the unit and replaces you. Can that half year of so-called experience buy tomorrow’s breakfast?

Second. Congratulations. You were not replaced. You became that role extremely perfectly. You offered the most deranged, most explosive, career-best performance of your twenty-some years. But after the film opened, the screens came down in three days. The audience did not buy it. The box office was miserable. Your performance was drowned in a film nobody watched. Do you still have the confidence to go experience another year, another half year, for the next role? Can you afford that cost in time? Who pays the rent? Are your parents willing to watch you disappear for another half year?

So the final conclusion I stand here to give today is—

Go with the current. Suit the time.

If you walk into a daily-drama unit that takes the script and ten minutes later yells action, those so-called Method divine arts in your head are waste paper with no use at all. What can save your life then is only the dry, rigid movement and diction fundamentals Dean Choi has been stressing, the ones formed by muscle memory.

Do not blindly worship, still less deify, any one acting method. Choose the most efficient method according to your own position, the prep cycle the unit gives you, and the balance in your bank account.

People who have taken Best Actor and Best Actress on pure method and control technique are not one fewer than the mad ones of the Method.

The above is everything I came to say.

Thank you.

The floor was quiet a few seconds.

Then the whole room stood again.

Applause exploded from four hundred pairs of palms at once.

Even Dean Choi, leaning by the door, nodded along, clapping.

In fact this set of reasoning about time-cost and industrial law that Si-on had just spoken, the old professors in the college had already turned over and told these professional students countless times.

But like children in absolute adolescent rebellion.

Facing those theory-faction professors who had never in their lives touched the edge of an international cup, the students would only feel that those rigid sermons about compromise were the rage of the mediocre, the incompetent — and would not take in so much as half a punctuation mark.

✿ ✿ ✿

When Si-on came out the side door, the air in the corridor was at least three degrees cooler than the hall.

He drew a deep breath.

The turbid air that four hundred people’s body heat had baked for twenty minutes was swapped out of the lungs by the cold.

Walked two steps.

Saw a figure leaning on the corridor wall.

Choi Jin-ri.

She was leaning on the wall with nothing to do, one toe tapping the floor, looking down at her phone.

Heard footsteps. Lifted her head.

The moment she saw Si-on, a smile rose on her face.

Half of this grey corridor was lit by that smile.

She waved at him.

The sleeve too long. Waving, you only saw a wad of cloth moving in the air.

Si-on walked over.

“How did you get here?”

“I heard from Eun-ah you were lecturing at K-Arts today. I happened to have something nearby, so I dropped—”

The sentence not finished.

The side door behind Si-on was shoved open hard.

The main door opened too.

Students like a barrier lake that had broken, pouring from both directions at once.

First to reach Si-on were three second-year boys, pens and notebooks clenched in their hands.

“Sunbae Baek Si-on! Can we have a signature?!”

“Sunbae! Can we have a photo?!”

Then the third-year girls.

Then the fourth-years.

Last the first-year freshmen squeezed out of the back-row corner. They ran slowest and shouted loudest.

In twenty seconds Si-on was ringed into a human circle about two metres across.

Choi Jin-ri was pushed two steps back by the flow, back to the corridor wall.

She did not squeeze back in.

She put both hands in her pockets and watched, quiet.

Si-on in the circle, ringed by dozens of faces just past twenty.

Someone passing a pen, someone passing a notebook, someone holding up a phone, someone purely standing beside him looking.

Si-on did not refuse anyone.

Signatures for the signatures. Photos for the photos.

Someone asked him the concrete prep method during the shoot. He answered short.

Someone asked how he controlled emotion under high pressure. He answered that too.

This state lasted about five minutes.

The circle grew from two metres to three, then from three to the whole width of the corridor.

Behind, people were still pouring out of the hall without end, as if the break in the barrier lake were tearing wider.

Si-on had already signed twenty-some names, taken a dozen photos, answered at least seven or eight professional questions.

Then a shout with real lung from the far end of the corridor exploded over.

“All of you scatter!!!”

Dean Choi stood at the corridor corner, both hands on his hips.

“He came to give a lecture, not to open a signing for you! You lot can’t even get your lines down on ordinary days, but chasing a star, the legs are quick!”

The circle loosened.

“Third years, fourth years! You still have the face to block a person here? Is next week’s midterm staging ready or not?”

“Second years! I still haven’t seen the makeup work for movement class!”

“First years! You don’t even have a clear idea who I am yet, do you! Go back!”

Absolute bloodline suppression.

The crowd that had been boiling a moment ago was instantly as if a basin of ice water had been dumped on their heads.

Students put away phones and notebooks at once, bowed properly in greeting, then heads down, began a fast withdrawal toward the stairs.

But the retreating flow, passing Choi Jin-ri, clearly slowed.

Someone had recognised her.

They exchanged a look, excited, and someone had already fished half of the phone just put away back out.

But the tail of the eye caught Dean Choi staring this way, hard.

Everyone very obediently swallowed the impulse to ask for a photo, sped up, and slunk off until they were gone.

The corridor was completely empty.

Dean Choi turned his head.

Looking at the young man in front of him who had just given four hundred professional students a class in cruel sociology, the gloom and severity on his face came off clean.

“Si-on-ah.”

“Dean Choi.”

Si-on inclined a little.

Dean Choi looked at him. In the gaze there was something complicated.

Appraising, moved, and a little wetness that could not be named.

“You’re looking more and more like your father.”

Si-on did not speak.

Dean Choi had known Si-on’s father, Baek Jeong-hwan.

Not a nodding acquaintance.

In his lifetime Baek Jeong-hwan had been section chief of arts education at the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism. K-Arts was the ministry’s only directly attached national arts university. All budget approvals, department evaluations, policy docking, had to go through that section.

Dean Choi, from lecturer to dean, had sat countless times across the long table in a Culture Ministry meeting room from that section chief in gold-wire glasses, talking admissions quotas and the allocation of funds.

From official business to private, from budgets to children, from the meeting room to a soju stall at the roadside.

Later Baek Jeong-hwan was gone.

He had sent a wreath to the funeral.

“If your father could see with his own eyes today that you had carried the Venice Best Actor cup back to Seoul, he would be extremely proud.”

“The thing that should make him proud is me taking Hallyu to the world.”

Dean Choi stalled a second.

Laugh-lines spread from the corners of the eyes toward the temple. He patted Si-on’s shoulder.

“Different roads, same arrival. Come often when you have time. You don’t have to lecture. Come watch the students’ stagings, give them a note.”

“All right.”

Dean Choi nodded once, then jerked his chin toward behind him.

“Go. Don’t let your girlfriend wait until she’s anxious.”

Si-on looked back at Choi Jin-ri, and when he turned again to explain, found Dean Choi already gone.

The body even cleaner than the actors on the stage.

Forget it.

Explaining a relationship status to the back of a head already almost at the corridor corner was a behaviour that did not hold, physically or logically.

He turned.

Choi Jin-ri still leaning at the wall, both hands in the coat pockets, head tilted, looking at him.

“Let’s go.”

“Mm.”

The two of them walked out along the corridor side by side.

Afternoon sun came in from the floor window at the far end, pulling both their shadows very long.

“How did the talk go?”

Choi Jin-ri turned her head to look at him.

“All right.”

“What did you talk?”

“Study properly. Don’t copy me.”

Choi Jin-ri laughed once.

“Very Baek Si-on.”

“Meaning?”

“Just… using the most punchable method to say the most solid reasoning.”

“…I’ll choose to take that as a compliment.”

Coming out the teaching-building doors, the ginkgo on campus had begun to put out the first thin layer of pale yellow.

Si-on walked two steps toward the parking lot, looked back.

“You wanted me for something?”

Choi Jin-ri caught up, came beside him.

“Wanted to take you to a meal.”

“Reason?”

“I went back yesterday and talked with the company’s management.”

Her tone was not the usual.

On ordinary days, when SM came up, her voice always carried a layer of something wrapped several times.

Today the voice was clean.

“Talked what?”

“Talked very seriously. The way you said in Venice. Sit down, face to face, from the start to the end, say everything in my heart.”

Si-on’s step slowed half a beat.

He looked at her.

Choi Jin-ri met his gaze. Did not hide.

“About my personal direction. About my relationship with the group. About the road I want to walk. All of it.”

“And the result?”

“They respect my choice. They agreed I walk a solo development line.”

The corner of her mouth bent a very shallow arc.

“Of course, I think the larger reason is the company has already prepared to concentrate resources on Red Velvet, so they went with the current and agreed.”

Si-on heard it.

Nodded once.

“Oh.”

Choi Jin-ri waited two seconds. No further text arrived.

“Just ‘oh’?”

“Congratulations.”

Si-on glanced at the Jaeger on his wrist.

Two fifteen in the afternoon.

“But skip the meal. I still have something. Another day.”

Choi Jin-ri’s smile took in a little.

“Something important?”

“Mm. Have to talk a new song with my producer.”

Choi Jin-ri’s eyes lit.

“A new song?”

She tilted her head.

“The one you wrote on the plane?”

“No.”

“Really?”

“Nothing to do with that.”

“Oh~~~”

Choi Jin-ri’s “oh” dragged an extremely unnecessary long note, the tone falling then rising, the tail up, up very high.

Si-on chose to ignore that tone.

“Then I’m going.”

“Can I come along and watch?”

She put in a companion application, smooth.

“You’re that free?”

Choi Jin-ri nodded, frank.

“The group has no group activity this month. Unni Victoria and Soo-jung are both on sets pulling all-nighters. Amber’s been spinning preparing her solo album. Luna’s in the practice room every day killing herself on vocals, rehearsing for that musical the company made.”

She counted on her fingers, reporting all four members’ calendars.

“I’m the only one with nothing to do.”

Si-on thought.

Taking her to the Hapjeong-dong studio, watching Jung Jae-joon grind that rock out, did not seem a bad thing either.

At least the violent tracks could plug the mouth that wanted to joke about the eighth-grade diary.

“Fine. Let’s go.”

“Mm!”

Choi Jin-ri’s eyes bent back.

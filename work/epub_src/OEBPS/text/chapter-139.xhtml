Chapter One Hundred Thirty Nine

What Kind of Person Wears Court Robes to an Awards Ceremony?

November 8 Late afternoon Seoul · the Insight newsroom · a room with three desks and three paid employees An iced americano, a phone call, and an order to go out and photograph a masterpiece of world art.

Park Jae-won had just pushed a straw into an iced americano and not yet taken his first sip when the phone rang.

The caller.

Son Nam-won.

Park Jae-won looked at the screen and felt his stomach drop.

His boss did not call at this hour to ask what he felt like for dinner. His boss called at this hour when something had happened — and, in Park Jae-won’s experience of the past few months, it had become wise to let that thought finish itself before the phone stopped ringing.

He answered.

Park Jae-won ← Son Nam-won · Insight newsroom · incoming

“Jamsil Sports Complex. Now. Immediately.”

“Jamsil? What’s happening over there today?”

“Something.”

“What?”

“A masterpiece of world art.”

“……”

His boss had been talking more and more like a fortune teller lately.

Which would have been fine, except that every single “fortune” had turned out to be accurate in a way that was frankly unsettling.

“I’m leaving now.”

The call ended.

Park Jae-won grabbed the long-lens bag and ran for the door.

The colleague at the next desk looked up.

“Where now?”

“Jamsil.”

“Shooting what?”

Park Jae-won stopped.

Thought about it.

“Art.”

The colleague: “?”

November 8 · the same afternoon Somewhere on Olympic-daero Inside a company car that had never been driven this fast by its own insurance A reporter who suspects his boss of being a creditor, and the corporate structure that keeps the suspicion exactly where it is.

The car bounced along the road toward the Olympic-daero expressway, and the further it went, the more certain Park Jae-won became that whatever this was, it had Baek Si-on written on it somewhere.

He had not thought much about it before.

It was the media business, after all.

Who in the industry did not have some money behind them?

But lately, the more he turned it over, the less it added up.

His boss’s reaction to any piece of news involving Baek Si-on was not the reaction of a journalist who had caught a scoop.

It was the reaction of a creditor who had just watched the god of money walk through his door.

So the night before last, unable to help himself, Park Jae-won had quietly pulled up the most recent shareholder structure for Insight.

What he found had made him put the phone down, pick it up, and look again.

Insight’s controlling fifty-one percent now sat with a company whose name sounded extremely civilised and whose registered address was extremely far away.

Cheongsong Investment.

He had gone one layer deeper.

The director of record was a trustee agent.

No actual controlling person was identifiable.

The money trail had been swept so clean you could have eaten off it.

After S.W Studio had been set up, Seo Eun-ju had rebuilt Baek Si-on’s asset structure for him from the ground up.

The shareholdings that would have looked conspicuous under Baek Si-on’s own name — the fifty-one percent of Insight, the thirty percent of Big Hit Entertainment — had all been moved into Cheongsong Investment, registered in the Cayman Islands.

Cheongsong Investment’s director of record was a trustee agent.

And the sole beneficiary of that trust was S.W Studio.

What the outside world saw was perfectly ordinary: foreign capital taking an interest in Korean new media and entertainment.

What the outside world did not see was that the real money simply looped back, at the end of the line, to Baek Si-on himself.

Clean.

Compliant.

And very hard to chase.

Park Jae-won, of course, could not see that last layer.

He could only drive, and keep privately wondering what on earth his boss and Baek Si-on were to each other.

They could not be relatives.

It did not look like that, either. Put Son Nam-won’s face next to Baek Si-on’s and the only thing they had in common was that they both had two eyes.

November 8 · later The air arriving before the sound does Jamsil Sports Complex · the temporary helipad south of Jamsil Stadium Five police cars, a convoy of black sedans, and a sound like the sky being unzipped.

The car made it as far as the outer ring of Jamsil Sports Complex, and no further. Three hundred metres short, a police officer held up a hand.

“Road’s closed ahead.”

Park Jae-won pulled over to the kerb, slung the camera bag over one shoulder, and ran toward Jamsil on foot.

At the second police line he finally got a clear look at what was inside.

Beside the temporary helipad on the south side of Jamsil Stadium stood five or six police cars.

Their lights were on.

Their sirens were not.

Behind the police cars waited a line of black Hyundai Aslans — the same model Baek Si-on had stood in front of at a launch dais in COEX not long ago, only this time there was a whole fleet of them, idling in formation.

Park Jae-won took two steps back, raised the camera, and took one wide shot.

For the record.

Fleet size, police count, helipad position — all of it would have to be checked and re-checked later, and a wide shot was how you kept the geometry honest.

Then he clipped on the Insight press badge and set up the long lens.

The position was excellent.

He could see the helipad.

He could see the convoy.

He could see the temporary police cordon.

Park Jae-won had just finished setting his parameters when a low drone came rolling in from the sky.

At first it was very far away.

And very muffled.

Then it got clearer, and closer, and louder, with the particular heavy thump of rotors working hard against their own noise.

Everyone around the helipad looked up at the same moment.

The police began easing people back.

On the Hyundai side, men opened doors and moved into position to receive whoever was coming.

Park Jae-won’s stomach tightened. He put the camera to his eye at once.

In the frame —

A silver-white helicopter was pressing down out of the grey-blue Seoul sky.

Under its belly, the blue SK Telecom logo was so crisp it was almost absurd.

And on the tail, the big “T” made of signal bars looked faintly ridiculous in the wash of rotor wind — as though connectivity itself had come to claim a parking space.

Park Jae-won stared.

An SK helicopter?

A Hyundai convoy?

Police escorts?

What exactly was this — a chaebol mixer?

The helicopter settled slowly.

The rotor wash whipped the cordon tape against its posts hard enough to crack.

Ground crew ran out, bent low at the waist.

The cabin door opened.

Park Jae-won’s finger was already resting on the shutter.

And then —

A man in a blue gonryongpo bent his way out of the helicopter door.

His coronet sat perfectly level.

His robe blew backward in the wind.

He looked like someone had torn a figure straight out of an eighteenth-century Joseon painting and dropped him, by some power no one could name, onto a temporary helipad at Jamsil Sports Complex in November 2014.

Suddenly Park Jae-won understood exactly what Son Nam-won had meant by “a masterpiece of world art.”

His hand moved almost by instinct.

Click. Click. Click. Click.

The shutter fired in a burst so dense it sounded like a small machine gun.

Between frames, Park Jae-won’s brain was already drafting headlines at full speed.

“Billboard Champion Descends on Jamsil in the Crown Prince’s Robes.”

No. Too ordinary.

“An SK Helicopter, a Hyundai Convoy, Police Escorts — and Baek Si-on …”

Too long.

“The Crown Prince of Sado Holds Court, and Modern Korea Gets Out of the Way.”

That one had something to it.

While he was still turning it over, Baek Si-on was already being walked toward the lead Aslan by an SK supervisor on one side and a Hyundai convoy supervisor on the other.

The door opened.

Baek Si-on bent and climbed in.

Baek Eun-ah, Park Ji-hun and the bodyguards followed and were gone into the car in a few seconds.

The police cars moved out first, red-and-blue lights flashing without a sound.

Then the Hyundai convoy followed, one car at a time, unhurried and exact.

Only when the last car had vanished around the bend did Park Jae-won slowly lower the camera.

Then he started going through the playback.

Frame after frame after frame.

And the more he looked, the more clearly he felt a certainty settle over him.

The entire entertainment-photography scene in Korea was going to go quiet for a week because of what was in his camera.

This was not entertainment news material anymore.

This was a slice of an era.

Korea’s entertainment industry held a red carpet every single day. Airport photos every single day. “God-tier visuals,” “raw shots,” “vibes” — every single day.

But a man in the Crown Prince Sado’s costume, stepping off an SK helicopter and into a police-escorted Hyundai Aslan convoy?

A normal photographer might not see that even once in a lifetime.

And he had it.

Park Jae-won looked at the pictures one more time, then sent Son Nam-won a message.

KakaoTalk · Son Nam-won

Park Jae-won

Got the shot.

Son Nam-won

Send it back now.

Less than three seconds later.

Park Jae-won looked at the screen and smiled a little.

His boss had not asked whether the pictures came out well.

Because his boss already knew: if the shot had been taken at all, it could not be bad.

November 8 · the same hour Three minutes of red light, and counting The arterial road out of Jamsil · an intersection that has decided to become a museum A silver Kia K5, a client meeting dying in real time, and a national honour that needs the lane.

At the same moment, on the main road between Jamsil and the awards venue, a silver Kia K5 sat stopped at a red light.

In the driver’s seat, a thirty-something office worker named Kim Min-su stared at the signal and felt his eyebrows draw closer and closer together.

The light had been red for nearly three minutes.

By any normal timing, this intersection should have let him through long ago.

It had not.

To make it worse, he had a client meeting.

Kim Min-su slapped the steering wheel.

“Damn it — which big shot is on the move today?”

His day had not started well. That morning his section chief had chewed him out in the department meeting, lunch had been a rice ball from a convenience store, and now he was pinned at an intersection like a bug under glass.

Life’s three great miseries: work, traffic, and traffic on the way to work.

The car behind him was losing patience. Somebody hit a horn.

Beep.

Kim Min-su wanted to hit his own. But there was a traffic officer standing right there at the intersection.

Fine.

He did not particularly want to be pulled over to the side of the road for a life lesson delivered by a man in a reflective vest.

And then —

Far down the road, two police cars appeared.

Lights flashing.

They crossed the intersection one after the other.

Behind them came a line of identical black Hyundai Aslans, every single one of them immaculate.

Kim Min-su blinked.

“The President’s convoy?”

He craned forward.

“No — that’s not what the presidential convoy looks like, is it?”

In the next lane over, a taxi driver rolled down his window and craned out to look.

“Hyundai convoy?”

“The Chung family?”

“Who knows.”

Somewhere behind him somebody had a phone out and was filming. Somebody else was cursing. A few people were just watching, the way you watch weather.

Kim Min-su reached for his own phone and opened Naver to search.

He never got there. Before the news page could even load, a Twitter notification jumped in first.

He did not use Twitter much.

But the name Baek Si-on had been so loud lately that a couple of days earlier he had followed a few brand accounts on a whim — and among them, SK Telecom.

The newest post had gone up moments ago.

SK Telecom · Twitter · just now

“From the 18th century to a global stage in 40 minutes.” — English copy

“Punctuality is respect for national honour.” — Korean copy

Attached: one blurred long shot — a silver-white helicopter, a blue SK Telecom logo, and a figure in blue robes bending to board.

Kim Min-su stared at the screen.

Then he looked up at the direction the black convoy had just taken.

Then back down at the screen.

“What the hell…”

The eighteenth century?

A global stage?

National honour?

That copy read like an advertising agency hallucinating after three straight nights with no sleep.

He was about to open the comments when the horn behind him went off again.

Beep. Beeeep.

The light had turned green.

The traffic officer lowered his hand.

The line of cars began to move.

Kim Min-su fumbled his phone onto the console, floored the accelerator, and the Kia rolled forward, still muttering to himself.

“Who the hell was that, anyway?”

A few seconds later, a young woman in the car in the next lane — window down — shouted across to her companion, bright with excitement:

“It’s Baek Si-on!”

Kim Min-su: “……”

All at once, he decided that the extra minutes he had just spent at that light were not entirely unacceptable.

After all, in this day and age, being stuck in traffic at the exact site where Korean popular-culture history was being made was a strange kind of luck.

The only question was whether his client would see it that way.

Probably not.

The client would only ask:

“Deputy Manager Kim, why are you late?”

And what was he supposed to say to that?

The Crown Prince of Sado dropped out of the eighteenth century onto Jamsil, and national honour needed the road.

November 8 · still later Not quite on time, despite everything The National Theater of Korea · main entrance The red carpet is gone, the press area is gone, and a door attendant’s “ssi” nearly becomes “Your Highness.”

For all that the combination — an SK helicopter, a Hyundai convoy, police escorts — was outrageous enough to be written into a corporate promotional film, Baek Si-on still arrived late.

The ceremony had already begun.

The red carpet had been rolled up.

The press area had been dismantled.

The theater staff at the entrance had braced themselves to receive a late-arriving guest — and then looked up, saw Baek Si-on step out of the Hyundai Aslan, and their expressions short-circuited on the spot.

The sentence they had prepared was “Baek Si-on-ssi, this way, please.”

One look at the outfit, and the “-ssi” nearly came out as “Your Highness.”

Since they were late anyway, Park Ji-hun had stopped rushing.

He opened the styling case he had carried aboard, pressed powder quickly under Baek Si-on’s nose and eyes, then carefully reset the coronet, the collar, and the belt.

“Let’s go in.”

The usher finally came back to himself and hurried ahead to lead the way.

The party entered the theater through the main doors.

Thick carpet ran down the corridors, swallowing footsteps. From somewhere ahead, the main stage’s music came through the walls, low frequencies thudding against the floor in steady pulses.

November 8 · the same evening The ceremony in progress The National Theater of Korea · the main hall Half a year-end music show, half a ministry commendation ceremony, one aisle left open, and a rumour moving through the rows.

On stage, a girl group in sequined stage outfits was performing the congratulatory segment between awards, moving through a perfectly drilled formation under a dense drum beat.

The cameras kept cutting.

The hall was full to the back walls.

In the front rows sat government and Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism officials, heads of industry associations, and assorted invited heavyweights, all in suits.

In the middle sat actors, directors, producers, singers, and variety stars.

Further back, row after row of K-pop idols, dressed and preened like peacocks heading to a ball.

An official state ceremony it was supposed to be — and from a distance it looked like half a year-end music festival and half a culture-ministry commendation assembly had been wedged into the same building.

A fair number of the idols in the audience had been whispering among themselves all along, because word of Baek Si-on’s lateness had already travelled.

“I heard he’s still filming down in Jeonju.”

“Didn’t they say he was coming by helicopter?”

“Helicopter?”

“SK’s.”

“Ya — really?”

“The Hyundai convoy went down there too.”

“What kind of drama plot is this?”

Some said it in hushed voices. Some refused to believe it at all.

And then —

The big doors at the back of the hall opened.

Very quietly.

But the people in the back rows felt the air move first.

A few idols turned around automatically.

And froze.

Baek Si-on was walking in from the very back of the hall, escorted by staff.

A blue gonryongpo.

A coronet set perfectly level.

The congratulatory performance was still running; the lights were still on the stage; by every rule of live events, the audience’s attention should have stayed in front of them.

It did not.

From the moment Baek Si-on stepped through the doors, the gazes at the back of the hall began to travel forward like a thread being pulled — first the idol section, then the actor section, then the producers and directors in the middle, and finally even some officials in the front row, nudged by a quiet word from a neighbour, turned to look.

And stared.

The picture was simply unreasonable.

An official awards ceremony. A modern K-pop stage on it. Suits and gowns and flash photography and state commendations in the seats.

And Baek Si-on, in the court robes of an eighteenth-century Joseon crown prince, coming down the aisle from the back of the hall.

As if the door of a period-drama set had been opened onto the wrong room.

As if a young prince, delivered through a crack in time, had wandered by mistake into the 2014 Popular Culture and Arts Awards.

Everyone he passed was looking at him.

A boy idol who had debuted only recently had been sitting very straight. The moment he saw Baek Si-on pass his row, he started to rise out of his seat by reflex to bow —

And the leader beside him slammed a hand onto his thigh.

He could not stand.

The live cameras were sweeping the hall.

The boy froze halfway out of his chair and completed the ninety-degree bow with his eyes alone.

Red Velvet sat further back and toward the inside of the hall.

Park Soo-young saw him first.

She had been watching the stage properly, heard the sharp intake of breath around her, turned her head — and clapped a hand over her mouth.

“Eonni …”

Kang Seul-gi followed her gaze.

Irene turned as well.

All three of them went quiet at the same moment.

Irene looked at the gonryongpo, and a thought surfaced in her head that was extremely inappropriate for the occasion:

A god had clocked off its shift and, on the way home, switched into the crown-prince commute.

She pushed the thought down at once — because as Baek Si-on passed along the aisle on their side, he turned his head a fraction and gave a small nod in the direction of the juniors who had all snapped to attention.

On stage, the performing idols had noticed that something was wrong.

The audience’s attention, which should have been on them, had plainly leaked away from the front and the sides all at once.

A main dancer glanced down at the audience mid-blocking.

And nearly missed her mark.

Only muscle memory saved her — she wrenched her body back into formation and kept dancing.

Inside, she was already cursing.

Who? Who is that flashy?

She stole another glance on a turn.

Got a good look this time.

Baek Si-on.

In a Joseon crown prince’s robes.

Walking up the hall from the back.

The main dancer: “……”

All right.

That man stole attention fair and square.

You could not out-steal that. You really could not.

November 8 · the same moment A hand trembles on a side camera The broadcast control room Where a director decides, in about half a second, that showing a crown prince is not rudeness. It is accident prevention.

In the control room, the main camera was following the performance on stage, easing in smoothly with the girl group’s blocking.

Everything was going by the book.

Until the cameraman on the side auxiliary camera saw Baek Si-on.

His hand jerked.

The director hissed into his headset.

“Steady!”

The next moment, he saw it himself on the return feed.

And gave the order without hesitation.

“Camera two, stay on him. Don’t push in too tight — full body first.”

“Camera three, get ready to cut to the audience reaction.”

“Main camera stays on the stage. Cut to the crowd when the chorus ends.”

The assistant PD beside him whispered:

“Cutting to him now — won’t that be rude to the act on stage?”

The director kept his eyes on the monitor.

The girl group was still giving it everything on stage.

Down in the hall, Baek Si-on was crossing the whole auditorium in his crown-prince robes.

The director hesitated for exactly half a second.

“This isn’t rudeness.”

He said it flatly.

“This is live-broadcast accident prevention.”

The assistant PD: “?”

The director added:

“If we don’t show him, the viewers will call us blind.”

And so, at the next transition in the on-stage performance, the live broadcast cut — and on millions of screens across Korea, the picture of girls dancing on a stage was replaced by the picture of a man in gonryongpo walking down the aisle of an auditorium.

The moment the screen cut to Baek Si-on, the live comments detonated.

The density of the comments quickly buried the live picture.

A large share of viewers simply turned the comments off.

By the time Baek Si-on had reached the middle of the hall, the screen was so plastered over with screaming and question marks that nothing else could be seen anyway.

The level of detonation was almost exactly like the Venice red carpet, back when Baek Si-on and Choi Jin-ri had walked arm in arm into the media pen — that time, too, the comments had piled up so thick that you had to switch them off to see the picture at all.

Back in the hall, Baek Si-on finally reached the front row.

His seat card sat dead centre, slightly left.

First he gave a small bow to the officials and seniors on both sides.

A Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism official smiled and nodded at him.

“Came straight from the set?”

Baek Si-on answered, low:

“Yes.”

The official chuckled.

“Good work out there.”

“You work harder.”

He sat down.

The gonryongpo was slightly awkward in the limited legroom, and he had to settle the robe around himself.

On stage, the girl group finally finished.

Applause rolled through the hall.

Half of it was for the stage.

The other half — well, it was hard to say whether it was for the Crown Prince of Sado, who had just taken his seat.

The host walked back out onto the stage.

He had clearly been tipped off by the control room. He glanced at his script, then at the front row.

“It seems we’ve just welcomed a very special guest.”

A ripple of laughter went around the hall.

The camera cut to Baek Si-on again.

The host grinned.

“Please welcome — Actor Baek Si-on, straight from the Joseon Dynasty.”

The whole theatre laughed, finally, properly. Even a few of the officials could not hold it in.

The live comments went through another round of chaos.

At the entrance to the backstage area, Baek Eun-ah finally let out a breath.

Late.

But it had not fallen apart.

It had, if anything, produced a legendary scene.

She looked down and texted the SK managing director.

KakaoTalk · SK Telecom managing director

Baek Eun-ah

He’s in. Seated.

SK Telecom managing director

The footage is excellent.

Baek Eun-ah glanced at Baek Si-on, sitting straight-backed in the front row.

Then at the tablet propped by the stage entrance, where the live comments were rolling up the screen in an unbroken wall.

She thought for a moment, and typed back:

KakaoTalk · SK Telecom managing director

Baek Eun-ah

Brand value: three-dimensional, multi-scenario linkage — achieved.

She sent it, and the sentence made her feel a little sick of herself even as it went through.

There was no helping it, though.

This was what adult work was like.

Sometimes you had to put “your share price is probably about to go up” in a way that sounded respectable.

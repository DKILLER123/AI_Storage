Chapter One Hundred Two

Legend Drops, and the Ground Moves

September 24 11:10 am KST Nonhyeon-dong · Big Hit Entertainment · 4F A mixed-use building. An office not much larger than Insight. A rotating chair, three withdrawal letters, and fried chicken that had stopped being food.

They called it Entertainment. It was a fourth floor in a mixed-use walk-up, not much bigger than the newsroom Si-on had put money into. Bang Si-hyuk sat in his swivel chair with paper fanned across the desk: statements, counsel’s letters, termination notices, and three letters of intent to withdraw, each from a different investor, each more polite than the last, each colder.

He took a piece of fried chicken from the box at the corner, chewed twice, swallowed. He chewed while he read. The wording was courtesy. The meaning was a door.

Kim Da-hee was almost a month gone. The aftertaste was still working. GLAM dissolved. Tens of billions in production, dorms, training, video — zero. The brand, in the industry’s mouth, was a thing you did not want on your shoes. He had sent Bangtan endorsement decks to six advertisers. Two had answered. Both no.

Second piece. He chewed hard. The stomach had started a protest. The mouth did not report to the stomach. The mouth reported to pressure.

On the seventh piece the phone went. The buzz lifted a corner of a withdrawal letter. He looked at the screen.

Unknown. Korean code, a block he did not know. His hand hung. At this hour an unknown call was eighty percent a debt, a complaint, or an investor’s assistant booking an exit. He picked up anyway. Not picking up was not a job he still had.

Unknown → Bang Si-hyuk · Big Hit, 4F · fried chicken still in the mouth

“Hello?”

“Is this Representative Bang?”

A man’s voice. Young. Level.

“It is. Who is this?”

“Baek Si-on.”

Half a second of empty. Then the name landed with the weight of a week of headlines. Venice. Why would that man call this room? A collab? No — a man with that cup would come through a manager, not through a president he had never sat across. Dahee? No. Different circle from Lee Byung-hun. Then —

“Representative Bang?”

“Yes, yes, here.”

He swallowed the last of the chicken too fast, choked, hit his own chest.

“Sorry. Mr. Baek. What — can I help you?”

“Nothing much. I wanted to ask if you need investment.”

The hand came off the chest. The whole man froze in the chair. The tube-light above him flickered once. Honey-chicken hung in the air. The three letters lay there being letters. He opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it.

“…What?”

Si-on said it again, from a city that was still yesterday’s afternoon.

“I said I want to invest in you.”

Bang’s circuit did not fail at language. It failed at permission. Big Hit, this month, was the potato nobody in Korean entertainment would pick up without gloves. He himself had started to think, seriously, about folding the stall and slinking back to JYP. And at that hour a man rang and said invest.

Stupid and rich was the only explanation he had. He licked his lips.

“Mr. Baek. You — you mean it?”

“I mean it. Three billion won. Thirty percent of Big Hit. If you agree, my counsel will fly back to Seoul the day after tomorrow and sit with your team on diligence and paper.”

Three billion. Thirty percent. The quote priced the company at ten billion. Ten billion, to the venture desks in this country, was not a number Big Hit was allowed to wear. Put it on a table and men shook their heads and said keep it. Even if you took an appraiser out, poured him well, slid an envelope of hard-work money across, you still would not know if the printout would climb as far as ten.

Ten seconds. Maybe more. His voice, when it came back, had a shake in it.

“Can I ask one thing?”

“Ask.”

“Why me? Or — why Big Hit?”

“I like your talent.”

Talent. It had been a long time since that word had come at him from someone else’s mouth. For a month the words had been risk, cut loss, exit, liquidate. Something acid came up the nose. He dragged the back of a hand across it.

“Mr. Baek.”

“Mm.”

“I agree. I’ll wait for your lawyer.”

“Good. We’ll be in touch.”

The line died. The busy tone occupied the empty office for a few seconds and then it, too, stopped.

He set the phone down. Reached into the box. Another piece. Still honey. Suddenly sweet. He leaned in the chair, chewing, watching the tube that had been flickering for a month.

What was this? A bounce off the bottom? He was not sure. He was sure of one thing. Tomorrow he would change the tube. It had flickered for a month. Before, he had not had the mood. Now, apparently, he did.

No performance ratchet. Seo Eun-ju, receiving the instruction in Los Angeles, had raised one eyebrow at that and had not asked why. A lawyer executes a will. She does not cross-examine a judgment.

Bang had no objection. In this room, at this hour, objection was a sport for men who still had a floor.

Sign. Stamp. Quieter than he had imagined. No congratulations theatre. No fake boom of capital walking in. Paper pushed back and forth. A company about to have three billion more on the book. A rope, almost gone, thrown from the other bank.

When the process was a process, Seo Eun-ju put Si-on’s copy in the case and stood.

“Wires and the equity change, we’ll keep walking.”

He stood too.

“…Thank you.”

She nodded. She did not linger in Seoul. She finished a supplemental and an authority confirmation, and took the paper back to Los Angeles. The jet came down on a Southern California day that was still day. By the time she reached the hotel, Love Yourself was already a finished vocal.

The mini-album, at that point, had a spine.

Mini-album · four rooms, four weathers

Track 3 · Way Down We Go · blues rock

Not luxurious. For a mini-album: enough. Four quadrants. No two sharing a climate.

September 25 8:10 pm PDT Los Angeles · a hotel room with a glass wall A short working meeting. One question. When does Legend go.

Not many people. Si-on, Eun-ah, Scooter, and Jung Jae-joon on a remote line that made his studio look like a smaller, more honest room. The only item: when to let Legend out.

Scooter’s view was a view he did not bother to dress.

“Audio and video, not the same day. Same-day is loud. Same-day is also the stupidest spend. You want the juice and the chart, you stagger. Audio first. Let stream, social, the numbers start running. When the first natural wave is up, you drop the video as a second hit on the same song. Two peaks. One track.”

Si-on did not argue. Professional things, you let professional people hold.

Eun-ah looked at a calendar.

“If audio first, the window is —”

“September twenty-eighth.” Scooter had the date before she finished the sentence. “Tail of a Sunday. Streams are awake. Asia still gets the attention before the new week opens. You’re firing the first shot. It’s a decent hour to fire.”

Jae-joon, on the other side of the glass, nodded.

“That’s a professional call.”

Si-on closed it.

“September twenty-eighth.”

The meeting ended. Los Angeles night came in through the wall. Eun-ah typed one line into a memo.

Eun-ah · memo

Legend: 9.28 audio, worldwide. MV date pending Bong-suk’s cut.

September 28 12:03 am PDT Los Angeles, and then every glass that followed a link A tweet. Two links. A continent that likes a fight as much as it likes a song.

Justin tweeted at three minutes past midnight.

Twitter · Justin Bieber

@justinbieber · 00:03 PDT

My bro just dropped something INSANE. This ain’t K-pop. This is just FIRE. Go listen to ‘Legend’ right now. Trust me.

Spotify. iTunes. That was the whole post. The effect did not wait to be invited. Justin, in this hour, was a controversy body: bad press, a messy personal life, dogs, cameras, a media that talked about him with its mouth sideways. Controversy and traffic had never been opposites. Often the fight was the fuel. And he was still, at this date, one of the strongest young male traffics on earth. Especially those North American girls with money in the account: the mouth could curse, the hand still tapped the link, still previewed, still bought, still sent it on.

Legend began to roll on Twitter. His fans first. Then his antis. Then the Western music blogs that already watched Scooter’s roster. Then the YouTube mashup rooms and the electro-pop ears that had, already, a faint file on this Korean.

Si-on was not zero in that world. Way Back Home had already done a base on SoundCloud, YouTube, the blog circuit. A channel at MrSuicideSheep’s weight had pushed it. Plays and comments had already proved he had a name in English, even if the name was still being learned.

Korea’s first reaction was not blood-hot. Legend was not a standard Hallyu single. It was not soft. It was not sweet. It did not want to be furniture. One listen and you knew it had not been written for a Korean streaming chart. It had been written for a stage that expected a country to stand up. So the real-time lists did not explode. Melon, first hour, not high. Not ugly. Sixteen. Genie a little kinder. Bugs kinder still. The whole thing sat in the band where the country thought: this is hard, and I still have to admit it is expensive.

The headlines bent with them. Not New Single Out. The other kind.

Korean press · evening of the 28th

Baek Si-on’s new single is out, and the ambition is North America

From Venice Best Actor to global singer? Justin Bieber stands for Baek Si-on’s new track — a direct North American push Korean acts almost never get.

The press understood a Justin tweet better than the fans did. It was not a share. It was a back. It was a pipe. It was the signal that Scooter’s house had actually taken him in.

First-day numbers firmed around four in the morning, Seoul time. Explosion, strictly, was the wrong word. Not the kind Korean entertainment liked to shout: debut at one, slaughtered the chart, the whole net down. Legend, at home, was steady. Melon from sixteen in the first hour, climbing to twelve, into the top ten around one a.m. Peak: six. TTS’s Holler sat above it. Genie peak five. Bugs peak three.

On a Korean streaming chart, that was good. For a man who had just taken Venice and was sitting on the hottest week in the country, it was not especially good.

Eun-ah sat on a sofa and watched the Melon curve for half a minute. She was not disappointed. She was, if anything, relieved. She could read a little now. Legend was not Way Back Home. Way Back Home slid into a public mood: commute, café, night run, insomnia, a crush you had not admitted. Legend was harder. Larger. A song that wanted a picture, a hall, a crowd that would stamp. A song like that not opening Korean real-time in the top three was the normal. The not-normal was overseas.

Alone, not a worldwide detonation. In the mouth of a Korean actor-singer on day one of an English rock single: a number that should not have been legal.

iTunes was ruder. U.S. peak eighteen. Canada twelve. Australia nine. Singapore, the Philippines, Thailand, Malaysia — top three without being asked. Korea, of course, one.

Eun-ah looked at that Korean iTunes one, finally picked up the seaweed crisp she had opened and not eaten, and put a sheet in her mouth. Crack. Very crisp. She chewed twice, said, around it:

“Not bad.”

Then she heard how much that sounded like her cousin, and corrected herself.

“Very not bad.”

Seo Eun-ju, on the single sofa, laptop on her knees, was in a North American distribution clause about data return. She looked up.

“You’re starting to talk like your cousin.”

Eun-ah did not have an answer that would not make it worse.

By morning the Korean headlines had changed a set. Yesterday afternoon the words had been Bieber retweet, North American market, Venice Best Actor’s new song. This morning the word was overseas numbers.

Korean press · morning of the 29th

Baek Si-on’s Legend enters Spotify Global New on day one — a rare line for a Korean actor-singer

Not only a Venice cup. Overseas response is strong. After Justin Bieber’s push, Legend lands on multiple iTunes charts; the North American strategy is no longer a rumour. Honest line of the morning: domestic audio did not explode. Overseas ran far past the estimate.

That last headline was the most honest, and the most accurate. It did not force the Korean chart. It put the weight where the song had put it. Everyone could see it. This track had not been written at the domestic market. Korea was the base. North America was the field.

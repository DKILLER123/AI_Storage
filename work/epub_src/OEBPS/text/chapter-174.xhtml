Chapter One Hundred Seventy Four

Crying Counts Toward the Clock

December 25 · Christmas Eve on the West Coast, Christmas morning in Seoul One pen lifted and set down twice · a doorbell at 7:30 p.m. · three minutes of bathroom deliberation and one can of pepper spray · a twenty-minute meeting that runs exactly to its own arithmetic West Coast · one cramped rental apartment → Beverly Hills · the Four Seasons, after midnight A songwriter with two pieces of paper and no third option gets a knock on the door, a bag of clothes with the tags still on, and the strangest Christmas of his life — in which the tears are put on a timer, the dinner is declared unbaited, and a five-hundred-fold difference changes a life before dessert.

West Coast.

In a cramped, cheap rental apartment, Charlie Puth sat in the only chair in the living room that still deserved the word. Two things lay on the table in front of him.

the coffee table · 7:29 p.m., Christmas Eve

Next month’s rent bill.

A notice from a record label’s lawyers.

One pen, lifted twice, set down twice.

He picked up the pen.

Put it down.

Picked it up again.

Put it down again.

Sign this, and he could breathe.

No more lying awake over next month’s eviction, no more credit-card balance rolling into a snowball.

But he knew he shouldn’t sign.

Because that chorus had come out of him.

That line — “It’s been a long day without you, my friend” — was a grief object. He had written it for the friend he’d lost to a motorcycle two years ago, not manufactured it in some conference room as a commercial unit.

But he also knew: rent does not grant extensions on account of talent.

The convenience store does not hand out bread because your artistic dignity is intact.

Ideals are expensive.

So expensive that these days he couldn’t quite afford next month’s rent.

Charlie sighed and set his fingers on the pen.

And at that exact moment —

Ding-dong.

He went absolutely still.

A few seconds later, the doorbell rang again.

Charlie checked the wall clock.

Seven thirty at night.

Christmas Eve.

Who knocks at this hour?

His first guess: the label’s lawyers, back for another round. Those people had excellent instincts for timing.

Emails in daylight.

Doorbell after dark.

As though a creator’s sleep, dignity, and hope were all officially part of their deliverables.

He stood and went to the door.

The lock turned, the door swung — he had “get out” loaded and ready —

But the person outside was faster: a business card came at him first.

“Hello, Mr. Puth. Sorry to disturb you.”

Charlie looked down.

the business card · 7:31 p.m.

SB Projects

One name. One title: the lead on the Baek Si-on project.

Justin Bieber’s agency. Ariana Grande’s agency. One of the most influential management companies in North American pop.

He knew the company.

Charlie swallowed the profanity that had already reached the loading dock.

“What do you want with me?”

“I run the project team for Mr. Baek Si-on,”

the visitor said.

“Mr. Baek Si-on is in the air back to Los Angeles right now. He heard your demo. He’s very interested in the song, and he’d like to meet you.”

Charlie blinked once.

“Who?”

“Mr. Baek Si-on.”

“The Baek Si-on?”

The visitor nodded.

“Yes.”

Charlie’s brain stuttered against the name.

Of course he knew it. The man had taken two trophies at the American Music Awards weeks ago. A Billboard chart-topper. The person who had just used one music video to keep Korea, the United States, and half the internet up at night.

“He… wants to meet me?”

“Yes.”

The visitor held out a paper bag next.

“A Christmas gift from Mr. Baek Si-on. His own Under Armour collaboration line.”

A pause, then:

“You’re among the first people outside the company to wear it.”

Charlie took the bag in a daze.

“The address and time of the meeting are inside. He hopes you can make it.”

“And — merry Christmas.”

“……”

Charlie stood in the doorway holding the bag, brain lagging behind reality by several frames.

Christmas Eve. Rental apartment. Rent bill. Lawyer’s letter. Then a knock, a superstar who wanted to meet him, and a bag of an Under Armour collaboration.

If you wrote this into a drama, the audience would roast the writers for skipping the setup.

He shut the door and set the bag on the table.

The bag’s paper felt expensive.

So expensive that, beside the peeling walls, the dying desk lamp, and the rent bill, it looked like contraband from a different tax bracket.

Charlie took the clothes out first.

the gift bag · Baek Si-on × Under Armour · the collaboration line

Three pieces, tags on

A black training jacket. A grey hoodie. A sports windbreaker that has not officially released yet.

Clean design, one small mark at the left chest — and fabric that costs more than anything currently on his body.

He looked at them for two seconds, then couldn’t help touching the fabric.

Expensive.

Considerably more expensive than the hoodie he had on, washed pale at the collar.

At the very bottom of the bag: a card.

He drew it out.

An address and a time.

The Four Seasons, Beverly Hills.

12:30 a.m.

Charlie flipped the card over.

Blank.

No fine print. No explanation.

And then a deeply undignified thought walked into Charlie’s head.

Was he about to be seduced?

The thought startled him more than the doorbell had.

But could you blame him for thinking it?

He was no innocent just off the bus. Two years-plus of drifting through Los Angeles will teach anyone how messy this city’s entertainment industry really is — messier than most people ever learn.

People stab each other over credits. People pour drinks and laugh at things that aren’t funny to get ahead. And some powerful men’s tastes could keep a Catholic parish running exorcisms around the clock.

Charlie went into the bathroom and looked at himself in the mirror.

Hair: chaos.

The gap in the middle of the brow: unchanged, as ever.

Overall assessment.

Handsome.

Objectively speaking: genuinely handsome.

He leaned into the mirror, checked left, checked right.

No wonder.

If he were the mogul, he’d pick himself too.

Charlie stepped back and began to deliberate.

Go, or don’t go?

Go.

What if it really was that kind of thing?

He was poor, but his body was his own.

Don’t go.

What if it was actually about the music?

An opportunity like that was, in his current position, rarer than a lottery win.

Charlie stood in the bathroom deliberating for three minutes.

Then he made a decision.

Go.

But prepared.

He turned the apartment upside down and found a can of pepper spray in the kitchen drawer.

He’d bought it for stray dogs.

Never used it once — because the first time a stray dog came at him, his instinct turned out to be flight, not combat.

He shook the can. Something rattled inside. Should still work.

He slipped the pepper spray into his inside jacket pocket, went back to the mirror, fixed his hair, and picked up his keys.

At the door he looked back once, at the unsigned paper on the coffee table.

If tonight really was about music — then that signature could wait.

If it wasn’t about music —

He had pepper spray.

The plane landed in Los Angeles close to midnight.

Baek Si-on’s party barely paused — off the plane, into the cars, straight for the Four Seasons in Beverly Hills.

The car was quiet.

Chicago’s wind still lived somewhere in the seams of their coats, and when Los Angeles night-warmth hit it, the result was an almost unreal looseness.

Baek Si-on sat in the back seat and asked:

“Is Charlie Puth there yet?”

Baek Eun-ah called to confirm. A few sentences later she covered the mic and turned around.

“He’s here. Hotel lounge.”

“Send him up a late dinner.”

A beat.

“Get me one too.”

She nodded.

“On it.”

Baek Si-on read the thread, stopped for a few seconds on it, then asked Baek Eun-ah out of nowhere:

“Eun-ah. Working through Christmas — does it bother you?”

She was mid-arrangement on the late-dinner logistics and blinked at the question.

“Nope.”

She looked up, and said it with complete sincerity.

“Cousin, you ARE my Christmas present.”

Out of context it sounded like flattery.

But Baek Eun-ah meant every syllable.

A few months ago she had been a trainee cut loose by SM — no direction, no guaranteed income, her self-respect repeatedly crumpled like waste paper.

And now she ran point on Korea-and-America logistics: concerts, endorsements, tours, music projects. Her income had long since lapped plenty of famous Korean managers.

More importantly — she no longer had to wait for someone else to decide whether she deserved to stay.

If that wasn’t a Christmas present, then Santa Claus could go ahead and retire.

Baek Si-on nodded and turned to Park Ji-hun and the two bodyguards.

“You all?”

Park Ji-hun was slumped in the back row with his eyes at half-mast, but the answer came instantly:

“Not suffering either.”

“Doing your styling is the Christmas present on my résumé.”

Bodyguard Kang said:

“Spending Christmas working for Mr. Baek is an honor.”

Bodyguard Han added:

“Also, the beds at the Four Seasons are excellent.”

Baek Si-on was satisfied.

Everyone around him understood gratitude.

From extremely practical angles, admittedly.

But practical is good.

Practical people do not suddenly become poets. Poets are the ones who message you at three in the morning about wanting to go see the ocean.

He looked back down and typed his replies to Choi Jin-ri.

Time zones had a way of making you lose track of whose Christmas finished first.

Baek Si-on scrolled past Choi Jin-ri’s thread and found Lee Ji-eun’s messages.

❧ ❧ ❧

Four Seasons, Beverly Hills.

Charlie sat in his room with the late dinner in front of him.

Steak.

Caesar salad.

A bottle of sparkling water.

A dessert that looked expensive from across the room.

Beautiful plating. Wonderful smell.

Charlie hadn’t touched a bite.

Not because he wasn’t hungry.

Because he didn’t dare eat.

What if it was drugged?

It wasn’t that he believed Baek Si-on would do such a thing.

It was the full picture: half past midnight, luxury hotel, a stranger-superstar, clothes delivered in advance, and now a dinner sent up by room service.

That combination did not read as “purely a music conversation” from any angle he knew.

Time crawled forward.

He sat on the sofa, staring at the steak, and his stomach betrayed him with one honest growl.

Charlie looked away at once.

“No.”

He told himself, quietly:

“Have some dignity.”

A few more minutes.

Then, finally — movement outside the door.

Every muscle in Charlie’s body locked. His right hand went into his jacket pocket and closed around the pepper spray.

The door opened.

Baek Si-on walked in.

Charlie’s first thought was:

Handsome.

He admitted it, objectively, in the privacy of his own skull.

His second thought: this was now considerably worse.

Baek Si-on was assessing him too.

Close to the impression he’d carried. Young. Thin.

And most importantly — the famous broken eyebrow, present and correct.

Right man.

Charlie Puth

YouTube songwriter · the chorus Universal rejected

Two years drifting in Los Angeles · one broken eyebrow · $50,000, refused

He wrote the line for a friend he lost, not for a conference room. Answers the door at 7:30 p.m. with pepper spray in his pocket and prices his dignity at ‘not for sale’ — right up until someone offers five hundred thousand and splits the song from the person. The gap in his eyebrow is the most reliable identification feature in the music industry.

“Hello.”

Baek Si-on put out his hand.

Charlie grabbed it with both the speed and the terror of a man defusing something.

“Hello.”

Baek Si-on glanced at the jacket he was wearing.

“Does everything fit, more or less?”

Charlie’s nerves spiked again.

“…It fits.”

They sat.

Baek Si-on’s eyes crossed the untouched dinner.

“Not to your taste?”

Charlie said instantly:

“I already ate.”

At which point his stomach growled again, completely without authorization.

“……”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Apparently not much.”

He didn’t expose the lie. He simply opened his own dinner container.

“I have twenty minutes. Let’s keep it short.”

“Okay.”

Baek Si-on said:

“First thing. I want to sign you.”

Charlie froze.

He had imagined a dozen versions of this meeting, and not one of them opened with that sentence.

How was this different from hamburgers falling out of the sky?

The kind with cheese and bacon on them, at that.

And the more it looked like a gift, the more his stomach — the mental one — knotted.

Nowhere in this industry is dinner free.

Especially when the dinner in front of you is one you haven’t dared to touch.

Charlie swallowed and tested the water:

“Why me? Is it just the demo?”

“Not just.”

Baek Si-on shook his head:

“Wiz tells me you wrote that demo in ten minutes?”

Charlie nodded.

“About that.”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

“Talent like yours is only missing three things — a platform, a chance, and connections.”

“I can supply all three.”

“I have experience developing new artists.”

Charlie’s heartbeat picked up.

An emotion he hadn’t felt in a very long time began to rise in his chest.

It was called hope.

But anyone who has lived in Los Angeles long enough knows: hope, in this town, is shaped exactly like a trap.

Charlie pinned the emotion down and asked the question that had to be asked.

“What’s the price?”

“The price is that demo of yours.”

Charlie’s heart dropped.

There it was.

Everyone was after the song.

Baek Si-on went on:

“Relax — songwriting credit stays in your name. All of it.”

Charlie gave a small, bitter laugh.

“Universal said that too.”

Baek Si-on read the hesitation and asked:

“What did Universal offer to buy it outright?”

“Fifty thousand.”

“Dollars?”

“Yes.”

“……”

Furious 7’s production budget was two hundred and fifty million dollars.

And for the song destined to become the film’s soul, the offer was fifty thousand.

Pure predation: no background, no manager, no leverage — so squeeze until the ribs show.

“I’ll pay five hundred thousand dollars.”

“…?”

Charlie wondered whether his ears, oversprung on nerves, had promoted fifty thousand into five hundred thousand on their own.

“Sorry.”

He sat up straighter.

“I didn’t catch that.”

Baek Si-on repeated it.

“Five hundred thousand dollars, for the exclusive recording and distribution license. Your songwriting credit stays on the song, and your writer’s royalties run exactly as they should.”

Charlie stared at him for several full seconds.

He was checking whether the man across the table had clocked out of a Santa Claus side-gig and simply emptied the gift sack over his head.

Because here is how the music business prices an unsigned kid: no commercial track record, no major-label deal, no management behind him — a YouTube creator’s advance usually runs from a few thousand to a few tens of thousands of dollars, and getting that much already counts as the label being generous.

If Universal had opened at five hundred thousand, Charlie would have signed on the spot, weeks ago.

All of it was true — the artistic dignity, the song being for his brother, refusing to let the commercial machine chew up something sacred.

But if the friend he lost could see it — if he knew that “It’s been a long day without you, my friend” could keep Charlie off the eviction list, stop the credit-card statements from breeding, let him keep making music with his spine straight —

the friend would probably be relieved.

He might even send a dream down from heaven, with instructions.

Brother. Sell.

Sell it high.

“You’re sure?”

Charlie’s voice wasn’t steady.

“The bank opens tomorrow morning. The transfer can go out then.”

Charlie went quiet again.

He had heard the stories. Baek Si-on was generous.

The rumor had started at the American Music Awards — the dancers had posted their gold coins on Twitter, and the internet had joked that Baek Si-on tipped his dancers the way medieval kings paid their knights.

Then there was the free concert in Korea, the one where even the seat cushions had been handed out.

Charlie had assumed the man was either genuinely rich, or running a charity persona.

Sitting across from him now, Charlie had his answer.

Genuinely rich.

But hearing about generosity is one thing.

Having it land on you is another creature entirely.

Charlie’s nose stung.

“Can I cry now?”

Baek Si-on checked the time.

Ten minutes of the twenty already gone.

“Crying counts toward the clock.”

“……”

The emotion that had just crested got stopped at the toll gate.

Charlie lowered his head and rubbed his face.

“Fine. I’ll try to be moved quickly, then.”

Baek Si-on pushed his own dinner container aside and wiped his hands on the napkin.

“For the chorus — I’ll re-arrange it, record it, and deliver the song to Universal as my vocal.”

Charlie’s brain caught up with a delay.

“Wait. You mean… you’ll sing the chorus?”

“Mm.”

“You’ll sing it yourself?”

“Otherwise what?”

And with that, the ruin-the-song anxiety Charlie had been carrying all week simply set itself down and left the room.

Then his brain finally circled back to the opening item.

“And the signing-me part…?”

“Separate negotiation.”

That sentence surprised him more than the half million.

He had assumed the money was the hook — song and singer bought together, the way labels do it: hand the kid a shiny number, watch his judgment evaporate, then fold the next five years into a contract dense enough to bend light.

Baek Si-on had split the two things apart.

The song: bought.

The person: discussed separately.

Charlie’s expression slowly turned serious.

“So if I refuse to sell the song — you’d still sign me?”

“Correct. Whether you sell the song has no bearing on whether I sign you. What I want is you, not only the song.”

“I can’t afford a lawyer to look at any contract right now.”

“Then hire one after the five hundred thousand clears.”

“……”

The logic was too complete.

So complete that, for a moment, Charlie could not locate a single seam to argue with.

“Tonight we sign the letter of intent for the song.”

Baek Si-on stood.

Charlie stood with him and took his hand.

“Baek.”

“Mm?”

“I came here with pepper spray in my pocket.”

Charlie explained, with some embarrassment:

“It’s just — half past midnight, Beverly Hills hotel, a superstar I’ve never met, free clothes, free dinner… you understand.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“That’s completely reasonable.”

Charlie exhaled in relief.

The next second, Baek Si-on added:

“But if you actually spray me, the signing bonus gets docked for eye-washing fees.”

“……”

Charlie finally laughed out loud.

“Is it too late to throw it away right now?”

“You’re in time.”

Baek Si-on glanced at the steak on the table, untouched since arrival.

“Also.”

“The dinner isn’t drugged.”

Charlie’s smile froze.

“You could tell?”

“Your stomach growled three times.”

“……”

Charlie looked down at the steak.

A few seconds later he sat back down on the sofa and picked up his knife and fork.

“Then I’ll have a couple bites first.”

“Eat quickly.”

“Why?”

“Eating also counts toward the clock.”

“……”

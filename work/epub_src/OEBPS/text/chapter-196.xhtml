Chapter One Hundred Ninety Six

Today Is Not a Day for Cindy

March · Los Angeles, then Seoul · release week for a song about goodbyes A highway jammed with monsters · two radio stations and one sentence about passports · a throne that was never hereditary · a soup restaurant where one bowl instead of two is a diagnosis · the first morning of ‘Producers’ · two support vans, one hot chocolate, and a card inside a script US radio → Korea-town, Western Ave → Yeouido, the KBS annex → a makeup room The noisiest month in American pop gets one more car on the road; a Busan auntie reads a customer’s heart in a single bowl; and the coldest idol on a drama call sheet keeps losing, by inches, to a very good morning.

March in the American pop market couldn’t be called busy. It had to be called a traffic jam.

the March grid · the highway, and what was on it

BRUNO MARS — still towing the entire country into a retro party with ‘Uptown Funk’.

TAYLOR SWIFT — ‘1989’, still firing singles like a scheduled artillery program.

MAROON 5 — ‘Sugar’ and its wedding-ambush video, still harvesting streams; American brides briefly nursing the absurd belief that an Adam could crawl out from behind any wedding tent.

JUSTIN BIEBER — back. With Skrillex and Diplo, riding ‘Where Are Ü Now’ straight into the mainstream’s field of view; a global top-tier name, a walking puberty accident scene, the Canadian menace who answers every “he’s finished” by crushing the comment section with data.

KENDRICK LAMAR — ‘To Pimp a Butterfly’, surprise-dropped; American critics collectively speechless, having discovered that writing only “it sounds nice” would make them look illiterate.

MAJOR LAZER — ‘Lean On’, spreading. No Taylor narrative, no Bruno radio dominance, no Bieber-return spectacle. Just one terrifying ability: once through the chorus, it’s fine. Twice, you’re nodding. Three times, you’re swaying your shoulders in front of a convenience-store fridge with no idea why. Early symptoms of a streaming monster.

Onto this packed highway, one more car merged: Baek Si-on’s. Not to defend ‘Love Yourself’ — that song had finished its cruelest assignment. March belonged to a different push: ‘See You Again’.

Universal had set the release date for March 10. ‘Furious 7’ would hit North American theaters in early April. The promotion math was precise: send the song out first, let the emotion ferment in advance, then let the movie catch it. And so Baek Si-on and Wiz started running radio. Early March at American radio stations had a very uniform smell: coffee, perfume, plastic soundproofing, and artists forced at nine in the morning to radiate the false vitality of a man who slept eight hours.

First stop, Z100. The host gripped his cue cards.

“A lot of people say they haven’t even seen the movie — they hear this song and it already feels like saying goodbye to a friend.”

Wiz nodded.

“Because that’s how it was made.”

He talked about Paul Walker, about the franchise, about the seconds when the whole room went quiet the first time he heard that chorus. The host turned to Baek Si-on.

“You’re not in the ‘Fast and Furious’ films, and you’re not American. How did you get inside this song’s emotion?”

Baek Si-on leaned toward the microphone.

“Farewell doesn’t need a passport.”

❝

Farewell doesn’t need a passport.

— Z100, live on air; a staff member looked up through the glass; the host needed a second

A staffer looked up through the control-room glass. Wiz turned his head too. The host paused one full second.

“That’s a good line.”

Wiz jumped in at once:

“Man, you should’ve said that weeks ago — we could print it on the movie poster.”

“There’s still time to print it.”

The host laughed, and asked whether the recording process had gone smoothly. Wiz leaned back and filed his complaint directly:

“Smoothly? This man shows up at 3 a.m., listens to the demo, and drags everybody into the studio like he’s robbing a bank.”

“That’s called efficiency,” Baek Si-on corrected.

Wiz turned to the host.

“No. That’s kidnapping, with better vocals.”

The director nearly switched to the wrong track laughing.

Second stop, KIIS FM. The host’s opening question came straight out:

“Do you two think this song is going to blow up?”

Wiz said he didn’t know. Baek Si-on said he knew. The host froze. So did Wiz.

“You know?”

“Mm.”

“Why?”

“Because humanity has no immunity to farewell.”

The host was silent for a few seconds, then nodded, seriously. Wiz studied Baek Si-on from the side.

“Are you secretly stockpiling lines like that?”

Baek Si-on shrugged.

“No.”

Wiz stared for two seconds and shook his head. The host glanced down at the listener data. The real-time comments were climbing. And with that, the first radio run of ‘See You Again’ was formally underway.

‘See You Again’ · the radio run, day one · as logged by the promo team

Line-stockpiling allegation: raised on air, denied on air. Nobody in the room believed the denial.

A week later. Korea-town, Los Angeles. Baek Si-on sat in the corner of a beef-soup-rice restaurant he had found himself — small storefront, the Korean sign faded a little by California sun, the owner a Busan auntie in her fifties whose apron never fully lost its chili-paste stains. He was a regular: every visit, two beef soup rices and a plate of fried dumplings. So today, when he sat down, she simply called it across the counter:

“The usual?”

“One’s fine today.”

She looked at him and said nothing. While he waited, he took out his phone and pulled up Billboard. The weekly chart had just refreshed. Number one had changed hands: Bruno Mars’ ‘Uptown Funk’ had finally pushed ‘Love Yourself’ out of the top seat. Baek Si-on felt very little about it. A chart throne was never a hereditary one — sitting in it once was enough, and sitting in it too long was bad for the back.

Billboard Hot 100 · the week ‘Love Yourself’ stepped down · selected rows, read slowly over soup

No. 1 — ‘Uptown Funk’ · Bruno Mars. The dethroning, completed.

No. 3 — ‘Sugar’ · Maroon 5. The wedding-ambush video still doing damage.

No. 4 — ‘Where Are Ü Now’ · the comeback, confirmed by arithmetic.

No. 5 — ‘Style’ · Taylor Swift. New lead single, still top five. Her understanding of charts is roughly her understanding of exes: every one can be turned into material, and every one can be turned into revenue.

No. 47 — ‘Marvin Gaye’ · Charlie Puth & Meghan Trainor. Top fifty, reached. Charlie’s own contribution to the position: about thirty percent. The other seventy: the sheer mass of a woman standing there with a Billboard No. 1 of her own — the chart system clears a seat for her on any collaboration. But Charlie’s voice had started being heard. That was enough.

Read at a corner table, with one bowl of soup instead of the usual two.

The soup arrived. Clear broth, thin-sliced beef, scallion floating; the rice went in, and carbohydrate and hot broth landed together, briefly convincing a person the world was not so bad. Baek Si-on ate slowly. The auntie watched him several times from behind the counter and finally couldn’t help it:

“Not feeling well today?”

“No.”

She clearly didn’t believe him. This young man normally ate two soups and a plate of dumplings; today, one bowl, and at that pace. In the Busan-auntie diagnostic system, eating little had exactly two causes: sickness, or something sitting on the heart. Something was, in fact, sitting on Baek Si-on’s. That afternoon he was due on set for the ‘Sign of the Times’ video — and to film frames with no one in them, he first had to fill his head with people. The uniformed sixteen-year-olds who should be dozing in classrooms, or sneaking looks at phones. He put down his spoon, left cash on the table, and stood.

“Thanks, auntie. I’ll eat more next time.”

March in Los Angeles was very bright — too bright to be the natural habitat of a mourning video. But some songs insist on singing the darkest things in the brightest places.

❧ ❧ ❧

Korea. Yeouido. The KBS annex. Day one of ‘Producers’, and the set had been running on a very particular organized chaos since eight in the morning. Everyone busy; nobody frantic — the first days of a shoot belonged to mutual reconnaissance. Actors probing the director’s temper, the director probing the actors’ rhythm, crew establishing who was approachable and who was not to be trifled with, agents locating the corner with the best signal for scrolling their phones.

And at this moment, Lee Ji-eun sat in the makeup room between two stylists, undergoing final adjustments. The woman in the mirror was no longer quite the usual IU. The stylists had chosen a sharply cut coat — clean shoulder line, a beautiful cinched waist — shoving her a full step off the “nation’s little sister” route and toward “ice-cold unnie.” The effect, to be fair, was real. At least in the mirror, she no longer resembled the sweet girl with the guitar from ‘Friday’.

The only problem was that the ice-cold-unnie concept was, on Lee Ji-eun, somewhat difficult to implement. Not a face problem. Certain objective conditions were simply, objectively objective. For instance: height. For instance: aura. For instance: the suspiciously serene geography of her chest. The head stylist studied the mirror for a few seconds, pressed the coat collar down, tugged it back up, and elected to surrender.

“There. This is good,” the stylist said.

Lee Ji-eun looked at her in the mirror.

“Eonni, your silence just now did not sound like ‘this is good.’”

The stylist’s hands stopped. Beside them, the assistant bent lower over her powder puff, shoulders shaking once. The stylist said, with immense professionalism:

“Cindy doesn’t press people with that.”

“With what?”

“…Aura.”

Lee Ji-eun regarded the mirror, expression flat.

“You hesitated.”

“I didn’t.”

“You did.”

“Really, I didn’t.”

Lee Ji-eun turned her head, slowly. The stylist grabbed the setting spray like a weapon.

“Eyes closed.”

Lee Ji-eun closed her eyes. The mist came down. Cold. When she opened them again, the Cindy in the mirror was, perhaps, one degree more complete.

“Han-teuk oppa.”

From the wall, Jung Han-teuk answered instantly:

“Mm?”

“Do I look like Cindy now?”

Han-teuk understood at once that this was not a question. This was a minefield. Answer yes, and he had admitted she was normally as difficult as the character. Answer no, and he had pronounced her acting insufficient. He deliberated for approximately zero point eight seconds and chose survival.

“The styling looks like her.”

Lee Ji-eun’s eyes narrowed.

“The styling?”

He nodded.

“Very much so.”

“And the person?”

“The person… is approaching.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

The stylist finally lost it — one small laugh escaped. Lee Ji-eun turned to her; the stylist coughed violently.

“I’ll go get hairpins.”

She left. The assistant left with her. The makeup room retained exactly Lee Ji-eun and Jung Han-teuk.

She looked back into the mirror. She knew perfectly well she hadn’t fully entered the state. The problem was not that she couldn’t do a cold face. She was excellent at cold faces. Years in the industry — the malicious comments, the rude reporters, the stage cues changed at the last minute, the 3 a.m. re-recordings — had long since taught her to fold her feelings away and carry them. The difficulty was that today her mood was genuinely good. Catastrophically unsuited to Cindy. And the reason was parked downstairs at the KBS entrance, in two support vans.

the two vans · parked at the KBS entrance · banners only, no sender named

VAN 01 — “To the entire ‘Producers’ crew: thank you for your hard work.”

VAN 02 — “Cindy — please work well.”

SCRIPT, PAGE TWO — a small card, slipped between pages: “Custodian duties: thank you for your hard work.”

The crew spent the morning guessing who sent them. She needed one glance. One hot chocolate, two vans, one card — and a certain someone far away in America, staying deliberately quiet.

Cindy wants cold — and her grin wants up. Cindy wants proud — and her eyes want crescents. Cindy looks at everyone like they owe her money — and she thinks the weather today is lovely.

— the makeup-room civil war, minute twelve

Outside the makeup room, an assistant director’s voice suddenly sounded:

“Cindy — get ready, blocking rehearsal in a minute!”

She sat up straight at once.

“Ne. Right away.”

She nudged the hot chocolate aside and picked up the script. The page that fell open had the little card tucked into it. She stared at the line for one second and shut the script, fast. No. Today she was Cindy. Cindy did not sit in a makeup room like a high-schooler with a love letter over one hot chocolate, two vans, and some man far away in America. She stood, faced the mirror, and pressed her mouth flat. Colder. Colder still. Good — barely passable as a top idol who hated the entire world.

She turned for the door — and stopped. Took her phone from Han-teuk’s hand and glanced at it. The screen was quiet. No new message from Baek Si-on. She exhaled, relieved. This man had better not message her today — she genuinely would fail to play Cindy. …And yet if he sent nothing at all, that wouldn’t be very polite of him either.

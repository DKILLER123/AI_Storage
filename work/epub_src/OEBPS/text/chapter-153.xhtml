Chapter One Hundred Fifty Three

Packing the Blue House for the Screen

November 30 · the tail of November One month in release · one million admissions Seoul · a hanwoo barbecue restaurant with very good privacy For a commercial blockbuster, a million is a passing grade. For a tiny, airless, uncommercial indie film with no effects and no scale, it is a miracle — and the kind of case study that will live in distribution decks forever, cited constantly and copied never.

One month after release, Green Bottle Fly crossed one million admissions.

It reset the all-time box office record for Korean independent cinema in a single stroke — which meant that from today onward, the film would be written into the closing slide of every Korean indie pitch meeting for the next decade: the sample case that gets name-checked forever and never reproduced.

Distributor Showbox hosted the party. Not in some overwrought hotel ballroom — in a private Korean beef restaurant, meat smoke and all.

Wrapping at Suwon a little after four, Lee Joon-ik performed a rare act of mercy and released Baek Si-on early to catch the dinner.

When he pushed open the private hall’s door, the room was nearly full. The smell hit first, a familiar mixture: champagne, Korean cold appetizers, grilled beef, and a few bottles of red opened well ahead of schedule. The lighting ran warm, dropping from the ceiling in a way that made the whole hall feel less like a corporate event and more like a family wedding.

Showbox’s distribution director stood near the head table, talking in low tones with Baek Jeong-hun. The gaffer, the cinematographer, the script supervisor — everyone was there. The people who normally crouched beside equipment cases in old coats had all put on respectable clothes tonight and clustered in threes and fours around their drinks, laughter hopping from table to table.

The moment he walked in, glasses went up across the room.

“Best Actor Baek!”

“Congratulations!”

“A million!”

“Over here — this table!”

Baek Si-on handed his down jacket to a waiter and worked his way in, nodding, shaking hands table by table, absorbing waves of you worked hard, congratulations, and you really are something. By the end of the circuit he hadn’t found a single second to drink so much as water.

Which was when Choi Jin-ri finally came over, carrying a glass of orange juice.

the wrap-party look · guest of honor, off duty one month in · one million in

The knit A light oatmeal-cream sweater, soft as a first snow, with the sleeves pushed carelessly to the forearm — the outfit of someone with nothing to prove to this room, because this room already stood up for her. No stylist, no statement earring, no red-carpet armor. On a night built for champagne, she is holding orange juice, and somehow that is the most confident item in the building.
The jeans High-waisted blue denim, plain as a promise. Her long hair hangs loose, pink fading into candlelight, and when she laughs — which is constantly tonight — her eyes go to crescents first and the rest of the room follows a beat later.

Sweater, jeans, orange juice — and the whole room orbiting her anyway.

Venice had water breeze and red carpets and a standing ovation in Sala Grande. This room has grill smoke and spilled beer and a hundred people who bled for one shot in an alley. She knows which one she’d keep.

“Do you know what they’re all calling me now?”

She handed him the glass. Baek Si-on took it and had a sip.

“Calling you what?”

“The Ten-Million Heroine~”

The tilde was audible. The tail of the syllable stretched out with a small, insolent pride, holding the door open for a compliment.

Baek Si-on looked at her for two seconds.

“Ten million sounds better than a million.”

Her smugness seized up like an engine without oil.

The sentence was, technically, flawless. That was the problem — it was also the least romantic thing said in the province of Gyeonggi all evening. And by the cold accounting of Korean film history, the actresses who had actually carried a ten-million-admission box office on their own shoulders numbered exactly three. Three. All of them over forty. She was twenty years old. Against that ledger, what exactly was she supposed to bid with?

And this man — this man had taken a challenge rated “stroll along the Han River” and re-priced it as “free-solo Everest.”

Choi Jin-ri inhaled slowly and elected to change topics, on the grounds that any further exposure might make her taste soju in the orange juice.

“Anyway. Thank you.”

“For what?”

“For making this film exist.”

She looked out at the crowded, laughing hall.

“Without this movie, I’d probably be off shooting idol dramas the company picked for me — the kind even I can’t sit through.”

“My mother put up the money for it.”

Choi Jin-ri blinked. Then she burst out laughing.

“Then thank your mother for me.”

“I’ll pass it along.”

At that point her eyes drifted sideways, and she asked, in a voice engineered to sound offhand:

“By the way — Red Velvet is confirmed, right?”

Baek Si-on glanced at her.

“Confirmed.”

“So my kindness as a senior was still useful?”

“Mm.”

“Just mm?” she protested under her breath, openly dissatisfied.

Before he could answer, a microphone squealed at the front of the hall.

“Everyone — everyone.”

The Showbox distribution director stepped up onto a temporary platform with the mic in hand.

“Nearly all of us are here now. Let’s properly begin.”

The speeches were standard issue. Thank the director. Thank the actors. Thank every crew member. Thank the audience. Thank the Korean film market, generous enough to leave oxygen in the room for an independent film. On the subject of release, he added one unprompted line:

“When we were working through distribution problems with actor Baek Si-on backstage, nobody in that room imagined we’d be standing here a month later celebrating a million admissions.”

The hall answered with laughter and applause at the same time.

Then Baek Jeong-hun took the mic. He didn’t bother with anything ornamental.

“When we shot this film, we all knew it wasn’t going to be a comfortable one.”

“It’s rough. It’s ugly to look at, sometimes. It hurts. Some of it is frankly hard to like.”

“But thank you — all of you — for letting a million people see it.”

The applause that followed was heavier than the polite kind. People in this business understand what an independent film actually fears. Not bad reviews.

Nobody watching.

Unwatched, it might as well never have existed. Green Bottle Fly had now been seen by a million people, which was the best of every possible ending.

The actors took their turns. Baek Si-on got pushed up first, took the mic, and surveyed the room.

“Thank you to the audience.”

“Thank you to the director.”

“Thank you to the entire crew.”

He stopped there. Someone downstairs laughed out loud.

“That’s it?”

Baek Si-on considered the question.

“Also — thank you, Showbox.”

The distribution director began clapping instantly and hard.

“That one matters. That one’s important.”

The hall collapsed into laughter.

Down among the tables, Choi Jin-ri laughed along with everyone. She knew he had never liked long speeches at parties. His real lines lived somewhere else — inside films, on stages, or dropped without warning in the five-second gaps nobody else had prepared for.

Then it was her turn.

“Ten-Million Heroine! A few words!”

She took the mic with a touch of pink in her face.

“Thank you to the director, for the chance.”

“Thank you to the seniors who looked after me.”

A pause. She looked at Baek Jeong-hun, and then at Baek Si-on.

“And thank you to this film — for making me feel, for the first time, that I could be something other than the Sulli everyone remembers.”

Plenty of people in that hall understood instantly. An idol by origin, wearing a stack of labels: f(x). Sulli. Things that had given her light once, and then built the frame around it. Green Bottle Fly had not turned her into a great actress overnight. It had handed her something better — a beginning.

The applause came up warm and stayed.

In the back half of the night, the party loosened its collar completely. Somebody started grilling. Somebody else refilled everything. People fought over photos with the standee. Showbox brought out champagne; a staffer shook a bottle, popped it, and foam detonated across the front rows.

the champagne line · t-minus zero, one million

“A MILLION!”

“A MILLION!”

“NOW GO GET ANOTHER MILLION!”

Baek Jeong-hun took a direct hit of foam and swatted at the air, swearing with a grin that ruined the effect entirely.

“To the alley scene! The one we almost didn’t survive!”

The cinematographer worked the room with his glass. The makeup artist, flushed pink, held the script supervisor hostage with the story of the alley night shoot — how the whole thing happens or doesn’t happen depending on whether a roomful of exhausted people decides, once, not to break.

Choi Jin-ri got extracted into a dozen group photos. Baek Si-on stood at the edge with a glass of water, watching the small, happy chaos of it.

This was not Venice. No water-city night wind. No red carpet. No standing ovation at Sala Grande. No international press lenses.

And this party was exactly as real. The smoke off the grill. The foam going flat in the glasses. The flush on the crew’s faces. The way her eyes bent into crescents every time somebody yelled Ten-Million Heroine at her.

Stack it all together and it beat every wire-service headline about record-breaking ever filed.

She finally escaped the photo pile and came back to his side, watching the crowd.

“This is nice.”

“Mm.”

“That mm I accept.”

Baek Si-on looked at her. Choi Jin-ri pressed her lips together, smiling.

“Because I also think — mm is exactly enough.”

She raised her glass of orange juice.

“Ten-Million Leading Man.”

Baek Si-on looked at her. Then he raised his water glass and touched it, lightly.

“Ten-Million Leading Lady.”

The rims clicked, very quietly, under the noise of the room.

“Photos! Main cast, front and centre!”

Baek Jeong-hun was waving from the middle of it all.

“Si-on! Jin-ri! Come on!”

She answered instantly.

“Coming!”

Two steps in, she looked back at him.

“Hurry up, Ten-Million Leading Man.”

Baek Si-on: “……”

Choi Jin-ri was, at last, satisfied. Excellent. One game back.

…

After the photos, the crowd broke into drifts. She had barely picked her orange juice back up when the distribution director approached her.

“Jin-ri-yah. If you have a moment, I’d love to talk about your next project.”

She started.

“My next project?”

“Mm.”

He looked at her evenly.

“We’d like to know what direction you’re thinking of taking.”

Her instinct was to glance at Baek Si-on — and she did. He was already looking at her. He did not answer for her. He simply gave the director one look, then put his eyes back on her.

“Go hear them out.”

She nodded.

“Okay.”

She followed the director toward the small side lounge at the far end of the hall, and looked back once, halfway there. Baek Si-on had already gone to sit beside his uncle. Only then did she take her eyes off him.

…

Baek Jeong-hun had drunk a fair amount tonight. Not drunk — lubricated. He held his glass and squinted at the crowd still making noise across the hall.

From shooting to release, Green Bottle Fly had been nothing but trouble the whole way. Nobody, including him, would have predicted it would walk into the Venice competition and then clear a million admissions at home.

Baek Si-on sat down beside him.

“Water? At your own party?”

“Shooting tomorrow.”

“Weak.”

And with that editorial note, he clinked his wine glass against the water glass anyway. A very small click.

Baek Si-on asked, without preamble:

“Uncle. What’s the plan for your next film?”

Baek Jeong-hun took a slow drink.

“Haven’t got one.”

Since finishing Green Bottle Fly, ordinary genre pictures had lost their flavor for him. Korean cinema was not short on gangster films. Not short on crime thrillers. Certainly not short on brotherhood betrayals and prosecutors with profiles chiseled for their own press releases.

“I do have an idea.”

Baek Jeong-hun’s eyebrow moved.

“Say it.”

Baek Si-on said:

“A wanted man finds out he’s dying.”

“Then he finds the police most-wanted list has him at number three. He’s spent his whole life being terrible — and he isn’t even the most famous villain in the country. So he decides to kill the two men above him.”

Baek Jeong-hun had been leaning back in his chair. By the second sentence his spine had straightened all on its own. The concept had its hooks in — not because it was lurid, but because the absurdity carried a distinctly Korean bitterness. A man lives his whole life and even his evil has to be ranked. Even the scum fight for position.

“Keep going.”

Baek Si-on said:

“Number one is violence. Number two is faith. Number three is him.”

Baek Jeong-hun frowned.

“That’s the whole core of the film?”

“Mm.”

“Violence is worn out. Eight out of ten Korean movies have a gang in them.”

“Then we film a different kind of gang.”

“Such as?”

“The kind that went legit — and takes photos with National Assembly members.”

The frown came apart. That was interesting. Gangs that swing knives in the street are yesterday’s news. The genuinely frightening kind is the kind with washed hands — the kind that gets seated at dinner parties, cuts ribbons, fronts the local election support association, and ends up photographed two arm-lengths from a politician. That isn’t violence disappearing.

That is violence changing into a suit.

“And number two? Is faith a cult?”

“Not only that. I’m thinking of a ‘teacher’ who can walk into the Blue House, take tea with chaebol wives, and rearrange people’s fates for a fee.”

Baek Jeong-hun froze for a beat.

Then he laughed. First one low note. Then the laugh widened, kept widening — until he set the glass down and pointed a finger across the table.

“Yah.”

“You little punk.”

Baek Si-on looked at him, perfectly calm.

Baek Jeong-hun dropped his voice, which did nothing for the excitement all over his face.

“You’re planning to put that particular person’s circle on camera, aren’t you.”

Baek Si-on declined to confirm anything.

“Film characters.”

“Spare me.”

Baek Jeong-hun swatted the air, still laughing.

“You think I’m one of those critics’-association windbags? Metaphor. Social allegory. The religious costume of power structures. You put this character on a screen and every person in the country will read him in one second.”

“Which is exactly why it can’t be shot like a street protest.”

“Obviously not.”

Baek Jeong-hun came back without missing a beat.

“Protest-chanting is the low road. That’s for people holding placards in a plaza.”

And then he stopped mid-sentence.

Because he remembered Busan. The festival. How close he had come, that autumn, to putting his own name on the protest list.

And now Baek Si-on had laid this on the table in front of him. It was not retreat. It was the long way around the mountain — with the knife getting a fresh edge on the way.

Baek Jeong-hun picked his glass back up. He didn’t drink. He just looked into it, at the wine sitting full and dark.

“Si-on-ah.”

“Mm.”

“Do you remember stopping me before Busan?”

“I remember.”

“I wanted to curse you out so badly that week.”

“You did curse me out.”

Baek Jeong-hun glared.

“That was education.”

Baek Si-on said nothing at all. It was, historically, the correct answer.

Baek Jeong-hun broke first, laughing.

“Fine. I’ll admit it — you were right that time.”

Reluctant words. But the moment they were out, something in his shoulders let go.

“Signing a protest is satisfying. Standing out in front of the cameras is satisfying.”

“But after the satisfaction — if the film is gone, and the next film is gone too — then all that’s left is the satisfaction.”

He looked up.

“What you’re handing me now is a different kind of satisfying.”

“Darker.”

“Crueller.”

“And much more like an actual film.”

Baek Si-on said:

“A film is not a statement.”

“Mm.”

Baek Jeong-hun nodded slowly.

“A film has to let her watch it to the end without being able to turn on it immediately.”

The idea was accelerating now, visibly, behind his eyes.

“Ideally it goes further. Ideally the Ministry of Culture is the one out front afterward, calling it an outstanding work of national reflection on violence and superstition.”

Baek Si-on gave him a long look.

Baek Jeong-hun cracked up at his own picture.

“Ssi-bal.”

“That would be perfect.”

He tipped his head back and emptied the glass in one motion.

Down by the long table, a crowd was still raising toasts to the box-office ceiling Green Bottle Fly had demolished, faces shining, already projecting how many tens of billions uncle-director’s next commercial picture would print.

They had no idea.

In the corner of this same banquet hall — a corner filled with champagne foam, applause and commercial flattery — the uncle and nephew who had just cashed in fame and fortune sat side by side with one glass of water and one empty glass, drawing up the execution order for a movie that would bundle the Blue House, the chaebols, the gangs, and the shamans, and deliver the whole package to the big screen for sentencing.

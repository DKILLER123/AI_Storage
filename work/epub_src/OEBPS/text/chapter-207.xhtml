Chapter Two Hundred Seven

Not Romantic in the Morning, Not Innocent at Night

April 2

Morning

A wire, and then a bank — two perfectly unromantic errands

Baek Si-on’s morning began with two deeply unromantic acts.

The first: wiring twenty million dollars into Bluehole’s account.

When Seo Eun-ju slid the final contract across, she reminded him, in passing, whether he wanted to reread the preferred-share clauses and the trigger conditions on the board-observer seat.

Baek Si-on looked at the page count, decided there was no need to waste both of their lives, and signed on the last page.

The instant the money left the Cheongsong Investment account, Baek Eun-ah sent a message from Los Angeles.

KakaoTalk · Baek Eun-ah

Baek Eun-ah

Cousin, your rate of spending money has now officially acquired the capacity to damage family bonds.

Baek Si-on

.

The single period meant: read, and not open to discussion.

The second act: a visit to KB Bank to talk about a loan.

The ledger, as it stood that morning

Inflows — first North American tour settlements, streaming and publishing splits, the first overseas commercial receivables — pooled into S.W Studio’s US account: forty million dollars of movable cash.

Minus the twenty million just wired to Bluehole: twenty million left. At the current exchange rate, a little over 22 billion won.

The asset — the Seongsu-dong red-brick factories plus the lot: 42 billion won, all-in.

Full cash was, technically, available: shuffle the accounts, press pause on later projects, scrape it together. But that would be stupid.

❝

Buying a building with full cash is parvenu etiquette. Learning to use the bank’s money to feed your own assets — that is when you finally understand capitalism’s most basic courtesy.

— the morning’s first principle

So the answer was: full loan.

42 billion won, drawn in one piece.

The loan, as processed

Applicant: the global spokesperson for KB Financial Group, annual tax figure on public record.

Collateral: S.W Studio’s cash flow plus a stack of brand contracts. Risk to the bank: close to zero.

Service: a division head appeared in person; the rate landed in the internal top tier. The whole process moved like a formality being walked through.

Signed. The folder went straight to Seo Eun-ju, with instructions to follow up on the title transfer and the construction planning.

Both errands done just past noon.

Back in the car, Baek Si-on finally pried his brain off contracts, loans and land, and rotated it to the other matter that needed handling.

— Goo Hara’s solo.

Yesterday at Jung Jae-joon’s studio, the three of them had settled the direction of ‘2002’: cut the British-folk foundation, move to Japanese City Pop, swap the Western millennial memories for elements the Japanese market could actually feel.

But ‘2002’ was one song.

If Goo Hara wanted to truly stand in Japan, one song that made a splash and ended there wouldn’t carry her. She needed at least one more work that resonated with Japanese listeners on a more direct frequency.

A more Japanese song.

Baek Si-on closed his eyes and let the library in his head turn its pages slowly.

The song the pages turned up

Summer. A river embankment. Fireworks. Two people standing side by side, watching the same sky, neither willing to speak first. A little youth, a little distance, something unnameable hanging in the air — like the one second of silence before a firework bursts.

The melody comes back first: very light, like a wind chime touched once. Then the chorus — clean piano, clean strings, a melody line so clean it makes you want to inhale.

‘Uchiage Hanabi.’

♪

Uchiage Hanabi

DAOKO × Kenshi Yonezu · cued from memory · summer festival duet · restrained, long afterglow

Baek Si-on opened his eyes.

The song was almost too perfect.

A summer fireworks festival, a male-female duet, emotion held in check but lingering long. Dropped into the Japanese market, it carried season and imagery in its bones.

If Goo Hara’s voice owned the transparency and softness, and a cooler-toned male voice took the other part, the finished record would be very good indeed.

He could already see the music video: yukata, the embankment, fireworks going off in the distance, half a step of distance between the two of them.

Very Japanese.

Extremely Japanese.

And then he stuck.

Because he didn’t speak Japanese.

This was not a question of whether he could memorize lyrics.

If the job was mouthing a few Japanese syllables to get a track finished, that was called turning in homework. It was not called singing.

The most valuable thing about this particular song was precisely the texture of the Japanese language itself — that drawn-out, bending, blurred-yet-clean way of pronouncing.

❝

Sung wrong, the whole record drops straight out of a summer night’s fireworks and lands under the fluorescent light of a convenience store.

— the audition nobody will ever be allowed to hold

Baek Si-on started running the numbers in his head.

Learning Japanese: time. Learning enough to sing it without embarrassing himself: more time.

If he truly wanted to ride this song into the Japanese market, there was more after that — promotion, variety shows, keigo, that entire social etiquette of smiling, bowing, leaving a third of every sentence unsaid, folding yourself smaller before you hand yourself over.

Doable?

Doable.

Worth it?

The answer arrived quickly.

Not worth it.

If he were still the version of himself fresh back from Venice, clawing for territory, then Japan was a market worth biting into personally.

The problem was, that was no longer the situation.

He now had North America, Korea, tours, films, capital lines, and a queue of money waiting to be invested. The same hours spent writing English songs, building an album, pushing into Europe, shooting films — even glancing at Bluehole’s development progress — returned more than learning Japanese from zero.

And there was one more account that no number could compute.

The emotional cost.

Compared with the West, Baek Si-on carried no cultural rose-tinting for the Japanese market. Deeper still: certain chapters of that nation’s history sat in him as complicated feelings, not easily wiped clean.

Study without love driving it, at a high cost in time, does not climb anyone’s schedule.

He didn’t even need to ask Scooter. He could predict the man’s exact face.

❝

“That’s a nation that’s very good at kneeling. You don’t need etiquette training — just be tall enough, and they’ll lower their heads for you.”

— Scooter Braun, as predicted, not yet consulted

And so the “Japan-invasion plan,” barely minutes old, was crossed out by its own author’s hand.

But the song could still be made.

‘Uchiage Hanabi’ still suited Goo Hara — the singer just couldn’t be him.

The better play: build the record, hand it to Hara, and pair her with a Japanese male star. The song gains native authenticity, Hara gains her entry point, and he still collects the production, the publishing, and the project upside.

That was what the current Baek Si-on should do.

At that thought, his mood improved slightly.

❝

Strategic vision, it turns out, is mostly the art of refusing decisions that sound cool and don’t pay enough.

— proven once again, in a parked car

OOPS_demo_CP_v2

Charlie Puth · demo v2 · lyrics reined in · art: mildly wounded · fully cured by Billboard No. 21

OOPS v2, as reviewed in a parked car

The melody: still that melody. The hook: still that hook that glues itself to the inside of your skull. This time, though, Charlie had genuinely taken “clean it up” to heart.

The lyrics had pulled in their elbows considerably — at least nobody reaching the chorus would start suspecting that bedsheets had been involved in the creative process. Growth had occurred.

He sent the reply. Boss-flavored to the last syllable.

Privately, though, he was satisfied.

The version wasn’t perfect, but it was enough — and it proved one thing: apart from living his life as raw material for demos, the kid’s brain did come online when actual work was on the table.

The remaining problem was the other half of ‘OOPS’.

Ji-eun’s rewritten version hadn’t come back yet.

His plan had never been either/or. Charlie’s English version grew out of Western pop soil — hooks and rhythm sprouting naturally. If Ji-eun truly rewrote it for the Asian market, the center of gravity would land somewhere else entirely.

The best treatment was not to bet on who was more right. Put the two versions on one table and see whether they fused into something more complete.

If the fusion worked — final version. If not — release both.

Songs, provided they’re good enough, are allowed to have doubles. Nothing shameful about it.

April 2

Afternoon

The Yeonnam-dong studio — occupied by no one, and by seven cigarette butts

When Baek Si-on walked into the Yeonnam-dong studio, he found it empty.

The studio, as found

A stack of storyboard sheets spread across the desk, the pencil lines scrawled like the output of a seismograph.

The ashtray, standing at attention with seven or eight butts — establishing that Baek Jeong-hoon had been parked here for no short while.

Baek Si-on stood in the doorway for two seconds, took out his phone, and called his uncle.

Baek Si-on ↔ Baek Jeong-hoon · voice · from the Bluehole floor · the ashtray directive

Several rings before pickup. The background was chaos — several voices, keyboards, the whir of a projector fan, all threaded through with one irritable line from Baek Jeong-hoon: “Not there — push the camera from here, or it doesn’t read as madness.”

“Where are you?”

“Bluehole. These game kids are fascinating. I mentioned the nightclub-shootout previz and they built me a full 3D space on the spot — they can simulate individual bullet trajectories.”

“I’m at your studio.”

“Then empty the ashtray on my desk.”

Click. He hung up just like that.

Baek Si-on looked at the ended call on his screen, standing there in the hollow studio, and blinked for a few seconds.

Then he emptied the ashtray.

Out the door, down the stairs, back in the car.

“Nonhyeon-dong.”

April 2

Afternoon

Nonhyeon-dong · the seventh floor · a skeleton session

When Baek Si-on walked in, Jung Jae-joon was hunched over a stack of waveforms on his screen.

Baek Si-on knocked twice on the doorframe. Jae-joon, without turning, raised a hand — wait — and only after ten seconds pulled off one earphone.

“Back again — what for?”

“We’re building the skeleton of a new song.”

“One second.”

Jae-joon saved the session file and turned around.

“Direction?”

“Japanese-style lyric duet.”

Jae-joon nodded, hands already settling on the keyboard:

“Start.”

Baek Si-on went into the booth and hummed the melody from his head, twice through.

When he finished, Jae-joon asked:

“Key?”

“Major — but the chorus borrows from the minor. Not much. Just enough to make the point.”

Jae-joon’s hand stopped for a beat.

Baek Si-on kept going.

“Intro piano — not too dense. Left-hand bass and the lead line must not collide; leave air in it. Chorus strings come in half a beat late, and we build the layers through dynamics. Not by stacking tracks.”

Jae-joon stared at him for five full seconds.

“What exactly have you been studying behind my back?”

Baek Si-on was genuinely baffled.

“Dynamics. Transients. Bass conflicts. Track-stacking. Last month you still called reverb ‘that thing that makes the sound go far away.’”

Baek Si-on’s expression stayed perfectly calm.

“Picked up a few things.”

Jae-joon’s face went instantly vigilant.

“Studying with whom?”

“Looking around. Here and there.”

“Baek Si-on!”

“Mm.”

“This is not acceptable. Trust is the entire foundation of this business. You can’t keep using me while secretly taking night classes to replace me.”

Baek Si-on gave him a look.

“Replace you with what — I don’t do all-night, death-by-mixing sessions.”

“That’s not the point either!”

Jae-joon was deeply aggrieved:

“Once you’ve learned it all, the next time you wake at 2 a.m. with a melody, the first person you call may no longer be me.”

Baek Si-on decided this man could, at times, go toe-to-toe with Bieber.

“Relax. I’m learning arrangement to reduce the number of times I drag you out of bed at dawn. Not to make you unemployed.”

Jae-joon stared at him for a few seconds and gave a cold laugh.

“That sentence sounds like tenderness. In practice, it reads like the opening bars of a layoff notice.”

Baek Si-on declined to spend one more second on the topic.

“Song.”

Jae-joon glared for two more seconds, then finally turned around and opened a fresh session file.

The skeleton went up fast. Piano first; strings given their marks; the chorus modulation point re-tuned three times over. Somewhere in the middle, Jae-joon forgot his crisis entirely and slid into the state he was best at, fingers accelerating between keyboard and mouse.

When the demo took shape, Jae-joon pulled off the headphones and gave it one quiet listen on the monitors.

“This one’s good.”

“Mm.”

Baek Si-on checked the time. About right.

He stood to grab his jacket, and Jae-joon went on full alert.

“Got somewhere to be?”

“Mm.”

“What thing?”

“An appointment.”

Jae-joon paused for two seconds, visibly running a risk assessment on the sentence.

“Man or woman?”

Baek Si-on looked back at him.

Jae-joon kept his eyes dead on the screen:

“I need to establish the true motive behind your studies. If it’s for some woman, the whole thing changes category.”

“Bye.”

“Answer me!”

Baek Si-on was already out the door.

April 2

Late afternoon

The ‘The Producers’ set · a slow day, and a corner to wait in

The ‘The Producers’ set was moving slowly today.

The moment Baek Si-on showed his face, Jeong Han-teuk popped out from around a corner.

“You’re here AGAIN.”

“What do you mean, ‘again’.”

“Your visit frequency to Ji-eun this week is approaching the level of a fixed schedule credit.”

“I came for the lyrics.”

Han-teuk looked at him, began a sentence, swallowed it, began it again — and finally lost the fight.

“Do you believe that sentence yourself?”

Baek Si-on didn’t answer.

Because at this particular moment, no answer could be correct.

Han-teuk sighed, steered him to a seat off to the side, and lowered his voice:

“One more scene, and we’re nearly wrapped. Wait a little.”

Baek Si-on made an “mm” sound and sat.

Through the door, as overheard

The scene inside was still running. The door didn’t shut tight, and every so often Ji-eun’s lines drifted out — cold, carrying that particular Cindy blend of imperiousness and impatience.

Baek Si-on listened against the chairback for a couple of exchanges. Her speed of dropping into character had gotten noticeably faster lately. Or — possibly — the recent real-life emotional material had simply been repurposed with great efficiency. Roughly twenty minutes later, a “Cut” finally came from inside, and the staff scattered like people allowed to breathe again.

Lee Ji-eun walked out, saw Baek Si-on sitting there, and the corner of her mouth lifted — very slightly.

She ambled over, accepted the water Han-teuk handed her, took a sip, and only then looked down at him.

“Here again?”

Baek Si-on looked up at her.

“Came for the lyrics.”

“Excuse.”

“……”

Unhurried, she screwed the cap back on and handed the bottle to Han-teuk.

“I’m starving right now.”

“Hunger kills inspiration.”

“No inspiration, no good lyrics.”

“Therefore you should feed me first.”

Baek Si-on regarded that utterly justified face for two seconds.

“You’ve started packaging extortion as a creative workflow now?”

Lee Ji-eun blinked, the picture of innocence.

“It is not your first day knowing that I package things.”

Baek Si-on stood up in the end.

“Let’s go.”

Lee Ji-eun trailed after him in an excellent mood.

“You’re so agreeable.”

“I’m afraid you’ll starve to death, and the lyrics will die with you.”

“Stubborn mouth.”

“You had better actually hand over those lyrics later.”

“Depends on my mood~”

April 2

Evening

Yeouido · an izakaya in an unremarkable alley · the innermost booth

The restaurant sat in an alley in Yeouido you’d never look twice at — a tiny storefront, izakaya style inside, booths divided by wooden lattice, the lights pressed down low.

Not explicit enough to be a candlelit dinner. Also absolutely not a place where people discussed work.

Baek Si-on scanned the environment and said nothing at all.

Lee Ji-eun greeted the staff with practiced ease, and the two of them were shown to the innermost booth.

Once seated, she pushed the menu in front of him.

“You order.”

“You’re the hungry one, you order.”

“The person being treated orders the dishes. Basic etiquette.”

Baek Si-on flipped through two pages and pointed at a few things at random. Lee Ji-eun added two more dishes of her own, closed the menu, folded her hands on the table, and looked at him.

“You should feed me more often from now on.”

Baek Si-on was pouring water. His hand didn’t stop, but his eyes came up.

“Why?”

“So I can look at a handsome man more often. Good for the eyes.”

She considered, then added:

“It helps a lyricist’s mental health.”

Baek Si-on set the glass down.

“How do you deliver a line this suggestive with a face this innocent?”

Lee Ji-eun propped her chin on one hand, gave the question some genuine thought, and blinked, lightly.

“Maybe because I have no designs on you.”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

Lee Ji-eun looked right back, her expression as blameless as someone freshly walked out of church.

“No designs?” Baek Si-on repeated.

“Mm.”

“No designs — then the chocolate you fed me mouth-to-mouth yesterday?”

“That was sharing.”

“Sharing with your mouth?”

“Imported goods deserve imported methods.”

“……”

Baek Si-on stopped talking altogether.

Watching his expression, the laughter in her eyes crept up bit by bit.

“Although… if you wanted me to have some…”

“I could arrange that.”

With that, she winked her right eye at him.

Baek Si-on lowered his head, picked up his chopsticks, and began eating the just-served appetizer with tremendous seriousness.

Lee Ji-eun propped her chin and watched him eat, the smile never leaving.

“Secretly, you’re enjoying this enormously, aren’t you?”

Baek Si-on’s chewing slowed by half a beat.

“What?”

The three-finger audit, as conducted over dinner

Finger one, raised: “A girl this pretty.”

Finger two: “This smart.”

Finger three: “And this rich — chasing after YOU.”

All three fingers waggled in front of his face, with the closing argument: “You must be thrilled to pieces, right?”

Baek Si-on looked up at her.

“Your self-assessed market valuation has always been optimistic.”

Not a gram of modesty — she even lifted her chin.

“Not optimistic. Accurate.”

Baek Si-on made an “mm” sound.

“Accurate to the point that in Milan, you wouldn’t even let me near the Fendi after-party?”

Her smile froze for half a second, then sailed up again:

“I was saving you.”

Baek Si-on raised one eyebrow.

“Places like Fendi after-parties don’t suit a type as easily led away as you.”

“I’m easily led away?” Baek Si-on asked.

“Look at where you’re sitting right now—”

She gestured at him loosely with her chopsticks:

“I say I’m hungry, you feed me. I say no inspiration, you wait obediently. And you’re telling me you’re not easily led away?”

Baek Si-on: “……”

“So that’s how it is.”

“Then you really should be a little nervous.”

Lee Ji-eun tilted her head slightly, waiting for the rest.

“A man this handsome, this good at making money, who hands out resources in passing.”

“Not common in the market.”

She broke — laughed outright.

“Baek Si-on.”

“Mm.”

“You got one part wrong.”

“Which part?”

“Not ‘not common.’ Extremely scarce.”

Chin on hand, the smile still hanging at the corner of her mouth:

“But it wasn’t all wrong either. Keep this up, and the people who like you will only multiply. The queue will need number tickets.”

“Coming from your mouth, that doesn’t sound like a warning no matter how I hear it.”

“What does it sound like, then?”

“Like you’re planning to cut the entire queue.”

Lee Ji-eun laughed hard enough that her shoulders shook.

“You can’t blame me for that.”

“The VIP lane is simply very close to me.”

Baek Si-on: “……”

He realized, too late, that he should never have taken the bait of her little boast.

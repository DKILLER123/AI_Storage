Chapter One Hundred Seventy Six

Your Dad Is Amazing

December 26–27 · one handshake, then three time zones One deal closed by arithmetic · a press release timed to Seoul’s 6 a.m. · one kingdom of hashtags · a Sado set that laughs until its director goes quiet · a toothbrush frozen mid-air Universal, Los Angeles → Seoul · the morning commute, Suwon Film Studio, one apartment with a hairless landlord Universal surrenders on costs, not art; a nation’s media sits bolt upright; a period-drama set discovers its young king has gone to Hollywood; a director dares to have an impractical thought; and a cat is told, inaccurately, whose daughter it should start rooting for.

Universal agreed in the end.

When the conference room door opened again, the music supervisor’s expression had lost its frost.

Which was normal.

Art is allowed to have principles. Marketing is required to have spreadsheets.

The production representative spoke first:

“We agree to Mr. Baek’s version.”

Wiz heard it and laughed outright.

“Now that’s the correct decision.”

The music supervisor gave him a look and let the line go unanswered.

The production rep continued:

“However — since Mr. Baek is now part of the theme song, we’d like his support for the film’s Asian marketing.”

The smile had just finished assembling itself on Scooter’s face when it stopped.

“Support to what extent?”

“Premieres and press in a few key Asian markets.”

“How many?”

“Seoul, Tokyo, Hong Kong, Shanghai, Singapore.”

Scooter laughed out loud.

“You want a Billboard chart-topping artist to spend a month running red carpets around Asia?”

“What kind of joke is this?”

The production rep’s face stiffened.

The marketing head hurried in:

“We can cover appearance fees.”

“You’ll certainly cover them. But the problem was never the money. It’s the time.”

Baek Si-on had North American radio ahead, magazine features, a test-show to prepare, and a stack of endorsements and music projects.

Stuffing him into a film’s Asia press line meant dismantling a machine running at full speed to use it as Universal’s display model.

The parties ground through another round.

The final arrangement was simple.

Baek Si-on would attend one Furious 7 premiere — the one in Korea — with a solo performance segment of his own.

“Then we’re done.”

Scooter rose and put out his hand.

“Pleasure doing business.”

Though when the music supervisor shook that hand, his face carried nothing that resembled pleasure.

❧ ❧ ❧

Korea time. A little after six in the morning.

Seoul wasn’t fully awake yet. Office workers’ alarms were on their first rings; students were still negotiating with fate beneath their blankets.

Universal Pictures’ official account published a press item.

Universal Pictures · official press release

@UniversalPictures · 6:04 a.m. KST

Baek Si-on joins Wiz Khalifa to lend his voice to the theme song of Furious 7. Coming soon.

Attached: one photo — Wiz’s arm slung over Baek Si-on’s shoulder, both mid-laugh, sunglasses pushed up on Wiz’s head.

The moment the item went live, Korean media sat bolt upright like the dying man in the old poem hearing the good news.

The keyboards of every newsroom lit up at once.

Kim Jae-wook, entertainment desk chief, had just reached the office when his editor shouted across the room:

“Chief! Universal posted!”

“What Universal?”

“Universal Pictures! Baek Si-on is singing the Furious 7 theme!”

Kim Jae-wook froze for a beat.

Then the whole man jolted as if electrified.

“Write! Now!”

“What headline?”

“You’re asking ME for the headline?”

He flung his scarf across the back of the chair.

“Baek Si-on marches into Hollywood!”

The editor hesitated:

“…He’s only singing the theme song.”

Kim Jae-wook glared at him.

“Is Furious not Hollywood?”

“It is.”

“Then we’re done here!”

❧ ❧ ❧

Minutes later, the portals’ entertainment sections went collectively insane.

The wire items poured out like dumplings off a fast ladle.

And in fairness to them — this wasn’t their fault.

Korean entertainment had spent twenty years knocking on Hollywood’s door.

Rain had gone. He played a ninja. He got roasted for it.

Lee Byung-hun had gone. He played a villain with fewer than twenty lines.

Jun Ji-hyun had tried. She took a Hollywood B-movie, came home, and never mentioned the experience again.

Every single time, the media wrote “marches into Hollywood” in a fever — and six months later the story went quiet.

Because the results were always about the same. An Asian face in Hollywood was kung fu, a sidekick, or the nameless bystander who dies first in the disaster movie.

This time was different.

Baek Si-on’s voice would sit at the end of this film. On screens across a hundred-plus countries and territories, in the ears of every red-eyed viewer walking out of the theater.

Seven a.m. The subway rush began.

In a Line 2 car, someone read the item, went still, and handed the phone to the colleague beside them.

“Look at this.”

“Baek Si-on? Furious?”

“No way.”

“It’s real. Universal posted it.”

The car filled with low murmuring.

In the convenience store, the clerk was stacking the morning papers when her phone buzzed; she looked down and nearly shelved the stack upside down.

At the bus stop, a high schooler let his bag slide off one shoulder and held his screen out to his friend.

“Baek Si-on is singing the Furious song!”

“What?”

“Furious Seven!”

“That’s insane, right?”

“Universal just posted it!”

In the office towers, workers who had just clocked in sat down and checked their phones before opening their computers — and froze in place.

The comment sections were already on fire.

By 8:30, the real-time boards belonged to him again.

Four of the top four. The board had stopped pretending to be a contest.

The Korean public was beginning to go numb.

A week ago: the free concert. Days ago: the ‘Love Yourself’ video charging the YouTube record. Today: a Hollywood blockbuster theme song.

Every time, the country decided this must surely be the biggest recent news.

And every time, Baek Si-on proved the same point with action.

Don’t rush.

There’s more.

❧ ❧ ❧

Suwon Film Studio. The Sado production had entered its final stretch of shooting.

The closer to the wrap, the less anyone dared to relax — everyone knew the last scenes before a film wraps are the ones most likely to go wrong.

The actors’ bodies were worn down. Months of shooting had scattered the crew’s attention.

And of course the scene now on the docket was the one where the guest star So Ji-sub — as King Jeongjo, after his enthronement — mourns Crown Prince Sado.

Incense burners, ritual vessels, screens, candle stands: not one object could be out of place.

Period drama is like that. Audiences may never notice the frame you got perfectly right, but one vessel on the wrong side of the table, and the forums will produce three pages of scholarship by dinner.

The crew moved equipment and gossiped in low voices.

“Did you see the news?”

“The Baek Si-on thing?”

“Yeah. Furious Seven.”

“Daebak. Our Crown Prince is about to ring out over Hollywood…”

A costume hand nearby nearly dropped the royal sash he was holding, laughing.

Moon Geun-young sat in her chair arranging her costume, listened to the crew, and looked over at So Ji-sub, who was settling into the scene’s sorrow a few steps away.

“Your Highness.”

So Ji-sub turned — the man carried a grave, heavy stillness between the brows without even trying.

“Your royal father has marched into Hollywood.”

So Ji-sub blinked, understood she meant Baek Si-on, and sighed on cue.

“Then the pressure on this son is enormous.”

Moon Geun-young held her laughter down:

“Shall you sing one as well?”

So Ji-sub considered it.

“If I sing, Father may climb out of the rice chest to stop me.”

Several crew members lost the fight and laughed out loud.

Humor on a period-drama set runs short by design.

Because the moment the laughter ends, you climb straight back into the heaviness.

Director Lee Joon-ik had been standing behind the monitors studying the set dressing.

He smiled at their joking too.

But as he smiled, a thought flashed through his head.

A thought that had always been too far away to see the road to — and it had just lit up, a little, right in front of him.

He turned to the assistant director:

“I need to make a call.”

The assistant director faltered.

“Now? We’re about to roll.”

“Wait for me, then start.”

The assistant director looked at the set, then at the actors.

“Okay.”

Lee Joon-ik walked out to an empty corner beyond the stage walls. Winter air knifed through the gaps in the buildings and stiffened his face; he pulled out Baek Si-on’s number and dialed.

A few rings — then it connected.

Baek Si-on had just come out of a meeting on the final release plan for his Under Armour collaboration line — January 1, a simultaneous global launch — and had barely reached for his water when the call came.

Baek Si-on ← Lee Joon-ik · Los Angeles ↔ Suwon Film Studio · incoming

Director.

Si-on-ah. It’s afternoon your side, yes? I’m not interrupting work?

Not at all. Go ahead.

Lee Joon-ik skipped the usual circling.

I just read the Furious Seven news.

Mm.

So you have a working relationship with Universal now.

I suppose I do.

If Sado gets chosen as Korea’s submission next year — do you know anyone in America who understands Academy PR?

The question sat there, enormous and unembarrassed.

Director. Are you serious?

A laugh came down the line — half sheepish, half absolutely serious.

A man has to keep one impractical thought, or what is he making films for?

Every year, Korea’s culture ministry and the film council selected one film of the year to compete for the Best Foreign Language Oscar. It sounded like a national team.

But on the actual field, Korean films had lost, and lost, and lost.

Not because the films were bad. Korea didn’t lack directors, and it didn’t lack actors.

The problem was that the Academy was not a post office. You didn’t mail a print and wait for the members to watch it at home, attentively, voting with their consciences.

It was a publicity war. A charm offensive. The mood management of several thousand Academy members.

Said bluntly: a foreign film without a North American PR channel rarely even got its file opened.

Korea had lost many times at the door, before the race began.

Baek Si-on understood all of it — and his own math was colder than the director’s.

Korean audiences would meet this film with historical resonance. East Asian audiences would understand the pressure of clan law, kingship, and family hierarchy.

But making a room full of white American elders understand why a father would lock his son inside a rice chest — that was a high wall. Subtitles couldn’t climb it.

He thought, then said:

“A campaign costs money.”

“Director — is Showbox willing to pay for that? Or, behind Showbox, is the Orion Group willing to spend on a real campaign?”

That was the realest point in the whole conversation.

Shouting “to the Oscars” is blood-warm. Actually going means spending.

He remembered, from the other timeline, exactly what a true campaign cost: CJ had burned nearly ten million US dollars on Parasite’s alone, start to finish.

“It should be possible.”

Lee Joon-ik was vague — but it wasn’t a no.

“When you’re back in Korea, we’ll have a meal and talk it through.”

Baek Si-on nodded, pointlessly, across an ocean.

“Done.”

Lee Joon-ik exhaled, lighter.

“Then back to work you go.”

“Hard at work, Director.”

“……”

❧ ❧ ❧

A little after eight in the morning, Choi Jin-ri saw the news while brushing her teeth.

Mouth full of foam, phone propped beside the sink. When the screen lit, she had only glanced at it in passing.

Then she read the push headline, and her toothbrush stopped.

the lock screen · 8:07 a.m.

Baek Si-on joins Wiz Khalifa on the Furious 7 special theme song

One toothbrush, suspended. One mouthful of foam, unclaimed.

She blinked. Leaned closer. Confirmed that no outlet had simply lost its mind on the headline — and the corners of her mouth curled up slowly.

Then, through a full mouth of toothpaste foam, she mumbled:

“So impressive.”

Only after saying it did she realize how silly she must look, and bent hurriedly to rinse.

When she came out washed, Blin was crouched on the sofa in its little hoodie, the absolutely-proprietor of the living room — a hairless landlord with no vacancies.

Choi Jin-ri crossed the room and swept it up.

“Blin!”

Blin’s face went instantly grave. It clearly disapproved of this sudden vertical mode of transport.

She was too excited to care, holding it up to eye level.

“Did you know?”

“He’s singing for Furious.”

“The one with the fast cars, the very short hair, and the enormous number of family members.”

“Pretty amazing, right?”

Blin turned its head toward the sofa.

She turned it back.

“Look at me.”

“Your dad is amazing.”

The words left her mouth — and she froze first of all, her cheeks going pink in slow degrees.

“Not—”

“I mean… the man who bought you.”

Blin stared at her.

Its green eyes carried a full sentence: go on, keep inventing.

She tucked it back into her arms and muttered:

“Whatever. You don’t understand anyway.”

Blin pressed a paw flat against her chest, as if to remind her: do not underestimate cats.

Just then her phone rang.

Her manager.

She picked up.

Choi Jin-ri ← her manager · the apartment, Seoul · incoming

Jin-ri-ya — I’ll be at your place in half an hour.

What? Why so sudden?

Director Yeon Sang-ho’s side contacted me.

What did he say?

Her heartbeat went from idle to racing in one beat.

He wants you at a second audition.

Really?

Really. I’ll bring coffee on the way. Wash up, get dressed — and don’t wind yourself tight.

Okay.

The call ended.

She stood in place for a few seconds, digesting, then looked down at the cat actively escaping her arms:

“You have to cheer for your mom, understood?”

Blin planted one paw on her forearm and kept climbing.

“Your line is: amazing!”

Blin kept climbing.

She pulled it back.

“Do not evade the question.”

Blin, forced to face her, produced:

“Meow—”

“Is that a yes?”

She studied the small face, which appeared to read life is exhausting, and burst out laughing.

She sat back on the sofa with the cat and scratched its chin.

“Blin-ah. Honestly? I’m a little nervous.”

“But if it were him — he’d say: if you’re nervous, go rehearse your lines. Stop talking to the cat.”

She did his flat, grave voice so faithfully that she cracked up first at herself.

“But it’s fine.”

She leaned her head back against the sofa and looked out at the bright morning.

“He’s off singing for Hollywood blockbusters now.”

“I can’t just stand in place.”

She bent down and kissed the hairless forehead.

Blin shifted in her arms, found a warmer position, and closed its eyes.

Choi Jin-ri stroked its back, and the smile never left.

Good news, kept to one person, is only luck.

But knowing that another person is also becoming better — that is called peace of mind.

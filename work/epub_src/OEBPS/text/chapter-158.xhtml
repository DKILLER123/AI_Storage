Chapter One Hundred Fifty Eight

The Wind That Never Got Stronger

December 4 · 8:00 a.m. — until past nine that night The morning after MAMA · a war ending before noon · a stadium after dark Seoul · the internet, then Hwaseong Haenggung, then Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium The wind, per the record, never picked up. Nearly everything else did.

At eight in the morning, the Korean internet had still not woken up from last night’s MAMA.

Accurately: it had never slept. From the moment the ceremony ended in Hong Kong to first light in Seoul, the portals, the forums, the social platforms and every fandom fansite operated like a single giant hand was holding down one shared refresh key. The hot searches rotated, and rotated, and rotated again.

The portal front pages had laid their first row out very neatly.

Portal front pages · first row · 8:00 a.m. · Naver / Dispatch / OSEN

Baek Si-on takes MAMA Artist of the Year — the opening stage that flattened the field

IU wins Song of the Year with ‘Friday’, then hugs Baek Si-on on stage — the internet ignites

EXO claims Album of the Year — physical sales and the fandom floor prove dominance all over again

The fourth slot had been left, per tradition, for whatever the forums were about to do to all three of them.

The forums were livelier.

Every year, after every major ceremony, the fandoms fought in the forums; it was practically the Korean entertainment industry’s fixed year-end programming. The rules were simple: the fandoms whose idol missed the expected award fired first, and the fandoms who had taken the award handled the replies.

All three daesang holders of the night — EXO, Lee Ji-eun, Baek Si-on — were dragged out for frame-by-frame trial, no exceptions.

The fiercest by far were Taeyang’s fans.

To most of them, Song of the Year not going to Taeyang was already hard to swallow. Worse: re-watching Ma-ryong’s improvised, rewritten bars, the more they listened the more convinced they became that the rap had been about Baek Si-on all along. The hot-post titles turned hostile at speed.

Six months ago, Baek Si-on couldn’t have scraped together a single person to defend him.

Now was different. Film fans in one squad, digital-music fans in another, face fans, overseas data fans — plus one strange cohort of passersby who had enlisted through the Silver Crown Crown Prince. All at once they had a unified front, and their first genuine large-scale brawl opportunity.

The two sides fought from before dawn. By 9:30 a.m., the topic had climbed onto the hot searches proper.

…

Baek Si-on found out about it in the makeup room at Hwaseong Haenggung.

He had flown back from Hong Kong to Incheon overnight, slept almost not at all, and been delivered to the Suwon set before dawn.

“Cousin.”

“Mm?”

“Your fans are fighting.”

Baek Si-on took the phone, read a few threads, scrolled further. Stopped for a few seconds.

“I have this many fans now?”

: “……”

She weighed whether that was the point, established that it was not, and moved on.

“Cousin — how about you play some ‘Eyes, Nose, Lips’.”

Baek Si-on: “?”

Baek Eun-ah thought it through and liked it more every second.

“You perform HIS song, and the meaning is unmistakable: you don’t think he’s bad, and you don’t think the rap was aimed at you. The moment fans see the man himself lowering the ladder, they’ll come down it.”

“Breaking up a fight with a love song?”

“Better than a statement.”

Baek Si-on considered.

“Alright.”

“That fast?”

“What, should I divine it first?”

“……No need.”

Baek Eun-ah turned a slow circle, surveying the rest tent. Props sat along the wall: a scholar’s desk, a go board, scrolls — and a geomungo that had just been carried over from the neighboring set.

Her eyes lit.

“That one works.”

Baek Si-on followed her gaze.

“You’re sure?”

“Crown Prince Sado — zither, go, calligraphy, painting. Master of all four.”

“Since when am I a master of all four?”

“From the moment the video posts.”

“……”

Very good. His manager had become frighteningly good at manufacturing personas on location.

Five minutes later, an unheated study set at the Haenggung had been roughly cleared out for use. Winter light came cold through the lattice window; the room was frigid as an ice cellar that had also slept badly.

the moon-white crown prince · Hwaseong Haenggung, unheated study set Suwon · 9:40 a.m. · cold as a plot device

The robe Moon-white crown prince silk, pale as the winter light and about as warm — the Sado drama wardrobe doing double duty as conflict-resolution equipment. No crown, no formal regalia: an off-duty prince, in theory, which the internet would read as effortless and the set medic would read as a workplace incident waiting to be filed.
The instrument A geomungo, technically. In practice, the set’s favorite piece of furniture-as-atmosphere, hauled in from the neighboring build and now promoted to international diplomacy. Two test notes in, it produced the specific creak of an object that had planned an easier Tuesday.
The atmosphere An unheated room in December, doing its absolute best — every sung note trailing a small white curl of breath, free of charge. Production could not have rented better dry ice.

Zither, go, calligraphy, painting — master of all four, from the moment the video posts.

Baek Eun-ah circled with the phone, hunting angles, and offered the only production note of the session: nobody would be auditing a pop idol against a traditional-music standard — the melody surviving intact was enough.

Baek Si-on sat in front of the geomungo and tried two notes, frowning.

“This instrument is drafting a resignation letter.”

“It survives on melody alone. Start?”

“Mm.”

The strings woke up.

The melody was simple. A few bars in, it was identifiable.

‘Eyes, Nose, Lips’.

No showing off. Head down, plucking slowly, singing slowly — and none of Taeyang’s heart-tearing grief in it. Composed, level, distant: as if love and hate were both, respectfully, somebody else’s business.

The comment counter gave up counting somewhere around the third laugh.

The internet laughed, and forwarded, and the video traveled fast. Baek Si-on’s own fans, watching the man himself do that, found it suddenly difficult to keep swinging at Taeyang’s fans.

Nothing ends a fan war faster than your own idol strolling in carrying the fire extinguisher. Keep fighting? Look unhinged. Stop fighting? The keyboard was still warm. In the end, the forums could only leave one line behind:

“The man himself has played. Dismissed.”

Before noon, Taeyang’s personal account followed Baek Si-on.

No post. No interaction. One follow.

It was enough.

the hot-search board · before noon

OUT — “Baek Si-on Taeyang fan war”

IN — “Taeyang follows Baek Si-on”

Across the forums, half-written essays on honor and conspiracy lay down where they stood and collectively gave up their outlines.

When Baek Eun-ah scrolled into the follow, she let out a breath she had been rationing all morning and carried the phone over to show him. He was changing robes for the next scene; he glanced.

“Resolved.”

“Mm.”

She stared at him.

“That’s the entire reaction?”

“What other reaction is there?”

“At least marvel a little.”

Baek Si-on considered the request on its merits.

“Taeyang’s nice.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

Beside them, Park Ji-hun lost the fight with his own face and laughed.

“Do you mean the singer Taeyang, or the one overhead?”

“Both nice.”

“……”

Wonderful. Baek Si-on-brand deadpan was polluting the indoor air again.

Baek Eun-ah went back to the comments. The overseas fans had arrived: was this official behind-the-scenes material for Sado, and why was a historical role singing a modern love song? Someone earnestly explained that Korean history did not, at any point, include ‘Eyes, Nose, Lips’. The reply came instantly:

“Thank you — I almost thought Yeongjo had heard Taeyang too.”

Baek Eun-ah’s shoulders shook once. She screenshotted it to Park Ji-hun, who read it with a scholar’s gravity.

“If Yeongjo had heard it, Sado’s ending would’ve been worse.”

“Why?”

“Because Father would have asked why his singing lacks Taeyang’s heart-tearing grief.”

“Maybe because the crown prince hadn’t been shut in the rice chest yet?”

“……That joke is freezing.”

So was the set, for the record.

…

Evening. After the Haenggung wrapped, Baek Si-on didn’t go to the hotel — he had Master Zhao point the car at Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium.

Just past nine, the car threaded into the venue district. The venue manager met them at the gate and walked them down into the infield.

The main stage had a skeleton now. The central steel ran high; the rear wall waited for the main screen; side-stage channels branched out both ways; and at the very front, a thrust stage already reached out into where the floor section would be. Protection boards covered the ground, workers pushed equipment cases back and forth, and somewhere in the distance a lighting rig fired test flashes — white pulses sweeping across the empty stands.

Baek Eun-ah stood at the field edge, taking the manager’s briefing — security corridors, spectator gates, the outdoor hot-drink zone, the medical stations — and editing notes into her tablet as she listened.

“What about out-of-town coach parking?”

“They’re marking the lot now — final sign-off rests with the traffic authorities.”

Mid-sentence, someone came wandering in through the entrance: knit beanie, scarf up to the chin, hands buried in her coat pockets, strolling the empty access aisle and looking left and right like a tourist who had misplaced her tour group.

Baek Eun-ah saw her first and blanked.

“Jin-ri eonni?”

Choi Jin-ri withdrew a hand from its pocket and waved.

“Eun-ah.”

The venue manager hurried to explain: Choi Jin-ri-ssi had phoned that afternoon, saying she wanted to confirm her concert seat; her name was already on Manager Baek’s list, so she had been let through. Baek Eun-ah remembered the afternoon question — is the venue building already? — which she had taken as small talk. This eonni had actually come.

the seat-inspection uniform · official business, allegedly Jamsil · 9:10 p.m. · temperature: personal

The white knit beanie Pulled low, round as a snowball, zeroes out recognizable idol hair at forty meters — provided nobody looks at her face, which is a well-known national treasure, or at her walk, which the whole country can identify from the back.
The scarf and coat A cream scarf wound up to the chin and a long dark coat with the hands permanently installed in the pockets: the official winter uniform of someone who is definitely just here to check whether a pillar blocks her view, and absolutely not here to see where a certain person is going to stand.
The gait Unhurried, hands pocketed, head on a slow swivel — a person conducting a seat inspection with the thoroughness of someone who intends to occupy that seat on December 18 and remember everything about tonight while sitting in it.

Beanie down, scarf up, hands pocketed — inspecting, officially, like anyone would.

The venue manager, having verified her name against the list, decided this was a completely normal pre-concert activity by a completely anonymous member of the public, and went back to his briefing with the calm of a man who valued his winter bonus.

“Eonni — you really came to inspect your seat?”

Choi Jin-ri smiled, innocence in bulk.

“Of course. What if a pillar blocks my view?”

“……Your seat is on the floor.”

“Inspecting in advance anyway.”

She said it, and then her gaze traveled past Baek Eun-ah with perfect naturalness and landed on Baek Si-on — and tipped up, at the unfinished main screen.

“Is this really going to be full? Sixty thousand people?”

“About fifty-eight thousand.”

“Even two thousand short sounds enormous.”

“Which is why we check the blind spots.”

“I want to check too.”

Baek Si-on tipped his head at her.

“What are you checking?”

Choi Jin-ri blinked, once.

“Where you’re going to be standing.”

The words left her mouth, were reviewed by their owner, and were judged too direct; her eyes snapped back to the stage at speed.

“……Also checking my seat.”

Baek Si-on didn’t take the confession apart.

They walked a full loop along the bottom of the stands. Somewhere in the second turn, she remembered.

“I saw your video.”

“Which one?”

“The crown prince playing ‘Eyes, Nose, Lips’ at the Haenggung.”

She laughed before she finished the sentence.

“The comments all say the rites have collapsed.”

“They’re right.”

“But why does your breath fog when you sing? Special effects?”

“Because it’s cold.”

“……”

At a random block of seats, Baek Si-on sat down to test the stage sightline — and stood straight back up the next second.

Baek Eun-ah looked up.

“What?”

Baek Si-on looked down at the plastic seat.

“Cold.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

Plastic seats in an open stadium on a December night being cold — she checked her memory — was, in fact, the normal operation of the physical universe.

“Order sixty thousand padded cushions.”

“Okay.”

She nodded.

“I’ll call the factories first thing tomorrow.”

“For sixty thousand, isn’t first thing tomorrow slightly tight?”

The question arrived in a sweet voice from directly beside him. Choi Jin-ri stood there, hands behind her back, beaming at Baek Eun-ah.

“Send the inquiry emails tonight, collect the first round of quotes tomorrow morning — right?”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

She looked at Choi Jin-ri, smiling with total harmlessness; she looked at Baek Si-on, providing zero interference; a cold wind went by; and Baek Eun-ah understood that she was now, officially, more surplus than that row of plastic seats.

“Fine.”

She clamped the tablet under her arm.

“I’ll go send the emails now.”

A few steps out, she looked back once. Choi Jin-ri waved at her, gently, smiling very, very sweetly. Baek Eun-ah turned back around with no expression whatsoever.

Sweet nothing.

She finally understood it now: kind sunbaes will also engineer your manager out of the way.

…

With Baek Eun-ah gone, Park Ji-hun tactfully evacuated himself too, following the venue manager off to inspect the backstage channels.

Baek Si-on and Choi Jin-ri walked the red rubber track along the infield, slowly. The work lights threw their shadows out across the ground. Out at the main stage the hammering went on, amplified by the empty bowl of the stadium and pressed back down again by the winter night.

Neither of them mentioned last night. Not the hug. Not scared, and did it anyway. They just walked, aimlessly, in front of a stage that was half a stage so far.

At the exact front of the main stage, Choi Jin-ri stopped.

She looked up at the blank rectangle where the main screen would hang, and thought about December 18 — sixty thousand people seated out there, watching Baek Si-on stand at the brightest point on earth. She would be in some seat of her own, watching him.

But right now the stadium stood empty. The lights weren’t all up yet. And the wind was cold in exactly the right measure.

So she took her hands out of her pockets and rubbed her arms, theatrically.

“Cold?” he asked.

“It’s fine.” She shook her head.

Baek Si-on said nothing. He pinched the zipper of his long Under Armour down coat and pulled it down. The zip ran to the bottom; the coat fell open in a slit.

the sixty-thousand-seat windbreak · Jamsil, unassisted 9:47 p.m. · wind: unchanged

The coat A long black Under Armour down coat, stadium-grade, never formally rated as two-person accommodation — and about to be informally rated so. Zipped to the collar it is winter equipment. Opened one deliberate notch, it becomes an invitation with plausible deniability, which is the only kind he issues.
The interior Down insulation, body heat, and a volume of calm air roughly the size of one person’s entire stubbornness. Acoustics: muffled hammering. Climate: improving on contact. Occupancy: about to double without a single form being filed.
The weather Wind, measured at the anemometer nobody deployed: no change whatsoever. Wind, measured inside the coat: irrelevant, and — by unanimous consent of all parties — recorded as “stronger.”

The wind did not, at any point, pick up. The coat did.

Somewhere across the infield, sixty thousand future cushions entered the procurement pipeline, entirely unaware they had made this moment possible by getting a manager out of the way.

He hadn’t managed any next move at all when a flash of white knit swung in front of him —

Choi Jin-ri dove in.

Fast: the speed of someone who had been waiting for exactly this cue since the parking lot. Her arms went around his waist, her face buried hard into his chest, her whole self folded into that wide down coat.

Baek Si-on looked down.

His entire field of view now contained one white knit beanie, and below its brim, a small section of ear.

The ear was visibly, conclusively red.

Out in the distance the workers kept hammering steel. But the small territory the down coat fenced off had, without asking anyone’s permission, been annexed out of the winter night entirely.

“……Didn’t you say it was fine?”

The reply came up from inside the coat, muffled to the edge of legibility.

“It stopped being fine.”

“Reason?”

“The wind picked up.”

Baek Si-on surveyed the record.

The wind had not picked up.

He didn’t take the data apart. He just gathered the coat a little closer over her shoulder.

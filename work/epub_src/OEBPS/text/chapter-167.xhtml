Chapter One Hundred Sixty Seven

The Peach Ripens, and Starts Growing Thorns

December 18 · from an hour after the bow to somewhere past midnight Charcoal, tongs, and territorial disputes · one intercepted fall · a car ride verdict A hanwoo grill house near Jamsil · the thank-you dinner Sixty thousand people have gone home; the people who sent them out now argue over who is allowed to touch the tongs. A peach, having quietly ripened all year, chooses tonight to grow thorns — and a hunter discovers what that costs.

Baek Si-on had not booked a hotel banquet hall.

Those are built for photographs. They are not built for letting an entire lighting crew, stage crew, and dance team actually eat their fill.

So the thank-you dinner went to a hanwoo grill house near Jamsil.

The place was nothing fancy. Its virtues were otherwise: it was big, the private rooms opened into each other, and the ventilation actually worked.

Two strings of little bulbs hung over the entrance. On the glass door, a sign taped up for the night:

the door sign · handwritten, temporary, absolute

PRIVATE EVENT — CLOSED FOR BUSINESS

Taped at eye level, letters large enough to read from across the street. Nobody in the industry had ever needed it translated.

Inside, the room was already full. The band at one table. The dancers at another. Stage crew, lighting, the broadcast truck delegation — the crews had claimed their own territories. Red Velvet and BTS had been seated toward the back: close enough to be looked after, far enough that nobody had to perform being casual around them.

The grills were already hot. The first slice of beef hit the pan with a hiss, and the smell of searing fat leaned down hard over the December chill everyone had carried in from Jamsil.

Baek Si-on arrived late.

The concert ending had not ended anything. Six thousand items on the checklist: did the sixty thousand clear out safely; had the shuttle buses actually departed; was the tvN footage handed over; was the venue’s account at first-pass reconciliation; had every sponsor rep been greeted by name. Only after the last box got ticked did he push open the grill house door.

The doorbell chimed once.

The room went quiet for exactly one instant.

Then the cheering detonated.

the door · the reception

“Baek Si-on-ssi!”

“You worked hard!”

“Daebak concert!”

“Tonight was really something!”

Somewhere a staffer hoisted a beer glass; a dancer beat out a rhythm on the tabletop. He nodded his way through it all.

“Thank you, everyone. Truly.”

Then, sideways, to the person who’d come in with him:

“Go find a seat first.”

Choi Jin-ri nodded and surveyed the room. People everywhere. Every table loud. She was still deciding which direction to drift when somebody waved at her from a corner.

Lee Ji-eun.

Window table. Side dishes and raw beef already laid out.

Choi Jin-ri thought about it for exactly as long as it took to pretend to think about it, and went over.

Lee Ji-eun looked her up and down, grinning.

“Peach. What is with the deeply ill-fitting coat? On purpose?”

Choi Jin-ri’s private style was, in fairness, nationally respected. Fashion-magazine favorite; fanscreens of her airport looks got passed around like currency. The figure she cut right now — a kid in a stolen adult coat — was a deliberate scandal.

“Ah~”

She looked down at herself and laughed.

“It’s not like that.”

She had deliberately left the sentence hanging — bait on the hook. The plan: Lee Ji-eun would ask, and then the conversation would proceed, smoothly and legally, to a certain someone.

Unfortunately, Lee Ji-eun had already clocked the coat as the same model as Baek Si-on’s, and had further concluded that “I bought it too big” was not the story here.

So she decided not to ask.

“Reading any new scripts lately?”

Choi Jin-ri: “……”

What is wrong with this person.

She sat down.

“Actually yes. I auditioned for something recently.”

“For what?”

Choi Jin-ri hesitated.

“Not decided yet.”

“Who’s directing?”

“Director Yeon Sang-ho.”

“A film?”

“Mm.”

“Genre?”

“Disaster, I think.”

“……”

Lee Ji-eun was drawing breath to pursue this — when a round of clapping broke out behind them. Baek Si-on had reached the middle of the room with a raised glass, asking for quiet. Both women stopped talking and turned.

“Getting tonight done took everyone here — sixty thousand people walked in, and walked out safe.”

“Thank you.”

A beat. He looked around the room.

“No rules tonight. Eat until you’re done.”

“If we run out, we order more.”

The cheer nearly lifted the ceiling.

the toast · the improvised lyrics of a full stomach

“OHHHHH!!!”

“Cheers!”

“You worked hard!”

“Baek Si-on-ssi, daebak!”

Glasses went up all over the room. Baek Si-on drained his in one go.

“Eat well, everyone.”

Which set off the table-banging all over again. Baek Si-on absorbed the assortment of chaotic toasts with no visible change in expression, and nodded.

At a corner table, Baek Eun-ah waved him over. He crossed, pulled out a chair, sat.

The grill in front of them had come up to temperature — beef edges glistening, garlic slices going gold at the rim.

Across the table: Choi Jin-ri, tongs in hand, flipping and turning, saying:

“Hungry?”

Baek Si-on didn’t answer. He turned his head and looked at Baek Eun-ah.

Because by the customs of this country, grill duty belongs to the youngest person at the table. Whichever way you rotated the roster, it did not rotate onto Choi Jin-ri.

Baek Eun-ah, under that look, radiated injustice.

She had genuinely wanted to grill. She had picked up the tongs. She had been barred by her eonni. That was the whole file.

Choi Jin-ri glanced up, read the geometry instantly, and smoothed it over:

“Oppa, don’t bully Eun-ah. She’s been run off her feet all day — and I haven’t done anything today anyway.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

She was suddenly aware that she had, perhaps, no situational awareness whatsoever.

In her defense: grilling, in her mental model, auto-assigned itself to people who were good at the small logistics of living. At home there was a younger brother to exploit; outside, there was Yoo In-na to deploy. If both were unavailable, there was always her manager, Jung Han-teul, to requisition.

But yes. Tonight, she was technically supposed to help.

She was opening her mouth to volunteer — when Choi Jin-ri, without breaking rhythm, placed a piece of finished beef into her small plate.

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

More embarrassing.

But none of that mattered. What mattered was this: she had just heard Choi Jin-ri call Baek Si-on “Oppa.” Bare. No name attached.

In Korea, a girl calling a boy a bare, prefix-free “Oppa” happens in exactly three circumstances.

Family. Childhood friend.

Fan.

Or something in the romantic-current column.

If the situation had been reversed — if someone had said this to her — Choi Jin-ri would now be spiraling, probing in small increments, and winding herself into a full emotional pretzel by the second side dish.

Lee Ji-eun was not Choi Jin-ri.

She asked. Directly.

“Peach.”

“Mm?”

Choi Jin-ri’s tongs paused mid-air.

Lee Ji-eun bumped her shoulder, and dropped her voice:

“What did you just call him?”

“Huh? Nothing…”

Choi Jin-ri went red to the ears, denied it at top speed — and kept sneaking glances at Baek Si-on to see if he’d reacted.

Lee Ji-eun looked once, and understood everything.

To summarize: the peach has fully defected. The man is still “observing.”

Observing.

She turned the word over in her head. He would, wouldn’t he. That type never steps forward early. What he wants is not someone who looks up at him.

He wants someone who can keep pace. Who can sit through a night rewriting lyrics. Who, when he says “this chorus is wrong,” answers “then we rewrite it.”

Lee Ji-eun bit into a piece of beef.

Good beef.

The conclusion was obvious. She simply couldn’t be bothered to say it out loud yet.

…

Elsewhere.

The seven of BTS sat at the deepest table. Kim Nam-joon had just picked up his glass, ready to lead the members over and toast Baek Si-on.

Tonight meant something: two songs in front of sixty thousand, and a personal introduction from Baek Si-on himself. The toast was mandatory.

But the moment he stood, he saw Red Velvet’s four girls also rising, glasses in hand, on their manager’s cue.

Kim Nam-joon paused. Then waved his members back into their chairs.

“Hold up.”

Jeon Jung-kook blinked.

“Hyung, we’re not going?”

“Ladies first.”

Kim Nam-joon said it like a weather report. Beside him, Min Yoon-gi set his glass back down and nodded.

“Sound logic.”

Jung Ho-seok looked across the room and sighed, quietly:

“Irene got a lot of cameras tonight, huh.”

Park Ji-min piled on instantly:

“She looked incredible on the big screen.”

Kim Tae-hyung nodded with total sincerity.

“Like an advertisement.”

Kim Seok-jin, hearing all this, touched his own face.

“And us?”

Min Yoon-gi looked at him.

“You also look like an advertisement.”

Kim Seok-jin was satisfied.

“For what, though?”

Min Yoon-gi looked down and picked up a slice of pork with his chopsticks.

“Kimchi.”

Kim Seok-jin: “……”

The youngest two nearly lost it, and forcibly did not — the toast run was still coming, and one does not walk into a senior’s toast line wearing a face full of crime.

Choi Jin-ri grilled.

Lee Ji-eun tasted.

Baek Si-on ate.

the coat, at dinner · table duty: grill December 18 · near midnight · hanwoo territory

The coat, in captivity The long black down coat, zipped to the chin over her own clothes, sleeves devouring her hands to the fingertips — worn indoors, over charcoal, in a room full of industry colleagues. Her private styling record is nationally admired; tonight’s silhouette is a kid wearing a parent to dinner. She knows exactly what it looks like. That is the entire reason she is wearing it.
The duty Tongs in both fists, flipping beef at the table where the man of the year is eating. By Korean table law, grill duty belongs to the youngest present; every way you rotate that roster, it lands on Baek Eun-ah. It does not land on an eonni with a possessive streak and a plan. The coat is not warm here. It is a flag.

Grill duty, claimed against all convention.

One table, three job titles, zero vacancies — and the grill job, against national custom, held by the person with the most to prove.

…

When Bae Joo-hyun led Kang Seul-gi, Son Seung-wan, and Park Soo-young over to Baek Si-on’s table, her first impression was that the atmosphere was… odd.

At one end, three managers traded industry gossip. Jung Han-teul was holding forth on some music-show format overhaul; Baek Eun-ah took notes while listening; Choi Jin-ri’s manager supplied occasional seasoning — “that producer’s temper really is terrible.”

At the other end, three idols were eating.

Or more precisely — see above. The division of labor inspired a kind of awe.

Bae Joo-hyun stood there with her glass, unsure whether interrupting was survivable.

Because Baek Si-on wasn’t even looking up.

He looked up only when the meat stopped arriving.

“Sunbaenim.”

Bae Joo-hyun raised her glass quickly, head dipping.

“Thank you so much for tonight.”

“Thank you for giving us a stage like that.”

The three younger members bowed in a row.

“Thank you, sunbaenim.”

Baek Si-on didn’t pick up his glass. His eyes went, first, to Bae Joo-hyun’s feet.

“Ankle’s healed?”

She blinked, then nodded.

“Healed.”

“Then no alcohol either.”

“Sorry?”

Baek Si-on said:

“I just told everyone — no rules tonight.”

“Which includes no forcing yourself to drink.”

Bae Joo-hyun had not expected that. The standard senior move at this exact beat is a warm “you worked hard — have a drink with me,” at which point a rookie drinks with her heart in her throat. He had skipped the script entirely and simply closed the alcohol road.

“Then — with a drink, a soft drink. May I toast you?”

At the gossip end of the table, Baek Eun-ah’s head whipped around.

This double-standard man! Ten minutes ago he was glaring at her with his whole face about the tongs — and now that a pretty junior was toasting, it was “no rules tonight”?!

Her reason pointed out, quietly, that grilling and toasting were not the same category. Her feelings overruled the objection: she was, formally, a victim of dual workplace-and-family oppression.

Choi Jin-ri caught Baek Eun-ah’s expression and lost the fight with a laugh.

And that laugh seemed to return something to Baek Si-on’s queue. He looked back at Bae Joo-hyun.

“If you’re toasting anyone — Sulli-sunbaenim first. She’s the one who put your name forward.”

“Then the broadcast crew. Those camera slots tonight didn’t grow out of your head.”

“And the medical team. The fact that you can jump around a stage is their achievement.”

“……”

Bae Joo-hyun pivoted fast, swinging her freshly-soft-drinked glass toward Choi Jin-ri — who got her question in first:

“Your foot — how did it happen?”

The voice was soft. No interrogation in it. No senior-to-junior altitude either.

Bae Joo-hyun stalled anyway — she had not expected the concern to come before the thanks.

“I rolled it during practice. Nothing serious.”

She bowed her head quickly.

“Thank you for asking.”

Choi Jin-ri appeared not to register the layer of politeness and distance in it at all. She just nodded to herself.

“Be careful from now on.”

“Yes, sunbaenim.”

Lee Ji-eun, listening, sighed internally.

This peach, honestly…

You deliver concern that soft, and expect the kid to decode it? Not a chance.

She put down her chopsticks and added the missing layer:

“What Peach means is: don’t try so hard to prove yourself in practice. The harder you want to be seen, the easier it is to forget the floor under you.”

It sounded like care.

Chewed slowly, it also sounded like: you got hurt because your head wasn’t on the practice.

Bae Joo-hyun didn’t dare push back — because it was true.

“Yes, IU-sunbaenim. We’ll keep it in mind.”

The three younger members bowed again.

“We’ll remember it.”

Bae Joo-hyun’s face stayed perfectly regulation.

Inside, a small confusion was filing itself: how does Sulli-sunbaenim — that gentle — get along with IU-sunbaenim, whose sentences carry knives?

None of it reached her face. She held her soft drink, finished every owed sentence of thanks in proper order.

“Really — thank you for the recommendation, Sulli-sunbaenim.”

Choi Jin-ri smiled.

“It was you who performed well.”

Lee Ji-eun heard that, looked down, and took a slice of beef brisket for herself.

Fine. A peach is a peach.

Bae Joo-hyun took her three girls off to toast the broadcast crew and the medical team, exactly as instructed.

The moment the four were gone, Lee Ji-eun spoke, slow as syrup:

“You’re a very thorough senior, aren’t you.”

“Asking after her ankle. Banning her from alcohol. Even the manners get a walkthrough.”

Baek Eun-ah looked down at her water. She recognized the temperature of that sentence. So did Jung Han-teul, who slid his chair one silent notch sideways.

Baek Si-on didn’t take the bait. He said, evenly:

“Screenwriter Park Ji-eun.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

She understood immediately.

On the subject of thorough: the man had dropped the role of Cindy in Producer directly at her feet — an account somewhat heavier than one soft drink tonight.

The problem: acknowledging that meant acknowledging she had also been looked after. Thoroughly.

Lee Ji-eun pursed her lips.

Is he planning to leverage that for the rest of my natural life?

Choi Jin-ri, who had followed approximately none of that, looked left and right.

“What about screenwriter Park Ji-eun?”

She had not been issued the cipher key. Baek Si-on looked down at the grill.

“Meat needs flipping.”

“Oh.”

Choi Jin-ri grabbed the tongs and turned the beef over piece by piece. Fat dripped and hit the pan, spitting.

Her thoughts, meanwhile, were circling. There was a story between these two that she didn’t know. She wasn’t sure she was entitled to ask. If it ran anywhere near the depth of that MV kiss — her entire day’s good mood would evaporate on contact. If it was just ordinary professional help, then she didn’t need to know.

In the end, she changed the subject herself:

“By the way — how did Irene hurt her foot?”

“She lost a fight with high heels.”

That description was, somehow, extremely specific.

Choi Jin-ri glanced at Lee Ji-eun before she could stop herself.

Lee Ji-eun: “?”

“Peach. Are you thinking something unfriendly about heights?”

“I’m not.”

Lee Ji-eun huffed.

She was, in fact, perfectly satisfied with her own height. Three centimeters taller and she’d stop being cute; three shorter and she’d be pocket-sized. Current specification: optimal.

Choi Jin-ri tried again:

“Getting hurt at rehearsal must have been scary?”

Baek Si-on picked up a finished slice and set it in his bowl.

“A bit.”

“And then?”

“Hospital.”

“Oh.”

She didn’t push further. But a shape was forming in the back of her mind — something had happened in that story. Otherwise Bae Joo-hyun would not have been looking at Baek Si-on with that un-definable eyes.

But some questions, asked, produce answers you didn’t want; unasked, they at least leave room. What she needed to know was already known: tonight, Baek Si-on sat across from her, eating the meat she grilled.

That was enough. The smoke and noise of the table folded itself over every delicate feeling in the room.

…

The dinner broke up around midnight.

Smoke climbed out of the vent over the door and turned to white fog the instant it met the December night. Inside, the room was nearly empty; the grills had gone cold, beer bottles and soft-drink cups were stacked in the middle of tables, and waitstaff bent over the wreckage.

The staff had left first — most of them had call times tomorrow. The junior labels came next: Red Velvet’s and BTS’s vans pulling out of the lot one after the other.

A handful of people stood at the door finishing the last conversations.

Lee Ji-eun came out to find Baek Si-on seeing people off.

“Get home safe.”

“Careful on the road.”

She watched for a few seconds. Then walked over and planted herself beside him.

“Hey.”

Baek Si-on turned.

“Mm?”

“Hearing about Irene’s ankle tonight reminded me of something.”

Her expression was serious now. Dead serious.

“In the Producer script, Cindy has an ankle-injury scene.”

She tilted her head slightly.

“When an injured person gets helped up — where does the weight go?”

“You’re asking me?”

“You have field experience, don’t you?”

Baek Si-on didn’t answer. He had already begun to sense the shape of what was coming.

And indeed.

Lee Ji-eun’s feet went sideways — her whole body tipping like a reed in wind.

“Aigo~”

She arrived against his chest, face tipping up, eyes going up at him from below.

“Like this? Does it work like this?”

The excuse was terrible.

But a good excuse doesn’t need to be good. It needs to be sudden.

Baek Si-on looked down at the upturned face.

“Like an insurance scam.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

Does this man understand NOTHING about helping an actress research a role?!

“Ahem!”

Choi Jin-ri had, in fact, seen it from the door.

She had seen Lee Ji-eun tip into Baek Si-on’s arms. She had seen him look down at her. For one clean moment, the sourness was unmistakable.

And in the next second, she did not stand in place waiting for him to manage the situation.

She covered the distance in three quick steps and took Lee Ji-eun off his hands with total naturalness.

“Eonni, do you need a scene partner for that?”

She hooked Lee Ji-eun’s arm through hers, voice soft as syrup.

“I can help. It’s more convenient between girls, anyway.”

Lee Ji-eun looked at her.

The peach was smiling much too sweetly. Sweet enough that a refusal here would retroactively make the ankle look premeditated.

She ran the numbers for half a second.

Then nodded.

“Sure.”

Choi Jin-ri steered her toward the car immediately.

“Then let’s rehearse properly in the car.”

Baek Si-on watched the two of them go, and offered the backs of their heads one sentence:

“Don’t actually fall.”

Both turned around at the same time.

“We know.”

Perfectly synchronized, too.

Jung Han-teul stood nearby, watching his artist get escorted away by Choi Jin-ri, wearing the expression of a man doing advanced accounting.

He had to admit: the ankle stunt had been artistically executed.

The landing needed work.

If this were a drama, you’d have cut to slow motion there. If it were a variety show, the captions would have read HUNT FAILED.

Unfortunately, it was reality.

And in reality, the hunter got carried off by the peach.

…

The car was quiet.

Jung Han-teul drove. Choi Jin-ri and Lee Ji-eun sat in the back. Outside the windows, Seoul’s nightlights went by, one light at a time.

About one stop’s worth of silence later, Lee Ji-eun spoke.

“Peach.”

“Mm?”

“You’ve gotten good at intercepting passes.”

Choi Jin-ri blinked, the picture of innocence.

“Eonni, whatever do you mean?”

“Drop it.”

Choi Jin-ri laughed then.

“I just think — Oppa flies to America tomorrow. He should sleep early tonight, that’s all.”

Lee Ji-eun’s gaze came back in from the window and settled on her face.

“The old you didn’t do this.”

Choi Jin-ri looked down at the down coat in her arms.

“The old me didn’t have something I wanted this much to keep.”

The car went quiet for a beat.

Lee Ji-eun looked at her — and suddenly smiled.

Good.

The peach had ripened.

And it had started growing thorns.

Chapter Eight

The Butterfly and the Mire

April Yeonnam-dong, back toward the studio The walk back after the barbecue

Coming out of the barbecue joint, Si-on and Han-teuk walked back along the little alleys of Yeonnam-dong. The night wind carried the charcoal smell from the shop. A small izakaya glowed warm yellow light, someone inside was playing a drinking game, voices muffled through the glass.

They reached the studio building at exactly the right moment.

The building door swung open from the inside. Eun-ah came out first, Choi Jin-ri trailing behind her, mask back on, hood pulled up — once more a person wrapped tight around herself.

Eun-ah saw Si-on and stopped.

“How’d it go?”

Eun-ah’s expression was complicated. Not disappointment, not joy. The look of someone who’d guessed it all along and still felt a little bitter about it.

“Dad said unnie is too pretty.”

Si-on didn’t answer. He looked at Choi Jin-ri. Above the mask, those eyes were watching the ground, expressionless. But her whole posture was turned inward, shoulders curled forward — instinct to make herself smaller.

“Miss Jin-ri.”

Eun-ah looked up. Choi Jin-ri looked up too. Si-on wasn’t looking at Eun-ah. He was looking straight at Jin-ri.

“Do you want to act?”

Choi Jin-ri’s eyes moved.

“I…”

“Yes. Or no.”

Si-on gave her no room to hedge. This question only needed a one-word answer.

Choi Jin-ri stood there, the streetlight above her throwing a small shadow over the top of her mask. Her fingers curled into her sleeve.

“Yes.”

The voice was soft, but clear.

Si-on nodded.

“Come in.”

Eun-ah blinked, looked from Jin-ri to the back of her cousin already holding the building door open, and quickly pulled Jin-ri along behind him. Han-teuk stood where he was, looked down at the takeaway bag still in his hand, and silently fell into step at the rear. His step count for today had already passed twenty thousand.

April Night Baek Jeong-hun's editing studio A director at the end of a long argument

When the studio door pushed open, Baek Jeong-hun was leaning back in his chair smoking. Two more butts stood in the ashtray; a third cigarette was pinched between his fingers. The storyboard was open to the “2002” page, covered in newly pencilled notes.

Clearly, he’d been chewing on the change-of-era problem this whole while.

At the sound of the door, he looked up. He thought Eun-ah had come back for something she’d forgotten.

Instead, four people filed in, packing his small studio like a sardine tin.

“Si-on?”

Si-on walked to the desk and sat half on its edge, one leg planted on the floor, the other dangling and swinging.

“Samchon, let me do some math for you.”

Baek Jeong-hun looked at his nephew’s expression.

There it is. Again.

“You said you’d go back to Chung-Ang and do an open audition, pick an unknown. Fine. Pull one suitable face out of several hundred acting students — preliminary, second round, interview, screen test — that’s two to three weeks minimum.”

He tapped the desk.

“When does shooting start?”

Baek Jeong-hun didn’t answer. His hand stopped mid-air, the cigarette holding a long, trembling column of ash.

“Second: money.”

“You pick an unknown, right face, but she’s never acted. That means for every scene you teach her from zero. Blocking, positioning, how to speak in front of a camera without sounding like reciting a textbook. Samchon, you’re shooting on film, not digital. Film is priced by the foot. One unknown, twenty takes per scene — can you afford to burn that?”

Baek Jeong-hun’s ash finally fell, silently scattering across the storyboard.

“And her.”

Si-on tilted his chin toward Jin-ri.

“Child-actor background. Done TV, done film. Camera sense. Fundamentals way more solid than the rookies at school who only know theory. You save time. You save money.”

Baek Jeong-hun leaned back and stared at the ceiling a few seconds. Then he brought his gaze down, resting it on Si-on’s face. He stubbed out the cigarette and leaned forward a little.

“I’m not saying she’s bad. I’m saying she’s too pretty. Put her in that setting and it doesn’t ring true.”

Yeon-hui was a high-school girl living in a slum, beaten daily by her father and her brother. Choi Jin-ri’s face — luminous even bare-faced, delicate as a porcelain doll — put against that ruined backdrop was jarring. She didn’t look poor. She didn’t look like someone who’d be beaten. How was an audience supposed to believe it?

But this was exactly what Si-on had been waiting for.

“Flip it, Samchon.”

He stood off the desk edge and took a step forward.

“If Yeon-hui were ordinary-looking, she gets beaten, and the audience thinks — yeah, that’s the bottom rung, life’s like that. Some sympathy, but no real punch.”

“But what if Yeon-hui is beautiful?”

“Tear something beautiful to shreds right in front of them, trample it into the mud, and the audience aches for her because of the beauty. The audience burns with outrage because of the cruelty.”

“The second version lands ten times harder than the first.”

Baek Jeong-hun’s fingers slowly unclasped. As a director, his mind ran the images on its own. A high-school girl beautiful enough to be impossible to look away from, blood at the corner of her mouth, wearing a filthy uniform, weeping numbly in a dim alley.

The image… was brilliant.

Still, he hesitated. Theory was theory. When it came to actually performing, could this girl set down her idol persona?

His gaze drifted slowly to Choi Jin-ri.

Choi Jin-ri stood at the door, unmoving, mask still on. But her eyes were watching, quietly, in this direction.

Nobody in the room spoke. Eun-ah clutched her sleeve, gaze jumping back and forth between her father and Jin-ri. Han-teuk leaned against the far doorframe, holding his breath.

Seeing this, Si-on turned to Jin-ri.

“Take the mask off.”

Choi Jin-ri reached up and pulled the mask down.

“You just got beaten by your brother and ran out of the house. You’re crouching at the mouth of an alley, holding five thousand won, wondering whether to buy a bottle of soju at the convenience store.”

“Go.”

No lines. No prep time.

Yeon-hui at the mouth of the alley

Action

Baek Jeong-hun sets the slate. The room closes its mouth.

Choi Jin-ri stands still. Five seconds pass.

Then her eyes change. Not a studied shift. Something is swapped out from the inside — the same eyes, but the light is gone.

Shoulders sink, inch by inch, as if bearing something invisible and carrying it a long time until it grew too heavy to hold. She never quite lets it drop. Just sags.

Knees bend, pause. The hesitation is either doubt, or the lack of strength to commit. Then the rest of her follows down, and all of her folds into a small shape, crouched on the studio floor.

She looks down at her clenched fist.

After a few seconds, her fingers go loose. Open, one by one, palm up, empty. She looks at that empty hand.

Then she buries her head in her knees.

Her shoulders don’t shake. No sound. She just sits there — gone quiet, like a piece of paper crumpled up and impossible to smooth out again.

A dozen seconds pass before she rises. The room has not made a sound.

A dozen seconds later, Choi Jin-ri stood up.

The moment she rose, Yeon-hui left, and Jin-ri came back. She looked down and wiped the bridge of her nose with her sleeve, a little embarrassed.

In Baek Jeong-hun’s head · the monitor

Theory is theory. Performance is the thing.

Could she set down the idol persona and be a girl the audience believes?

“Too pretty” is no longer the problem.

Baek Jeong-hun was silent a long while — long enough that Eun-ah thought he was about to say “too pretty” again. Then he spoke.

“Miss Jin-ri.”

Choi Jin-ri looked at him.

“You available?”

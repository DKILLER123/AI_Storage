December 21. A little after five in the afternoon.

Seoul. Yongsan. The SKT T1 practice room.

Winter dark falls early, and the window had already gone cold-blue — but inside, the practice room ran at full daylight brightness. Keyboards, mice, chair casters rolling over the floor, teammates calling positions in low voices.

The winter of 2014 had not been a calm season for Korean League of Legends. Samsung White had taken the world championship, Riot had forced the one-team-per-organization rule down everyone’s throats, and Korea’s best players had hemorrhaged overseas. The LPL had shown up with money — clubs, streaming platforms, sponsors, all of it — with offers so large that many players learned, for the first time, that the most valuable years of a career could be cashed out abroad.

Into that exodus, Lee Sang-hyeok had taken a pay cut to stay at SKT.

It sounds plain in one sentence. In that room, in that winter, it was not plain at all. Loyalty doesn’t cover groceries, and trophies don’t service a family’s loans.

Plenty of people called him foolish for it. Others said he was the team’s banner and had no choice. Lee Sang-hyeok himself said nothing at all. He just kept practicing, kept queuing, kept sitting in front of the screen — folding all of it into last-hitting, positioning, ability timing, and the small margins of lane.

Right now, he sat at his monitor in black-rimmed glasses, face at factory settings, staring down a clock that had ticked past twenty-seven minutes.

Twenty-seven minutes is enough to boil a pot of ramyeon, eat it, wash the pot, and scrub the stove.

That is Korean solo-queue Challenger at work. The top of the ladder is too thin, the matchmaking pool too small — sometimes the queue outlasts the game.

Lee Sang-hyeok leaned back, right hand clicking away inside a whack-a-mole-style reaction trainer, keeping the fingers warm.

But the queue was pushing half an hour, and the chat would not stop. He thought for a moment, opened a browser, typed YouTube.

The picture brightened slowly. Old-film grey-white. Baek Si-on sat at a big window with a guitar, watching the skyline.

The camera pulled back, room by room. The apartment was full of two people’s living: Polaroids on the walls, the cabinets, the mirror frame. Video-Baek Si-on walked the living room, the kitchen, the hallway. He reached the bed, lay down, one hand across his forehead, eyes closed. An old ceiling fan turned above him.

The camera shot straight down from the ceiling. The blade swept across, and through the gaps between blades came his face. The blade kept turning. The next blade crossed the lens and blocked the frame.

And when the frame cleared —

Same bed. Same angle.

But next to Baek Si-on there was now a second person.

Lee Ji-eun, lying on her stomach beside him, chin propped on both hands, feet up in the air behind her, watching him without blinking.

Grey became color.

Loneliness became company.

Like a memory being turned, gently, by a ceiling fan.

Lee Ji-eun was watching too.

She sat on her own sofa, knees pulled up, the full short film playing on her television. Third viewing.

Not — obviously — because the kissing scene was beautifully shot.

She had only replayed that part seven or eight times, that was all.

The real reason was that the praise had changed key. It wasn’t “IU is so cute” anymore. It wasn’t “IU’s voice is as lovely as ever” either.

It was this:

Those comments she read with real attention. More attention than any praise of her singing, honestly.

Because singing was her strength. Acting was not.

She had always known the public kept her in a fixed frame. And this time, for once, the audience wasn’t praising her for being cute. They were praising her for playing someone not easily loved.

For her, that mattered more than “pretty,” and more than “well-matched.”

Lee Ji-eun leaned back into the sofa and watched her own face go cold on screen — and felt something shift about Cindy, in Producer.

Cindy was also not a character anyone was required to like at first sight. She had to make the audience hate her — and be unable to look away.

And it made her recompute the weight of what Baek Si-on had done, recommending screenwriter Park Ji-eun to her. It was heavier than she’d priced it. He hadn’t just handed her a role. He’d known what she needed before she did, laid the road, and left it there for her to walk at her own pace.

This person.

Genuinely infuriating.

Infuriating to the point that sometimes she couldn’t tell whether she was competing with Choi Jin-ri for a man — or chasing someone who kept making her better.

…

Choi Jin-ri, by contrast, was not having a refined afternoon.

She had not planned to watch the MV.

Truly not.

But the entire internet was one big related-content feed. Forums scrolling it. Portals pushing it. YouTube’s front page wearing it. Even the fashion bloggers she followed had posted:

“Lee Ji-eun’s styling and performance in this are exactly right for her.”

Choi Jin-ri stared at that sentence for a long, long time.

Then she surrendered.

She fished Blin out of the pile of down coats, installed the cat on her lap, and squared them both toward the television.

“We’re watching it together.”

Blin, in the little hoodie, four paws tucked into the blanket, wore the expression of a party that wished to be excused from human emotional litigation.

Choi Jin-ri conducted the pre-show briefing. For herself. And for the cat.

“Blin. This is work.”

“Actors kissing is work.”

“Understood?”

Blin yawned.

Choi Jin-ri took that as ratification.

The video played. For the first stretch she remained a composed professional:

“The lighting is very professional too.”

“……”

And then the fan blade turned, grey became color — and Lee Ji-eun appeared. Lying next to him, chin in hands, feet in the air, eyes shining at him like a lit window.

“That’s not fair…”

Choi Jin-ri looked down at Blin.

Ji-eun eonni sings, writes, and acts this well on top of it. And her? Apparently still in training for: how to be an actress, how not to be steered by her feelings, how to watch a screen without switching it off mid-frame.

Blin licked a paw.

Choi Jin-ri chose to understand the gesture as “no, it really isn’t fair.”

The film rolled on. The couple’s days got sweeter: brushing teeth together. Fighting over a cup in the kitchen. Curling up on the sofa for a movie. Lee Ji-eun reached for the remote in Baek Si-on’s hand — and he caught her wrist without even looking up.

It was shot with unbearable naturalness. Natural enough that Choi Jin-ri filed a formal grievance against the director. How dare it be that natural. Is that even legal?

One second before the kiss scene —

Choi Jin-ri’s hand shot out and covered Blin’s eyes.

“You’re too young for this.”

Blin squirmed under the palm.

Her other hand came up and covered her own eyes.

“So am I.”

Five seconds passed. She lowered her hands slowly. Blin extracted itself from under her palm and stared up at her, deeply confused — visibly filing the question of why this human had chosen the single most important frame to block its view.

The MV ended.

Choi Jin-ri sat on the sofa and said nothing for a long time.

It had to be admitted. The film was very good.

Good enough that even her jealousy felt under-supported — because none of it was flirtation. It was work. Camera, grade, story, performance: every gear professional. It’s just that after watching it, a person could no longer fully tell where the acting stopped.

She looked down at Blin. The cat sat on her lap, head tilted, green eyes fixed on her face without blinking.

“Why do you think he didn’t tell me the MV was coming out today?”

Blin did not respond.

Choi Jin-ri thought for a moment, then answered for the cat herself.

“Oh — he probably assumed I knew.”

She reached out and tapped Blin’s little head, gently.

“Don’t grow up to be like him.”

Blin recoiled from the tap, deeply offended.

Choi Jin-ri went quiet again. Then her voice dropped, all of a sudden.

“But he didn’t wipe it off.”

Blin looked up at her.

Choi Jin-ri touched the corner of her own mouth — as if confirming the corridor from that day was still on file somewhere.

“Right?”

Blin, a consummate professional, delivered one supportive meow.

“Meow.”

Choi Jin-ri’s eyes slowly bent into crescents.

“You think so too?”

Blin understood nothing, obviously. It yawned and pushed its bald little head into her palm again, on principle — the principle being that her hand was the warmest object in the jurisdiction.

And she decided that this, too, was a fine way to win.

The MV was work. But Blin was a gift he’d carried over himself. He hadn’t told her the MV was coming — but he’d remembered a cat mentioned once at an elevator, and he hadn’t wiped off the kiss.

She bent down and kissed the wrinkled little head.

“Mm.”

“Then for now, we’re winning by a little.”

Evening.

Seoul, Gangseo district, Deungchon-dong — the open broadcast hall.

A year-end stage rehearsal is always a small disaster wearing lanyards. Rookie boy groups and girl groups from every company, packed onto one floor to rehearse a joint number of ‘Moves Like Jagger’.

The blocking was complicated. The beats had to unify. Boy-group and girl-group positions crossed through each other. Cameras were still being placed; the director kept shouting adjustments.

The managers stood along both walls of the hall with their arms crossed — a row of human traffic barriers. Some watched their phones. Some watched to make sure their kids weren’t getting shoved to the edge of the frame. Some did silent arithmetic: in a five-minute joint stage, how many seconds of screen time does my artist actually get?

“Again! From the top!”

The SBS stage manager bellowed through a megaphone.

Everyone reset. Nobody complained. At least nobody complained out loud. Year-end stage allocations run on these exact days — who cooperates, who keeps attitude, who doesn’t give the director headaches. The staff see all of it and write it all down.

Rookies have no entitlement budget.

The music restarted.

Bae Joo-hyun stood second row, right side. On the chorus she had to cut diagonally from the right into center, cross-switch with a boy-group member, and land her mark on the third beat.

She had walked that path nine times now.

Every time, careful about how she landed.

She was in low practice heels today. The stylist had wanted her in something higher; she’d shaken her head and refused.

The reason was simple. Someone had told her, very clearly, that she doesn’t need seven centimeters.

the low heels, on purpose · Deungchon-dong open hall December 21 · evening · twelve run-throughs · zero altitude dependency

The shoes Low block practice heels, nude, strapped, broken in — the opposite of the pair that started this story. She turned down the stylist’s seven-centimeter upgrade without drama and without explanation, because the explanation already lives somewhere in her posture: she has been told, by the exact person whose attention she values, that the face is enough.
The uniform Black long-sleeve practice top, dark joggers, hair tied back off her face. Rookie rehearsal armor — no stage lights to hide behind, no close-up lens to flatter. Just a hall full of managers counting seconds, and a leader who has decided that landing clean on beat three is the whole job.

Someone said the face is enough. She built her whole stance on it.

Twelve run-throughs. Nine of the same diagonal. And a shoe choice that is, functionally, a keepsake.

…

After the twelfth run-through, the stage manager finally called a break.

Bae Joo-hyun leaned against the side wall, unscrewed a bottle of water, drank twice.

A staffer carrying a coil of audio cable passed her — and stopped mid-step.

“You’re Irene, right? From the big screen at Baek Si-on’s concert?”

Bae Joo-hyun blinked.

“Ne — yes.”

“My girlfriend watched the rebroadcast yesterday and sent me, like, five screenshots of you.”

Then he remembered something, and added:

“She asked me whether the SBS year-end stage will have you on camera.”

He laughed at himself.

“I told her I move cables, I don’t direct cameras.”

And he walked off with his coil.

Bae Joo-hyun stood there with the water bottle and took a while to actually process it.

This was not fan praise. This was not a company-planted feedback report. This was an ordinary SBS staffer recognizing her, in the gap between two cable runs, because his girlfriend had screenshotted her.

Which meant the camera time Baek Si-on gave her had seeped all the way down into ordinary people’s camera rolls.

And suddenly she wanted, very badly, to tell him about it.

Not the way she did at the thank-you dinner — soft drink held properly, sentences recited in regulation order. Thank you for giving us this stage.

Something plainer. More private. Something like:

“Sunbaenim. Someone recognized me today.”

And:

“Music Bank contacted us.”

And maybe, if she were brave:

“Did I hold up my end of the camera time you gave me?”

Bae Joo-hyun looked down at the bottle in her hands — and realized these thoughts, sincere as they sounded, died at step one of execution.

Because she did not have Baek Si-on’s contact information.

More practically still:

As a rookie girl-group idol barely out of debut, she did not even own a phone.

Well then…

Bae Joo-hyun sighed quietly against the wall.

Before she could work up the nerve to ask Baek Si-on-sunbaenim for his contact information, she would first need to obtain a communication device of her very own.

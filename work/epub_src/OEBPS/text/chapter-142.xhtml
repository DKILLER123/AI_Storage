Chapter One Hundred Forty Two

The Pledge from Half the Republic

For all that heavy good news, the party most stimulated was not the retail investors.

It was the other listed brands.

Retail investors could still comfort themselves with “I didn’t buy because I’m rational.”

The brands had no such refuge.

All they could do was watch Hyundai Motor’s and SK Telecom’s market-cap curves bend upward, glance at their own “global marketing plan” still living inside a pitch deck, and silently arrive at the same sentence.

It’s over. The Baek Si-on bus left the station, and everyone else is already on it.

November 9 · 1 p.m. Twenty minutes into a nap that is not going well Jeonju · the set · the rear cabin of the high-roof Starex Three takes survived this morning, half a lunch survived this afternoon, and nobody scheduled the visitors who just drove in.

At one in the afternoon, on the Jeonju set, Baek Si-on lay flat in the rear cabin of the high-roof Starex, asleep.

Strictly speaking, he hovered between sleep and shutdown.

The morning had taken three takes out of him — one in which Song Kang-ho tore into him, one in which Lee Joon-ik asked for it “a little more shattered”, and one spent kneeling in front of the great hall until his knees ached.

He had managed half his lunch, and had been lying down for less than twenty minutes.

The van door was tapped, softly.

Baek Eun-ah put her head in, voice pressed low.

“Oppa.”

Baek Si-on kept his eyes closed.

“Mm.”

“Hyundai Motor’s Vice Chairman Chung Eui-sun is here.”

His brain took two full seconds to move that sentence out of the dream where Yeongjo had come down to lecture him all over again, and back into reality.

He opened his eyes.

“Who?”

“Vice Chairman Chung Eui-sun.”

Baek Si-on sat up.

Park Ji-hun instantly passed over the damp towel waiting beside him.

Baek Si-on wiped his face and straightened the collar of his pale moon-white casual shirt — and by then footsteps were already sounding outside the door.

The next second, two knocks from outside.

Chung Eui-sun bent slightly and stepped up into the cabin.

Behind him came a man in a deep grey suit.

“Si-on-ah — hope we’re not interrupting your rest?”

That “Si-on-ah” came out very naturally.

So naturally that Baek Eun-ah’s eyelid twitched.

Sure enough. A chaebol’s familiarity always has a price.

“Not at all. Please, sit.”

Baek Eun-ah folded the little table away and swung the seats into reception mode, while Park Ji-hun, reading the room, tactfully stepped back down out of the van.

Four people left in the cabin.

Chung Eui-sun looked around it first.

“How’s the van?”

Baek Si-on considered.

“Thanks to you, I’ve slept on the road these two days better than in a hotel.”

Chung Eui-sun laughed.

“Then it was delivered right.”

Finished laughing, he turned to introduce the man beside him:

“This is my cousin, Chung Ji-sun.”

Baek Eun-ah blinked.

Chung Ji-sun.

The name rang a bell.

She flipped through her mental files for two seconds.

Ah. The chairman of the Hyundai Department Store Group!

Baek Si-on had surfaced the rest of the way too, and had obviously made the same connection.

He put out his hand.

“The honour is mine, Chairman Chung.”

Chung Ji-sun took his hand, smiling.

“Actor Baek — the honour should be mine. Venice Volpi Cup, Billboard champion, and now the Silver Crown Cultural Medal. These past few days, you are the person our family’s dinner table has talked about most.”

“You flatter me.”

“Not flattery. You are no longer an entertainer in the ordinary sense. You are someone who can drag a brand into a national public cultural event.”

The moment that line landed, the cabin went quiet for an instant.

Very capitalist.

Also very accurate.

Baek Si-on leaned back into the seat.

“Chairman-nim has come all this way in person. What can I do for you?”

Chung Eui-sun took over the topic:

“Hyundai Department Store is preparing to enter the duty-free business. They want you as their global director.”

A global director is on a different scale from an ambassador.

An ambassador shoots commercials, shows up at events, collects an endorsement fee.

A global director goes into the bid book. Goes into brand strategy. Appears in overseas campaign materials. Could even end up written into the thick stack of documents that goes in front of the Korea Customs Service.

Two things on entirely different dimensions.

Baek Si-on understood the shape of it well enough.

Korea’s duty-free market was dominated by Lotte and Shilla.

Between the two of them, they had eaten nearly all of the downtown duty-free traffic that mattered.

Lotte ran on scale. Shilla ran on Samsung lineage and high-end service.

And these last few years, Chinese tourists had been sweeping Seoul with suitcases and woven bags — the duty-free strips around Myeong-dong, Eulji-ro and Jangchung-dong could take in a single day what an ordinary department store took months to earn.

This was not ordinary retail.

This was a cash cow.

Fat margins.

And it pulled hotels, department stores, dining, airport logistics and brand licensing along behind it.

Precisely because Lotte and Shilla had monopolized things so thoroughly, the Blue House and the Korea Customs Service had lately let it be known that new downtown duty-free licences would be opened up in Seoul.

Practically the entire chaebol circle had begun moving at the same time.

But a licence was not something money alone could buy.

The official bidding criteria, beyond capital strength, an operating plan and brand-recruitment power, also asked whether you could drive tourism, raise GDP, create jobs, and raise Korea’s cultural influence.

To put it plainly:

You had to prove that taking the licence didn’t just mean you made money.

It meant more foreign tourists coming to Korea, spending more, and learning more about Korea.

Why had Lotte Duty Free been able to keep Shilla pressed underneath it for so long?

Because they understood hallyu economics better than anyone.

Lee Min-ho, EXO, Jang Keun-suk, Super Junior, 2PM, Park Shin-hye — the hottest roster of hallyu stars had been signed up by Lotte practically in one bite.

Chinese tourists, Southeast Asian tourists, Japanese tourists — whichever fandom came to Korea, who didn’t drop by Lotte while they were at it?

And the thing the duty-free business feared most was homogeneity.

Everybody carried Chanel.

Everybody fought over Hermès.

Sulwhasoo, Laneige, Innisfree — it wasn’t as if only you could sell them.

If the goods were the same, what brought a tourist through your door?

Discounts?

Lotte and Shilla did not fear a price war.

Location?

A newcomer was not guaranteed the best address.

Which left exactly one door: the cultural entrance.

And the cultural entrance had exactly one answer — Baek Si-on.

To consumers, the achievements behind him were a spectacle. To a duty-free bid book, they were an imperial travel pass — the stamp that got you through customs.

Hanwha was looking.

SK was looking too.

Only, SK had not yet decided what to offer him. They already had the telecom endorsement, and yesterday’s helicopter had just handed them a windfall — but SK had exactly one duty-free arm to gamble with. Offer too much and it hurt; offer too little and they’d lose the man. So the group was still fighting itself in meeting rooms.

Hanwha had no history with him at all, and its offer sat quietly in Baek Eun-ah’s inbox.

Hyundai was different.

The department-store group and the motor group were two independent companies — but the leadership was, after all, one family.

So Hyundai Department Store’s Chung Ji-sun had not taken the long way around. He had simply called his cousin Chung Eui-sun in to act as the middleman.

The water beneath the tower gets drawn first.

Get the man in the van before anything else.

“The terms?”

Chung Ji-sun did not circle.

“If we win the licence, three percent of Hyundai Duty Free’s annual profit goes straight into your account, every year, as a dividend.”

Baek Eun-ah ran the arithmetic in her head.

Not revenue.

A share of profit.

A duty-free store, once it got running, was a cash machine. Even if licensing, fit-out, rent and operations squeezed the early numbers ugly, once the traffic stabilized, three percent a year was not a small figure.

Chung Ji-sun continued:

“And if the licence doesn’t come through this round, we would still like to engage Actor Baek as Hyundai Department Store’s brand ambassador, at one and a half billion won a year.”

“Two years minimum. Three preferred.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“That is a very sincere price.”

Chung Ji-sun smiled.

“Of course, our sincerity hardly stops there.”

He glanced at Chung Eui-sun, then back at Baek Si-on.

“If you sign with Hyundai Department Store, I can also help move the CJ CheilJedang and LG Electronics endorsements along for you.”

Baek Eun-ah lowered her head and sneaked a look at her inbox.

She hadn’t had a spare minute for the business mail all morning.

She filtered it now.

And sure enough.

CJ CheilJedang · brand partnerships desk

[Offer] bibigo — global ambassador · two years

₩4,000,000,000 · two years

Attached: the full global campaign deck. Status: unread until this second.

LG Electronics · home entertainment

[Offer] LG OLED TV — global ambassador · two years

₩3,500,000,000 · two years

Attached: the global campaign brief. Status: unread until this second.

Baek Eun-ah looked at the two invitations lying quietly in her inbox, then back up at Chung Ji-sun.

Fine.

Let him have his moment.

After all, Chung Ji-sun had come today carrying real sincerity.

Not the kind of sincerity that sends a brand-office section chief over with a quote sheet.

The kind where the chairman of Hyundai Department Store himself brings the vice chairman of Hyundai Motor along as a middleman and sits down inside a nanny van.

Baek Si-on raised an eyebrow, sounding genuinely interested.

“You seem very keen for me to have nothing to do with Samsung?”

Chung Eui-sun said nothing.

Chung Ji-sun was perfectly frank about it.

“Yes.”

So direct that Baek Eun-ah couldn’t help looking up at him.

Hyundai, LG, CJ, SK — in the chaebol world these four made up one very stable little alliance.

The original reason they had gathered was simple.

They all disliked Samsung.

But what had actually kept the alliance alive was business complementarity — nobody poached the other’s core rice bowl.

Samsung was another matter.

The Samsung group was too vast, too proud, and too overbearing.

It wasn’t that Samsung had friction with someone.

It was that Samsung had, at one point or another, offended practically everybody.

If Korean business had to vote for a “public enemy of all the people”, Samsung would win by a landslide.

Especially between Hyundai’s circle and Samsung, this had long since stopped being ordinary competition.

Old accounts stacked on new ones — the press releases called it industrial competition, and the dinner table called it a blood feud.

“Good.”

Baek Si-on said.

“I’ll sign.”

“……?”

Chung Ji-sun was visibly caught off guard.

Even Chung Eui-sun paused for half a beat.

They had of course hoped he would say yes.

They just hadn’t expected him to say it this fast.

By the normal process, cooperation at this level meant lawyers reading the clauses first, the management team modelling the returns, and an exclusivity-conflict review against every other brand.

Instead, one “Good” — as brisk as agreeing to one more take on set.

Once he recovered, Chung Ji-sun asked at once:

“Actor Baek — won’t you take a little time to think it over?”

Baek Si-on looked at Chung Eui-sun.

“With the Vice Chairman here in person as my witness, how could I possibly refuse?”

The line was delivered flatly.

And it worked beautifully.

Chung Eui-sun’s mood improved at a visible rate.

Very much so.

Face: in abundance.

He had come today, of course, to do Hyundai Department Store a favour as middleman.

But Baek Si-on’s sentence had accepted that favour in broad daylight, with a receipt.

Between chaebol, interests mattered.

Face mattered too.

Chung Eui-sun waved a hand, smiling.

“Si-on-ah, you’ve given me this much face — I can’t let you come out of it short.”

He turned to Chung Ji-sun.

“Ji-sun-ah — three percent? Isn’t that a little stingy?”

He looked back at Baek Si-on.

“What level is our Si-on at now? Two straight Billboard number-ones, the Venice Volpi Cup, the Silver Crown Cultural Medal. Yesterday he walked into the National Theater in Crown Prince Sado’s robes — and how much did Hyundai and SK’s market value add up to gaining today?”

He clicked his tongue softly.

“Five percent. At the very least.”

Baek Eun-ah’s eyes flickered.

Here it comes.

Here it comes.

The chaebol family’s brotherly double act.

Chung Ji-sun played his part perfectly. He lowered his head, thought for two seconds, and nodded with great sincerity.

“Cousin’s right. I was being short-sighted.”

He looked at Baek Si-on.

“Five percent.”

“For as long as Hyundai Duty Free keeps its doors open, five percent of net profit lands in Actor Baek’s account every year-end.”

Baek Eun-ah watched the two of them and harboured quiet suspicions.

It had probably been five percent from the very start.

The vice chairman had then sold his own cousin a favour on the side.

More importantly, the little performance had retired, once and for all, the risk of a certain somebody remembering the Aslan sales-commission idea.

And now: the department store bled. The motor company played the generous one. And Chung Eui-sun, sitting right there, banked a standing image as “Hyundai’s warm and honourable vice chairman”.

Baek Si-on looked at Chung Eui-sun.

“Vice Chairman-nim — won’t this put Chairman Chung in a difficult position?”

Chung Eui-sun waved it off, smiling.

“It won’t.”

Chung Ji-sun picked it up at once:

“Of course it won’t. Actor Baek’s value deserves the number.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Then I have no objections.”

By this point in a negotiation, a great many things no longer needed to be said out loud.

Chung Ji-sun rose and offered his hand first.

“I’ll have Hyundai Department Store’s legal team draft up the terms as fast as they can, and coordinate with your manager’s side.”

Baek Si-on shook it.

“Thank you for your trouble.”

Chung Eui-sun stood as well and patted him on the shoulder.

“Film well.”

With that, he glanced over the high-roof Starex one more time.

“If anything about the van isn’t comfortable, tell Hyundai directly.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“It’s already very good.”

Chung Eui-sun smiled.

“Then sleep more.”

That sentence was more practical than any business pleasantry in the language.

Hyundai Duty Free · the deal, as agreed inside a van

If the licence lands5% of net profit, every year-end, for as long as the doors stay open
If it doesn’tBrand ambassador · ₩1,500,000,000 a year · two years minimum

One word had closed it. Paperwork to follow from Hyundai Department Store’s legal team.

The Baek siblings walked the two Chungs out of the van.

Outside, Hyundai Department Store’s and Hyundai Motor’s people were already waiting at a polite distance.

A few steps out, Chung Eui-sun turned back and waved at Baek Si-on.

Chung Ji-sun nodded his goodbye too.

The two parties left the set parking area quickly.

Baek Eun-ah stood where she was and watched the black sedans out of sight before following her cousin back toward the Starex.

Two steps along, she remembered something, and said quietly:

“Oppa. CJ and LG actually both sent offers.”

“Pretend you didn’t see them.”

“Oh…”

Two seconds later, she couldn’t help asking anyway:

“Then the other endorsement offers — do you want to look at any of them?”

“Not taking any.”

Baek Si-on said it flatly.

Baek Eun-ah looked up.

“None of them at all?”

“Mm.”

“Including the ones with very big numbers?”

“Mm.”

The door slid open.

Baek Si-on bent his way in.

“I’ll sleep more. Wake me when they call me to set.”

“Okay.”

Baek Eun-ah shut the door for him, obediently, and stood outside looking up at the Jeonju sky.

Then she let out a very long sigh.

One “not taking any” from her cousin — that was a good ten billion won of endorsement fees, gone.

She understood.

She truly did.

She could even have written it up in her business-strategy notes, under the title Top Artists: Brand Exclusivity and Long-Term Value Management.

But understanding does not stop the pain.

Adults live their lives being torn, over and over, between “strategically correct” and “but that was money”.

Baek Eun-ah lowered her head and wrote in her memo app:

memo · notes

Other endorsements: paused. Not taking them.

Don’t cry. Five percent of net profit is worth more.

Written, she locked the screen.

There.

Self-comfort: complete.

Effectiveness: mediocre.

November 9 · night Fresh from the shower, and the phone is already ringing Jeonju · the hotel One call from Los Angeles, one tablet from downstairs, and a chair that thinks it is a throne.

Night.

After the Sado night shoot wrapped, Baek Si-on got back to the hotel.

He had just finished his shower when the phone buzzed.

Caller ID: Scooter Braun.

Baek Si-on answered with a towel in his hair.

Baek Si-on ← Scooter Braun · the hotel, Jeonju · incoming

“Hello.”

“Baek — the AMAs stage choreography is done. Tabitha finished the draft, the video’s in Eun-ah’s inbox, and you’ve got half a month to rehearse.”

“Dance?”

“Relax. Nobody is turning you into Michael Jackson. The stage design leans ceremonial — the movement load is not going to be extreme.”

“Okay.”

“For the outfit, you wear the series from yesterday. Not the gonryongpo — that cross-cultural reading is too complicated, American audiences will think you’re playing a Qing emperor. Same series, simplified.”

Baek Si-on’s eyebrow moves.

“How are you this well informed?”

Down the line comes that laugh of his, the signature blend of self-satisfaction and mischief.

“Baek, I’m the expensive kind of manager.”

“…Fine. Noted.”

“Rehearse the choreography.”

“I will.”

The call ended.

Baek Si-on rang Baek Eun-ah next.

Very soon after, the doorbell went — she had already been on her way up with the tablet.

Baek Si-on reached out and took it from her hands, walking back inside.

“Oppa, you’re going to dance?”

“Mm.”

“Daebak.”

Baek Eun-ah did not leave. She simply followed him in.

Opened the inbox.

Downloaded the attachment.

Played the video.

AMAs KING Stage Choreo Draft · first play · a rehearsal room in Los Angeles

A choreographer in black, seated on a chair at centre frame. Backup dancers flanking him on both sides. Lights bright, floor spotless.

The intro hits — drums and claps pressing in, layer upon layer.

The man in the chair does not move. The dancers do: a cutting-step sequence on his left, a shoulder-drop sequence on his right.

First verse. The choreographer sits steady as a mountain — fingers resting light on the armrest, occasionally a small lift of the chin, occasionally a new angle toward the camera.

The dancers split and regroup at his sides, back and forth — a court of extremely busy palace attendants.

Verse one ends. He is still sitting.

Baek Eun-ah watched, baffled.

“Oppa — does this count as dancing?”

Baek Si-on kept his eyes on the screen.

“At the moment, it counts as office posture.”

“……”

AMAs KING Stage Choreo Draft · continued

Second verse. The choreographer rises from the chair at last — slowly.

The dancers split instantly into two rows in front of him and go down on one knee.

He walks the corridor between them. They rise and fan out behind him into a human wedge — a guard of honour deployed behind a throne.

The chorus: at last he truly moves. Nothing complex — a raised hand, a turn, a step, the shoulder and upper body pressing down on the beat.

The point is not technique. It is presence. Every step he takes forward, the formation behind him changes once.

Watching, Baek Eun-ah suddenly understood.

“This isn’t dancing.”

“Mm?”

“It’s an enthronement ceremony.”

“……”

And she wasn’t wrong.

This choreography had never planned to make Baek Si-on compete with professional dancers on explosive movement.

He was not built to suddenly become a sharp-cutting, full-formation idol. That would have been the jarring thing.

What fit Baek Si-on now was “move little — and with every move, make everyone else move”.

King was arrogant enough already.

Have him genuinely tear through a dance break, and it would tip into trying too hard.

This, exactly as it was, was right.

The video ended.

Baek Eun-ah took the tablet back and played it a second time.

“Oppa, are you going to crack up laughing on stage?”

“No.”

“Okay. The movement load really is light, but the beats have to land exactly. Especially the getting-up-from-the-chair part — rise too early and it looks like a bathroom emergency; rise too late and it looks like you overslept.”

Baek Si-on turned his head to look at her.

Baek Eun-ah shut up at once.

Privately, though, she felt her note had been very professional.

Even vivid.

AMAs KING Stage Choreo Draft · second play

Second verse again. The formation cracks open to both sides, and the choreographer stands dead centre, arm rising to point forward.

The lyric lands on the line:

“Long live the king.”

Watching the frame, Baek Eun-ah felt goosebumps rise up her back.

She could almost see the AMAs stage.

the AMAs · the KING stage · live to all of America · as she imagines it

The stage goes dark. The drums come up.

Baek Si-on, in the modified court robes, seated on the chair.

Every dancer goes down.

And then, in a live shot beamed across America — he rises.

At that moment, Korean audiences will probably lose their minds. American audiences will lose their minds too. The only calm person in the building may be the man on the chair.

Baek Eun-ah glanced sideways to check.

Sure enough.

Her cousin was studying the footwork in the video, dead serious — like a man researching tomorrow’s blocking on set.

Not one gram of awareness that he was about to be crowned on an American stage.

Baek Eun-ah sighed.

Fine.

He handles the crowning.

She handles the excitement on his behalf.

A clear division of labour. All good.

Chapter Two Hundred Thirty-One

Corny Pickup Lines Are Also Accepted

Early June · the shoot’s fifteenth day

Two hundred extras · the faders at zero

The underground nightclub · ‘The Three Evils’ on set

Early June.

‘The Three Evils’ had been shooting for half a month.

Those days, the production lived almost entirely inside the underground nightclub the art department had built.

A glowing sign at the entrance, and security in black.

Walk in further.

A dance floor shredded by neon, corridors paneled in mirrors, dark-red leather sofas, sealed VIP rooms.

The core passage of the nightclub sequence began with the male lead pushing through the front door.

The center of the dance floor was packed with a hundred-plus extras, blue-violet light cutting every face into fragments.

The sound system at the DJ booth was at maximum.

The walls were shaking. The floor was shaking.

Baek Si-on, in an old jacket, came down the steps from the entrance.

Nobody noticed him.

At that volume and in that light, a stranger’s presence rounded to zero.

He walked along the edge of the dance floor, drew the gun from his waistband, and aimed it at the ceiling.

BANG—!

The subwoofers swallowed most of the gunshot.

Sparks and debris rained from overhead.

The crowd looked up first — and got more excited.

They assumed the club had staged the effect.

Somebody screamed. Somebody waved along to the beat.

The male lead played by Baek Si-on wore no particular expression, because he had discovered the place was rotten enough that even gunfire registered as entertainment programming.

So he walked to the DJ booth and slammed every fader to the bottom.

In one second, the room fell from deafening heaviness into absolute silence.

A hundred-plus people stopped at once.

What remained in their ears was only the whine of the sound that had just vanished.

Baek Si-on raised the gun.

This time, everybody heard it.

Screams burst out of the silence, and the crowd poured for the exits like water.

Baek Si-on stood on the DJ booth and waited for the crowd to drain away before he came down and set foot in the first corridor toward the back of the house.

VIP rooms lined both sides, numbers pasted on the doors.

He kicked in the first one.

The three doors, as opened

Door one: a drugged woman blurred into the sofa, the man beside her caught with his trousers half off, the fright not yet assembled on his face. One shot — blood across the wallpaper.

Door two: white powder fanned across the glass table, several young people melted into the cushions, eyes too scattered to find focus. Several shots — then a fresh magazine.

Door three: a full wall of surveillance. A dozen screens showing different rooms. A bespectacled tech folded under the console, arms over his head.

On the feeds: women drunk past the edge of knowing. Guests who didn’t know at all. Trades in progress between politicians. A blackmail chain running behind every single screen.

He pulled the main power. The screens went dark one after another. The tech under the desk raised his face, believing himself saved.

One shot. No.

Baek Si-on kept moving deeper.

Every door he kicked open exposed one more layer of the club’s underside.

The sequence didn’t carry many lines.

❝

Baek Jeong-hoon did not let his character explain the evil. The evil speaks for itself.

— the entire script policy for the massacre sequence

Before shooting these scenes, Baek Jeong-hoon had long since run every critical route through the game engine at Bluehole Studio.

The routes, as pre-run at Bluehole

From the male lead’s first shot, to the crowd evacuation, to the floor-by-floor push through the VIP block, ending at the nightclub owner’s office — every line walked in the model first.

Where the camera enters. Which door the actor exits through.

Which gunshot triggers the extras’ run.

Which landing spots are reserved for the stunt team going down.

Executed to the model on the day, the efficiency bordered on absurd: a massacre with a hundred-plus extras, gunfire, shattered glass, club lighting, and four levels of blocking — shot cleaner than some two-hander romance scenes, because romance reshoots for a single glance, and here the male lead only has to pull the trigger.

The nightclub owner played by Yoo Ah-in, of course, heard the news.

He just couldn’t get out.

The entire club had a single exit that ran to the surface — and Baek Si-on had walked in through it.

Originally this scene, like the ones before it, had ended one shot to the head, the male lead’s business concluded and a clean exit made.

But Baek Jeong-hoon decided that death was too merciful for this particular species of filth, and rewrote Yoo Ah-in’s ending: Baek Si-on would beat him to death with a baseball bat.

This part, Baek Si-on thoroughly enjoyed shooting.

By the time Baek Jeong-hoon called the cut, Yoo Ah-in lay on the floor coated in fake blood, syrup stuck in his hair.

He pushed himself up off the floor, panting, and asked:

“Director — my character does die for good in this one, right?”

Baek Jeong-hoon studied the monitor playback.

“The audience is going to want you to die one more time.”

Yoo Ah-in was quiet for a beat.

“Then I acted it well.”

Baek Si-on handed the bat back to the props team, a fleck of fake blood still on the back of his hand.

“Don’t worry. You die quite likably.”

Yoo Ah-in sat down on the floor where he was and raised a middle finger at him.

❧ ❧ ❧

The day the shoot moved out of the basement

One stage going up in the open air

A citizen’s plaza · the promo-stage set

The day Choi Jin-ri came to the set, the production had already left the underground stage and was building a promo stage in a citizen’s plaza.

The ‘Train to Busan’ shoot had just wrapped, but wrap hadn’t bought her a single idle day.

The lip-line deal with Estée Lauder was still in negotiation, and the group’s comeback had entered its preparation window.

Today’s set visit was an hour stolen out of a stacked schedule.

The security at the gate didn’t even stop her.

“Jin-ri-ssi, you made it?”

Choi Jin-ri nodded, smiling.

“Appreciate you. Hard at work.”

The deeper she went, the more familiar the faces.

Baek Jeong-hoon’s old crew was all here; one of them recognized her passing and broke into a grin.

“Jin-ri-ssi, wrapped already?”

“Ye — just wrapped.”

“The zombie shoots were rough, weren’t they?”

“A bit gentler than Director Baek.”

The man nearly laughed out loud — that sentence could never be allowed within range of the monitor.

It tended to raise the director’s attack power for the rest of the day.

❧ ❧ ❧

A production assistant walked Choi Jin-ri toward the shade canopy.

Baek Si-on was reading his script with his head down.

She signaled the assistant to keep quiet, circled behind him, bent down, and covered his eyes from behind, her voice deliberately low:

“Guess who?”

“Choi Jin-ri.”

“......”

She let go, came around in front of him, thoroughly deflated:

“I lowered my voice and everything. How did you still guess?”

“Because nobody else would dare.”

The deflation dissolved off her face at once. Lips pressed against a grin, she reached out and ran her palm over the buzz cut.

Her hand glided along the stubble, lightly.

“The hand feel is genuinely good.”

Baek Si-on let her finish the second pass before he spoke:

“Are you here on a set visit, or to pet my head?”

“Both.”

Delivered with a fully clear conscience.

Baek Si-on set the script aside and indicated she should sit.

She didn’t sit right away — she stood in front of him, studying the buzz cut.

“It feels like being back when we first met.”

Suddenly she raised a hand and shielded her own eyes.

“What’s wrong?” Baek Si-on asked.

“Do you have sunglasses?”

“What do you need sunglasses for?”

She kept her eyes covered, her voice sweet enough to have been freshly rolled in sugar.

“You’re too bright. I can barely keep my eyes open.”

Baek Si-on picked the script back up and turned a page.

“Who taught you corny pickup lines?”

She dropped the hand, insubordination written across her face a hundred and eighty ways.

“Is it not good?”

“It’s good to the point I’d like to report it.”

She swatted him, lightly.

“You are genuinely gifted at wrecking a mood.”

“Your line was beyond saving to begin with.”

“It was adorable and you know it.”

“Right. Crime scenes can be adorable too.”

She hit him again, properly annoyed this time.

But the hand didn’t come back — it stayed where it landed, resting on his forearm.

“On the ‘Train to Busan’ shoot, there was a stretch where I genuinely couldn’t stand to eat anything red.”

“Why?”

“I’d be sitting next to extras in full blood makeup, and there’d be ketchup in my lunchbox.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Very reasonable.”

“And the zombie makeup. You have no idea — after they finished the makeup they’d lie down on the ground to rest, and every crew member walking past gave them a wide berth.”

“Afraid of stepping on them?”

“Afraid they’d open their eyes.”

Baek Si-on gave the picture a moment of thought.

It was, genuinely, a little frightening.

“Are you heading back to Seoul tonight?” he asked.

She turned her face sideways and returned serve:

“Do you want me to go back?”

“No.”

The smile surfaced fast at the corners of her eyes.

Her mouth insisted on maintaining form anyway:

“Since you’ve begged so sincerely, I’ll be magnanimous and stay.”

Baek Si-on nodded along, cooperative.

“Thank you for gracing me with your presence, Miss Choi.”

Satisfied, she gave the hand on his forearm a small squeeze — the seal of confirmation stamped.

❧ ❧ ❧

That night

One script open on the sofa · one bathroom running

The hotel · the suite

The hotel room door unlocked with a card-swish.

The corridor lighting stayed outside with the door; inside, only a soft warmth remained.

Choi Jin-ri pulled off her cap, her hair pressed into a slight arc.

She changed shoes at the entryway, toed into the hotel slippers, and the whole person exhaled.

The dating radius of a public figure is narrow.

Streets couldn’t be wandered freely.

Cinemas couldn’t be entered casually.

In a crowded restaurant, the phone lenses arrived at your face before the menu was even open.

The last place a person could genuinely relax usually came down to a hotel room.

Said aloud, it sounded pathetic.

But both of them understood it perfectly.

Choi Jin-ri set her bag down and headed for the bathroom.

“Shower first.”

“Mm.”

Baek Si-on lay back on the sofa and opened the ‘The Three Evils’ script.

Tomorrow’s block was the heavy passage of the politician line — few lines, extremely fine blocking.

Baek Jeong-hoon had crowded the storyboard margins with handwritten notes, some of the lines legible only at the threshold of a pharmacy prescription; unless you were him, reading them exacted a psychological toll.

Water sounded behind the bathroom door.

Baek Si-on turned to the next page.

The room was quiet.

Only the whisper of pages and the water.

The water stopped.

The bathroom door opened; heat rolled out, and Choi Jin-ri emerged in the hotel bathrobe.

She walked to the sofa and dropped onto him directly.

Her face burrowed out from between his hands and the script, wedging itself bodily into the space between the plot and the character.

Baek Si-on lowered his eyes.

Choi Jin-ri blinked up at him.

“Are you still working tonight?”

“Your girlfriend is requesting additional scenes~”

The words on the page lost all reading value.

Baek Si-on closed the script and tossed it onto the coffee table.

Paper met glass with a small sound.

Her smile had just surfaced when a phone rang, and her face collapsed by half on the spot.

Baek Si-on picked up the phone.

The caller display read Baek Eun-ah.

Choi Jin-ri, still draped across him, buried her face in his shoulder and hummed one flat note into it.

“She had better actually have an emergency.”

Baek Si-on answered.

Baek Si-on → Baek Eun-ah · voice · the suite, past eleven

“Talk.”

“The first production run is out — about fifty thousand units. COSMAX ran random spot checks: film-forming speed, color deviation, scent, seal integrity — all pass.”

“And the goods?”

“Already in the CJ Korea Express warehouse. The storage records and the inspection reports are in your inbox.”

Baek Si-on’s other hand settled on Choi Jin-ri’s back, palm resting lightly on the edge of the bathrobe.

“Release date?”

“We can set the official date as soon as Joohyun-unnie finishes shooting the endorsement commercial.”

The face buried in his shoulder shifted, slightly.

Baek Eun-ah kept reporting.

“The commercial director — do you want one sourced fresh from the industry, or will you name one yourself?”

Baek Si-on thought about it.

“Get director Hwang Soo-ah.”

“Ask her schedule first. If she’s free, have her bring three proposals — I’ll decide after I’ve read them.”

“Understood.”

The call ended.

The screen went dark.

Choi Jin-ri’s face was still buried in the hollow of his shoulder.

She had heard the entire call.

Back then, Baek Si-on had only talked to her in the car about the idea of an independent makeup brand.

It had been a thought from after a party in New York.

Now: the brand was registered, the product was in production, the goods were in a warehouse, and the commercial was about to shoot.

Fast to the point of being unreal.

Something in her chest swelled with pride.

The man she had picked was, genuinely, formidable.

And yet something else sat on the other side of the scale — small, small enough that she herself barely wanted to admit it existed.

Choi Jin-ri lifted her face and asked, elaborately casual:

“The endorser you picked is Irene?”

“Mm.”

“Why her?”

Baek Si-on didn’t circle.

“Value.”

Choi Jin-ri propped herself up a little.

He explained it flatly:

The math, as stated

A lip line at the Song Hye-kyo tier: two billion four hundred million won minimum over three years — plus a rival-brand clause to negotiate with Laneige.

Bae Joo-hyun: three years, six hundred million won. The contract is clean. The face is built for makeup close-ups.

The decisive line item: not yet locked down by a major.

“You’re that certain she’ll blow up?”

Baek Si-on found the question mildly surprising.

“Name one SM girl-group face who didn’t?”

Choi Jin-ri was caught with it.

The answer was crude and annoyingly persuasive.

From S.E.S. to Girls’ Generation, then f(x), then Red Velvet — SM girl-group faces carried market memory in their DNA.

And Bae Joo-hyun’s face really was made for a beauty close-up.

Then she caught herself drifting off the point.

Baek Si-on had taken “value”, “SM face”, and “rival clause” and routed her question into an entirely rational frequency.

While she was still hunting a way to drag the topic back, Baek Si-on had already picked up his phone.

“What are you doing?”

He unlocked the screen.

“If you don’t like her, I’ll have Eun-ah pull the deal right now. Nothing’s been shot. Changing the face is still cheap.”

“Ya!”

Choi Jin-ri pushed up out of his arms and snatched the phone clean out of his hand.

Baek Si-on’s shoulder was pressed down by her weight.

“Then what were you sulking about just now?”

She hugged the phone to her chest, answering with maximum stubbornness.

“I was making a normal inquiry.”

“Normal inquiries grab my phone out of my hand?”

“......”

She bit her lower lip.

She killed the screen, tossed the phone to the far end of the sofa, and draped herself back into his arms, face buried in his chest.

“I really don’t dislike her.”

One of Baek Si-on’s hands landed on her back.

“Mm.”

“And I’m not saying to replace her.”

“Mm.”

After nesting a while, her face came up again.

“Did you do that on purpose?”

“On purpose what?”

“Making it sound that serious. Pulling the deal, replacing people.”

His palm moved a little way up her spine.

“Your being upset is more serious than the endorsement.”

Choi Jin-ri froze.

The weight of that sentence landed on her chest more accurately than any stretch of business logic.

He was willing — over one sentence from her — to withdraw a brand decision already deep into production.

That act was itself the answer.

She wanted to hold on to her small sulk, but the corners of her mouth had already defected.

All she could do was bury her face deeper, so he wouldn’t discover too fast how thoroughly she’d been soothed soft.

“What kind of comfort is that?”

“Did it work?”

She held out.

She did not hold out.

The laughter leaked out, muffled, from against his shoulder.

“A little.”

Baek Si-on looked down.

“Then it’ll do.”

She poked him in the shoulder.

“Don’t ever do that again.”

“Which part?”

“Throwing the choice at me.”

❧ ❧ ❧

When Baek Si-on came out of his shower, Choi Jin-ri had annexed the right side of the bed.

The duvet stopped at her waist, her phone discarded by the pillow, hair fanned across the hotel’s white pillowcase.

Baek Si-on lifted the far side and lay down.

A big shoot, and an early call, tomorrow.

But she had finally gotten hold of him, and had no intention whatsoever of sleeping.

She turned over, reached out, and found the buzz cut with her fingertips.

One pass. Another.

“Oppa.”

“Mm?”

“When do you wrap?”

“Late July. Maybe early August.”

“Then when you wrap, let’s go on a trip?”

“Where?”

Her fingertip drew a small circle on the stubble.

“Inside Oppa’s heart.”

She started laughing first, shoulders shaking.

Baek Si-on: “......”

“You have a lot of corny pickup lines in stock today.”

“So will you take delivery or not?”

Baek Si-on rolled over to face her.

Their faces were very close.

The pillow was pressed into one deep crease.

“Delivery accepted.”

The bedside lamp was switched off at some later point.

What remained in the room was a thin line of city light through the curtain gap, and two breathing patterns slowly folding into one.

The night was long.

The hotel’s soundproofing was excellent.

Chapter One Hundred Eighty Eight

Boss Baek Does Not Accept Naming Rights

Chicago → Toronto · the tour’s cold northern run Ninety seconds of a song that isn’t out yet · one Canadian son singing another man’s chorus · a thirty-percent publishing deal struck in a Dubai party · a demo called Starboy · and half a million dollars refused in the name of art The United Center → the Universal war room → Toronto → a tour bus salad → two inboxes, two deserts Chicago meets the future early and applauds in the wrong silence; Toronto rediscovers its most famous son as a harmony vocalist; a song renamed for flattery is renamed back; and a producer discovers that money, like art, is negotiable — briefly.

Chicago’s flavor ran different from the first note. The city was bitter cold. The arena was boiling — boiling enough that the backstage crew had begun to suspect the air conditioning of structural damage by scream.

Partway into the show, the stage lights sank. The tail of ‘Sign of the Times’ hadn’t fully dissolved, and the room had already gone quieter than it had any right to be. Then, from the dark: piano. A few simple chords. A scatter of the audience caught on first — small, broken exclamations rippling out.

Baek Si-on stood at center stage and didn’t rush. No song title on the big screen. No film footage. Nothing that would send Universal’s legal department sprinting into the control room. One white beam, and him.

He took the mic and sang the chorus first.

‘See You Again’ · Chicago · the first breath of it, ninety seconds before the world

“It’s been a long day without you, my friend”

“And I’ll tell you all about it when I see you again”

“We’ve come a long way from where we began”

“Oh, I’ll tell you all about it when I see you again”

“When I see you again”

Most of the room didn’t know the song yet. But sometimes the human race doesn’t need a title to recognize a farewell.

When the first section ended, a second follow-spot bloomed from the wings. Wiz walked into it in a black jacket, and this time the arena’s scream properly detonated — because they understood they were colliding, months early, with a future famous moment.

Wiz reached his side. The drums came in. The rap pressed down on top — that loose, wounded American-street cadence of his, half the story of an old friend, half a conversation with someone no longer there. Baek Si-on caught the chorus and held it. One of them spoke outward. One of them gathered inward.

‘See You Again’ · the verse Chicago wasn’t supposed to hear yet

“We’ve come a long way from where we began”

“Oh, won’t we tell it all when we see you again”

— and the chorus, once more, both voices on it —

“So let the light guide your way, yeah”

“Hold every memory as you go”

“And every road you take will always lead you home, home”

Ninety seconds. That was all. The lights sank before the second full chorus could land.

And the arena — didn’t scream. For a few seconds, the whole building sat inside a strange, shared quiet. Then the applause began, one section at a time, and grew, and grew.

Behind the stage, Universal’s promo team watched the real-time social trends with faces that slid from professional smiles into undisguised euphoria. Ninety seconds. No movie frames. No full arrangement. And the keywords were already climbing.

Scooter stood at the monitoring wall, watching the curve go nearly vertical, and turned to the Universal people.

“Now you understand what I meant about not letting it grow mold in a vault.”

Nobody from Universal argued. Data is more honest than a mouth.

That night, ‘See You Again’ still wasn’t a song. It was a seed, planted early in twenty thousand people — enough, after ninety seconds, that everyone who heard it had started waiting for the flower.

❧ ❧ ❧

Toronto was another species of chaos entirely. This was Canada. The moment Justin Bieber’s name appeared on the show poster, the atmosphere outside the arena had already left the ground.

Baek Eun-ah watched the trending topics and felt her scalp prickle.

“Cousin — I genuinely can’t tell if this is your concert or a mass therapy session for Canadian teenagers’ childhoods.”

“Both work.”

“You’re very generous.”

“The tickets sold out anyway.”

“……”

Capitalism’s famous tolerance is, as a rule, built on a sell-out basis.

Past eight o’clock. When the intro of ‘Love Yourself’ began, the roar already filled the building. The song was too big in North America — so big that every guitar pluck arrived with a reminder attached: this is the song that dragged ‘Uptown Funk’ off the throne.

Baek Si-on finished the first verse. Before the chorus — he stopped. The light slid off him and swung to the wings, and a figure so familiar it needed no introduction walked in. The scream came out of the crowd like an explosion. Half the girls were on their feet.

Justin Bieber, microphone in hand, walked to Baek Si-on’s side. The two looked at each other — and went into the chorus together.

‘Love Yourself’ · Toronto · two voices, one chorus

“‘Cause if you like the way you look that much”

“Oh baby you should go and love yourself”

— and on the next line, the whole arena, regardless of language —

“And I didn’t wanna write a song”

“I didn’t wanna write you a song”

“……”

In the backstage dark, Scooter’s face was a whole film festival. The Canadian boy he had raised, up there harmonizing on a Korean man’s Billboard champion. Capitalism, occasionally, does keep track of reincarnations.

The shared chorus ran barely a minute. But when the two voices stacked, the room experienced a strange, dislocated vertigo — as if something that belonged to another timeline had been dragged, bodily, into this one.

The chorus ended. The screaming kept going. Bieber looked at Baek Si-on, leaned into the microphone, and said:

“This song should’ve been mine.”

The arena froze for a beat — then broke into laughter. Everyone knew the writing credits on this song carried a certain Canadian’s name. Baek Si-on gave him one glance.

“You just sang it.”

Bieber: “……”

The crowd laughed itself sick. He clutched his chest and staggered back a step, as if the sentence had scored a direct hit on the soul of Canadian youth. Scooter, backstage, closed his eyes.

Sure enough, off stage, Bieber’s first sentence was:

“When do I release my song?”

Scooter regarded him, expressionless.

“Right now?”

Bieber pointed at the audience, still screaming beyond the wall.

“You heard that?”

“Heard it.”

“They still remember me.”

Scooter studied him for two seconds — and finally handed over the tablet. On the screen: a release plan, already prepared. Where Are Ü Now. Bieber looked at the title, and his eyes came up lit. He knew Baek Si-on’s existence had tightened the screws on him. But crisis is not a bad master. Sometimes a person needs to see someone standing higher before he truly understands that he cannot keep standing where he is.

Baek Si-on passed by in the corridor, water bottle in hand, and asked, offhand:

“Releasing soon?”

Bieber looked up.

“Soon.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Good luck not getting buried under my ‘See You Again.’”

Bieber: “……”

Confirmed, again: this Korean man was, at times, genuinely unfit to be trusted with well-wishing.

❧ ❧ ❧

The Toronto green room, after the show. Baek Si-on sat on the sofa eating.

Precisely: eating a chicken-breast salad that Baek Eun-ah had forcibly deployed at him. Since the tour began, his diet was under armed guard — carbs counted, sodium controlled, fats exiled, sauce permitted strictly by dip and never by pour. He looked down at the few soulless leaves in the plastic box and was struck, suddenly, by a full-sensory memory of Yoon Hye-ja’s cooking. He had just speared a piece of chicken when his phone rang. Caller ID: Charlie Puth.

Charlie Puth → Baek Si-on · video · the Dubai debrief · Toronto green room, salad time

Charlie talked at approximately twice legal speed. The first item of business was not, in fact, the Dubai exclusive — it was a thing that looked unrelated to work and was about to become work. He was in Dubai. On a “research trip.” A research trip which, in theory, meant deserts by day, local music by night, and city-skyline inspiration at 3 a.m. against the hotel glass. Charlie had interpreted the word “research” with unusual latitude. He had seen the desert. He had also attended parties.

So there was this party — don’t know if it was royal-adjacent, a brand thing, or some heir’s Tuesday — and guess who’s there. Meghan Trainor.

Two young American writers on the way up. One had just been fished out from under Universal’s fifty-thousand-dollar buyout. The other had brainwashed an entire nation with ‘All About That Bass’. Between rooms full of custom tailoring and couture gowns, they had bonded instantly for the purest of reasons: neither of them looked like they belonged there either. They talked music, retro harmony, old-school soul.

— and then I played her one of my demos. The one you listened to. And picked apart. And rejected.

Get to the point.

The song was called ‘Marvin Gaye’. BUT. Out of respect for you, boss — I renamed it. ‘SW Baek’.

Whether this was flattery or a phonetic disaster was genuinely hard to rule on. Meghan had loved it. Loved the song. And asked if she could sing on it. Which was lovely — except Charlie was signed to Baek Si-on now, and whether the song came out, how it came out, and how the credits split were all questions with one address.

The duet is fine.

The relief on the other end was audible.

Really?!

The excitement came through the speaker at a volume the green room did not need.

But the title goes back.

…What?

‘Marvin Gaye.’

A pause.

None of that apple-polishing garbage.

……

And thus concluded, on the spot, the flattery campaign of one Charles Puth.

And then, at last, the actual agenda. The Dubai exclusive. He sent the demo over. The file was named —

‘Desert Rose’.

Baek Si-on opened it. Headphones on. The first thing in was a light string section — then a melodic line with the Gulf in it, surfacing slow. It sounded like a desert at night: wind moving fine sand, a distant city burning gold. When the chorus arrived, Baek Si-on’s famously flat face registered a change.

The song had something. And it was obvious Charlie had done more than touch sand out there. He had, by the sound of it, also touched champagne coupes, yacht railings, hotel-suite glass, and a certain hormone content in the air of certain parties.

He played it through twice, then called Scooter.

“Charlie’s Dubai song. It’s good. We make it.”

Scooter didn’t hesitate.

“Send it.”

Baek Si-on forwarded the file. Five minutes later, Scooter called back.

“That kid didn’t go on a research trip.”

“Mm.”

“He went and got himself marinated in capitalism.”

“You can hear it.”

“But the song’s good.”

“So we make it.”

Scooter snapped into work mode: Middle Eastern session players, an Arab-heritage production consultant, language consultants to certify the title and imagery, the show director re-cutting Dubai’s opening visuals around it.

Once the call with Charlie ended, Baek Si-on brought the same matter to Scooter in person.

“A genius idea.”

Scooter slowly raised a thumb.

“His Highness the Crown Prince of Dubai hears this, he’ll probably send you a purebred Arabian horse.”

“Where would I keep a horse?”

Scooter: “……”

He had noticed it before: Baek Si-on’s angle on any question was always more practical than the gift itself.

“Forget the horse for now. Qatar can’t be left out either.”

“Find the people.”

“Mm. I’m on it right now.”

Scooter turned to go. At the door, he looked back.

“Baek.”

“Mm?”

“This Middle East run of yours might not be a tour.”

“Then what is it?”

“A diplomatic-incident prevention program.”

“……”

Research, noun — the act of touching sand, champagne, yacht rail, and suite glass in one week, and billing the sand.

— the tour office, on the definition of fieldwork

The Jung Jae-joon demo had landed in his inbox that same evening, file name one word long: Starboy. Baek Si-on’s eyebrow rose a degree at the name. He put the headphones on. Pressed play.

The first second was an entirely different animal. If Charlie’s ‘Desert Rose’ was a flower opening in the desert night, Jung Jae-joon’s demo was a golden gate getting kicked off its hinges, crystal chandeliers coming down across the floor. Hard drums. Dirty bass. Synths dripping money. When the chorus hit, Baek Si-on laughed out loud.

What a magnificent, glittering sin. This wasn’t a song dedicated to a city. This was a song dedicated to everyone who believed they could buy the world.

Jung Jae-joon → Baek Si-on · voice · the Starboy debrief · the producer’s studio, Seoul

Listened?

Listened.

Verdict?

Baek Si-on leaned back in his chair.

Are you short on money lately?

Two full seconds of silence.

…You can HEAR that?

……

A cough came down the line.

You wanted something the Middle East dates could use. I did some research. That region isn’t short on deserts, or night skylines, or golden buildings.

Mm.

What they’re short of is someone singing their luxury like it’s a crime.

Baek Si-on looked at the two file names on his screen. ‘Desert Rose’. ‘Starboy’. One for Dubai. One for Doha. One, the romance of the desert night. One, the bragging of the gulf lights. Perfectly balanced, actually.

Approved. Eun-ah will transfer you five hundred thousand dollars in a bit.

Jung Jae-joon answered immediately:

Boss Baek — I believe art should never be polluted by money.

Then I won’t pay you.

But the producer can be.

Chapter Nine

A Late-Night Meal, Two Souls in Silent Resonance

April Late night The alleys of Yeonnam-dong Parting ways after the first meeting

Coming out of the studio, the night wind had turned colder. The alleys of Yeonnam-dong were still. Occasionally a cat bolted across a wall, knocking an empty can rolling away into the dark.

Han-teuk had already left, having exchanged phone numbers with Si-on beforehand — “for convenience,” he said, though Si-on suspected Han-teuk mainly wanted a record. A man who’d been choked today tends to keep the contact information of his attacker close, just in case the police ever need it.

Choi Jin-ri walked beside Eun-ah, and at the alley mouth she suddenly stopped.

“That…”

Si-on turned.

“Let me take you to dinner.”

She said it without looking at him, staring at a brick in the ground.

Si-on checked his phone. 10:20 p.m.

“Alright. I’m hungry anyway.”

Beside him, Eun-ah’s face twitched. She turned and stared at Si-on, an expression full of accusation.

Forty minutes ago this man was at an alley mouth eating barbecue. Forty minutes. Just forty minutes!

She wanted to say something, but she looked at Choi Jin-ri and swallowed it.

Fine. Her cousin’s stomach was not a territory she was qualified to understand.

That night Two alleys into Yeonnam-dong The little eatery Eun-ah found

The restaurant was Eun-ah’s find. Two alleys deeper into Yeonnam-dong, down a lane narrow enough that even nav apps struggled, at the very end stood a small shop with no sign at all. The front looked like a single wooden door with two pots of pothos beside it.

Pushing the door open, inside was roomier than it looked. Six tables, only one occupied — an elderly couple eating in silence, each lost in their own bowl. It was a comfortable kind of quiet.

The light was dim, a warm yellow. The handwritten menu on the wall was scrawled so badly it looked like a doctor’s prescription. The owner, a woman in her fifties with her hair pinned up and an apron stained with grease, leaned out of the kitchen.

“Sit down. Shout if you want anything.”

Eun-ah chose the table farthest in, against the wall.

Unmarked eatery · Yeonnam-dong

Evening set · table of three

Doenjang-jjigae — soybean-paste stew, bubbling

Soon-dubu jjigae — soft tofu quivering in red broth

Jangjorim — braised beef in soy, glossy

Dotori-muk muchim — acorn jelly salad

Three bowls of rice — “generous.”

Dish of finely sliced scallions and a small bowl of sesame salt, set to one side.

“Doenjang-jjigae, a soft tofu stew, braised beef, and acorn jelly salad.”

Si-on called toward the kitchen, then added.

“Three bowls of rice. Generous.”

Eun-ah said, very quietly.

“Cousin, did you really just eat?”

“That meal was an apology. It doesn’t count.”

“What kind of logic is that?”

“An apology meal fills you with sincerity, not satiety. This is a real meal.”

Eun-ah gave up. Arguing with this man about the reasonableness of eating was about as productive as debating the necessity of hibernation with a bear.

The food came quickly. The stew bubbled, the tofu quivered in its red broth. Si-on scooped a spoonful of the stew and put it in his mouth.

Hot. He drew in a breath, held it, swallowed, then narrowed his eyes and let out a low, satisfied hum.

“Mm—”

Not exclamation. Just the sound a person makes when hot soup hits their stomach. Then he went at the rice. A mouthful of rice, a mouthful of soup, an occasional slice of beef. Chewing with full cheeks, eating with focus. No talking, no phone, no looking up. Just eating.

Across the table, Choi Jin-ri held a spoon, her stew steaming, untouched. She hadn’t had much appetite. Lately, eating was a chore — get through it, two mouthfuls, stay alive. She had long since stopped caring what food tasted like.

Tonight’s invitation was only to say thanks.

Except… the person across from her had a strange, quiet magnetism to the way he ate. Like a banked fire — not blazing, but warm to sit beside.

About half a minute later, Jin-ri lowered her head and scooped a spoonful of tofu into her mouth. A little hot, but soft. She took another.

Eun-ah’s pancake stopped at her lips, uneaten.

She’d noticed. Jin-ri unnie was eating. Not the token, move-the-chopsticks kind of eating. Real eating. Slow, with long pauses between each bite, but spoon by spoon, and once she even took a piece of pancake.

Eun-ah said nothing. She was afraid that if she spoke, the moment would shatter.

Si-on was on his second bowl of rice when Jin-ri spoke.

“Um… Si-on-ssi.”

“?”

“Today…”

She paused.

“Th…”

The word caught in her throat, couldn’t go up, couldn’t come down. The spoon turned half a circle in her hand, then turned back. She had heard so many words lately. Cheer up. It’ll be okay. Be strong. Don’t listen to those people.

Every one well-meant, and every one exhausting. Because the subtext was always: you are not okay right now, and you need to become okay.

And the word “thank you,” spoken out loud, would mean admitting she was someone who needed help.

“You don’t have to force those words.”

He was fishing the last piece of tofu out of the pot and setting it on his rice, then ladling a spoonful of broth over it, satisfied. His hands didn’t stop. His eyes didn’t lift. His tone was the same as when he’d ordered.

Someone who’s looked after other people getting looked after for once — that’s fair.

✦ ✦ ✦ Baek Si-on

Jin-ri’s spoon paused over her bowl two seconds.

Then she moved it again. Another spoonful of soup. Then a second. Then a third. Between them, a piece of braised beef, chewed a long time, swallowed, and another piece taken.

Si-on kept going at his rice.

The table was quiet for a while. Only the sound of spoons against bowls, and the dishwasher clattering in the kitchen.

When the owner came out to clear, she looked at the table and her hands slowed a beat.

Six plates. Five bowls. One stew pot. One tofu pot.

All empty. Not even the broth left.

“Good eat’in.”

The woman smiled and stacked the dishes deftly. As Jin-ri reached into her pocket for money, Si-on was a step faster, slapping the bills down on the table.

“I…”

“You treat me next time.”

Jin-ri wanted to say something, but found there was nothing much to say. So she didn’t.

The three of them stepped out of the shop. The Yeonnam-dong alleys were quieter than before. Even the cats were silent. The wind carried a cool edge, and somewhere far off the smell of frying chicken.

Choi Jin-ri pulled her mask back up and lowered her hood. Eun-ah yawned beside her.

“Cousin, how are you getting home?”

“Walking.”

“Walking home?”

“Digesting.”

Eun-ah couldn’t be bothered with him. She pulled Jin-ri in the other direction, and after a few steps Jin-ri glanced back. Si-on was walking away down the lane, hands in his pockets, back to them. The streetlight dragged his shadow out long.

Jin-ri turned back and fell in beside Eun-ah. The night wind narrowed her eyes above the mask — not from the wind.

Because she’d eaten too much, and it was making her drowsy. It had been a long time since she’d felt that.

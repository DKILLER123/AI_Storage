Chapter Two Hundred Twenty-One

The Consequences of Copying Your Sunbae

May 15, 2015 · Friday

6:03 a.m. — the alarm, answered on the first ring

The Red Velvet dormitory · four of five still under blankets

The alarm went off at 6:03.

Irene reached out and silenced it.

The other four were still buried in their blankets, breathing in overlapping rhythms.

She lay for ten seconds and ran the day through her head.

‘Music Bank’, third recording. She had read the script once last night, but a few of the segues still didn’t chain smoothly — she’d drill them in the car.

Wendy was due at KBS today to record an OST for ‘School 2015’. Same car, convenient.

Yeri had school.

Ten seconds used up, Irene threw back the covers and got out of bed.

She padded out, pushed open the bathroom door.

Five minutes for washing up.

Then the kitchen: eggs, bread, and milk out of the fridge.

The flame stayed low on the fried egg — last time Soo-young fried eggs, she painted the wall into an abstract.

The bread went through the toaster.

Then she stood in front of Yeri’s wardrobe, took out the school uniform jacket, stretched the shoulders and collar loose with her hands, and hung it on the back of a chair.

6:25.

Irene walked to Yeri’s bed.

“Yeri.”

No response from inside the blanket.

“Yeri. Up.”

The blanket arched, then got hauled upward from within, over the top of her head.

One hand emerged from the edge and waved, weakly, at the air.

“Five more minutes.”

Irene stood at the bedside, arms crossed.

“Yesterday’s five minutes lasted twenty.”

“Today’s will end on time.”

She waited three seconds.

Then she pulled the blanket off.

“Ah—”

“Up. Eggs stop being good when they go cold.”

Irene turned and left.

Behind her came the sound of Yeri staggering toward the bathroom.

Breakfast eaten, Yeri changed into her uniform.

Irene straightened her shirt collar and tucked the rebel corner of fabric back in.

“Phone charged?”

“Ye.”

“Eat a proper lunch.”

“I know.”

Yeri shouldered her bag and looked back once while changing shoes at the door.

“Unnie — good luck today, too.”

“Mm.”

The door clicked shut.

The dorm went quiet.

Irene stood in the entryway, listening to Yeri’s footsteps fade down the corridor.

Then she turned.

Next.

She walked to Wendy’s bed.

Wendy was easier than Yeri — two shoulder-taps and she was awake.

7:40.

The two of them headed out.

❧ ❧ ❧

May 15

Morning commute

The nanny van · script on knees, demo in one ear

In the van, Irene sat in the rear left with the ‘Music Bank’ script open on her knees.

She inked small marks beside several of the segues.

Half-beat pause here. Lift the stress on this word a touch. When this intro ends, look to camera three — not camera two.

Now and then her lips moved silently, running a line; wherever it snagged, she went back to the top.

Wendy sat on the right, headphones on.

The demo of ‘Return’ played in her ears.

She marked breath points and breaks on the lyrics with her pen, glancing out the window now and then.

Passing an intersection in Apgujeong, Wendy’s body tipped toward the window.

Then she tapped Irene’s arm a few times.

“Unnie, look.”

Irene looked up from the script and followed her eyes.

A bus-stop lightbox.

Inside it, an enormous ad.

Saturated, dopamine-loud color. Wardrobe, backdrop, and bottle all keyed to each other.

Five girls, each hoisting a different flavor of BodyArmor, smiles so sweet it looked like the fruit itself had been poured into the lightbox’s tubes.

Irene’s script lowered, slowly.

The shoot was still vivid in memory. The fruit headpieces, ridiculous on. The same smile, take after take after take. The interaction drills against a Baek Si-on standee — expression management on par with winning the Venice Best Actor.

But when the finished product turned up in a Seoul bus-stop lightbox, the feeling was entirely different.

It meant their faces had begun entering strangers’ daily sightlines.

Office workers waiting for the bus would glance. Uncles and aunties passing would scan. Someone might remember their names; most probably wouldn’t.

But their faces were, factually, here.

The van moved on.

Before long, a bus overtook them up the dedicated lane.

“There’s another!”

The entire side of the bus was their ad.

As it swung through its turn, five painted smiles slid slowly past the window.

Wendy scrambled for her phone to photograph it.

But the car was moving; the focus hunted and missed, and the picture came out mostly blur.

She looked at it for a while — and saved it anyway.

Irene watched the ad vanish into the traffic ahead, and the morning’s stored-up fatigue drained out of her all at once.

“At your recording today,” she said, turning to Wendy, “recommend our drink to the producer and the staff.”

Wendy nodded.

“Got it.”

Then she asked:

“Which flavor do you think sells best, unnie?”

Irene thought about it.

Of course she wanted to say watermelon — her flavor.

But in the end she was honest.

“Probably Baek sunbae’s original, reduced-sugar.”

Wendy considered.

“Fair.”

The sales race, as handicapped

Two idols barely half a year into their debut, trying to out-sell Baek Si-on’s personal traffic on fan purchasing power alone.

At this stage of the numbers, that was bordering on disrespectful to mathematics.

❧ ❧ ❧

The van rolled into the streets around KBS.

People were already gathered outside the building — fans, fansite masters, entertainment reporters, passersby with phones out waiting for anyone famous — standing in twos and threes along both sides of the road.

Irene and Wendy got out and bowed toward the fans.

Shutters started up.

Some voices called Irene’s name; some called Wendy’s.

A few steps forward, a turn, a wave — then through KBS’s front doors.

In the elevator they stood side by side.

At Wendy’s floor she got off first.

“Unnie, fighting.”

“You too. Take care of that throat.”

The doors closed.

Irene rode up alone toward the ‘Music Bank’ floor.

The corridor was busy — staff coming and going, equipment carts, walkie-talkies.

Passing the lounge area, she caught two staff members leaning against the coin coffee machine, chatting.

“‘The Producers’ premieres tonight, right?”

“Mm. Cast is monstrous. Wonder if the premiere can break ten percent.”

“Probably, right? A Park Ji-eun script — and look how much KBS spent promoting it.”

“Mm, and IU’s popularity is absurd right now. Ever since Milan Fashion Week it hasn’t come down once.”

“……”

Irene’s steps didn’t stop.

But her ear twitched at “IU.”

She knew ‘The Producers’ was no competitor of hers. As KBS ‘Music Bank’’s MC, she was positioned — technically — to want her own network’s show to explode.

But somewhere in her chest lived a profoundly unreasonable little voice, and it said, softly:

The wish, as amended twice

Draft one: may the premiere miss its numbers.

Too vicious. Withdrawn.

Draft two, final: it’s fine if the show does well — so long as Lee Ji-eun personally receives not one word of praise.

❧ ❧ ❧

Pre-recording prep unfolded in its proper order.

Irene paid her greetings to the PD and the writer, confirmed the day’s run-sheet changes, and went to change into her MC wardrobe.

Today’s look: a blue-and-white sailor suit, hair in a side-parted low ponytail.

During makeup she kept the cue cards on her knees, segues running under her breath while the artist worked.

Park Bo-gum

Music Bank MC · the politest man on Korean television

Navy-blue MC suit · camera-three instincts · co-hosted to a legend

Greets precisely, rehearses twice, and never mixes up camera two and camera three. Has no idea his new co-host’s morning included a corridor arrest he will never hear about — and by the time he might, the scene partner beside him will be the most famous face in the country.

Out of the chair, Park Bo-gum was already waiting.

The two bowed to each other in greeting.

Opening lines, run twice. Transition lines, walked once. Blocking and camera-switches given a light rehearsal.

Third time MCing, and it sat much easier on the hands.

At minimum, camera three and camera two no longer got confused.

❧ ❧ ❧

One p.m.

The artists had finished partial rehearsal; the live broadcast hadn’t begun.

Rookie groups carried signed albums around to pay respects to their seniors, managers exchanged cards and compliments in the corridors, and the greenroom became a small social exchange.

For people who liked socializing, a networking harvest.

For Irene, a large-scale physical-endurance event.

After roughly ten minutes she stood.

“I’m going to check whether Wendy has eaten.”

And out of the greenroom she went.

This was not social escape; this was teammate care — at least, that was the version she gave herself.

Irene, escaped, first went to the KBS canteen and had two lunch trays packed.

Then, plastic bag in hand, she headed for the recording studio serving ‘School 2015’.

The studio door was shut; through the glass, the inside was visible.

Wendy stood at the microphone in huge headphones, singing.

Irene watched from outside, the corner of her mouth curling into a distinctly my kid is going places maternal expression.

It occurred to her that Wendy had been at it long enough to get thirsty. She set the two lunch trays on the table outside the studio and turned to find the coin coffee machine.

❧ ❧ ❧

Inside the studio.

The producer pulled off his headphones and keyed the talkback.

“Wendy, take a break. We continue this afternoon.”

“Ye.”

Wendy removed the headphones and pushed out through the door.

Two lunch trays already sat on the table.

She asked the manager beside her:

“Oppa bought them?”

“Irene bought them.”

“Where is she?”

“Said she was going to get you something to drink.”

Wendy nodded.

She knew the leader’s social battery had probably died half an hour ago, and decided to go meet her partway.

Wendy followed the corridor toward the coin coffee machine.

She rounded a corner.

Her steps stopped.

By the coin coffee machine, Irene was indeed standing.

But she was not buying coffee.

She was standing in front of a man, winding her body in a manner of tremendous abstraction — the swing of her hips deviating wholesale from the aesthetic standards of every dance school currently operating.

Wendy squinted.

The man’s back looked vaguely familiar.

The next second, the man raised one hand, pinched the back collar of Irene’s jacket, pushed open a nearby door with the other, and threw her in.

The door shut.

The corridor regained its quiet.

The corridor, as processing the evidence

The coin coffee machine hummed on, and dispensed one americano into the paper-cup slot that nobody came to collect.

Wendy stood at the corner in silence for five seconds — and finally placed the back: Baek sunbae!

Her hand was in her pocket and on her phone before thought. Intelligence of this magnitude naturally belonged in the group chat, immediately.

Her head had even finished composing the message.

KakaoTalk · Red Velvet — the draft, never sent

Wendy

BREAKING: Irene unnie performed an abnormal dance for Baek Si-on sunbae in the KBS corridor and has been taken into custody.

Her thumb had just touched the screen when Wendy stopped.

If this went into the group.

Soo-young would reply with a hundred question marks within three seconds, then chase details until Irene herself surfaced to silence everyone.

Yeri would screenshot it and bank it as core blackmail assets for the next ten years.

Seulgi might not follow the point, but she would very likely join the other two in asking anyway.

And when Irene later worked out who had leaked, Wendy could plausibly be smothered with a pillow in her bunk some deep night.

Wendy seriously weighed team solidarity against personal survival.

The phone went back into the pocket.

She had seen nothing.

She had only come out to find her leader for lunch.

The leader had been temporarily carried off by someone else.

Cold lunch was not her responsibility.

Wendy collected the abandoned americano and walked back to the studio to sit down.

She opened her own tray.

“Weren’t you going to meet her? Where’s Irene?” the manager asked.

Wendy looked down, took a bite of rice, chewed twice.

“She might… have something else on.”

“Like what?”

Wendy thought of the abstract dance in the corridor.

Then of the door that had closed.

She answered with great caution:

“A private schedule.”

The manager: ???

❧ ❧ ❧

Kim Eun-sook was leafing through a sponsor’s quote sheet.

Lee Eung-bok sat across from her, marking shots on the storyboard sketches.

When the door pushed open, both looked up.

First in: a blonde girl.

Baek Si-on followed, closed the door behind him with a flick, and pointed at the corner of the room.

“Face the wall.”

Irene: “……”

“Normal people do not perform a man’s ten-year-old blacklist routine in front of him.”

Irene wanted to say it was six years — and reason stopped her.

Correcting the date would only prove how seriously she had done the archaeology.

So she shut her mouth and went to stand in the corner, facing the wall.

❧ ❧ ❧

Baek Si-on turned to Kim Eun-sook and Lee Eung-bok.

“Writer Kim, Director Lee — my apologies, a small trouble on the way. I’m late.”

Kim Eun-sook rose to shake his hand.

Lee Eung-bok stood too.

Both their gazes traveled over Baek Si-on’s shoulder and settled on the blonde girl in the corner, drawing circles on the wall.

“And this would be…?”

“A small episode.”

Baek Si-on pulled out a chair and sat.

“Pay her no mind.”

Kim Eun-sook’s eyes lingered on Irene one extra second. Hauled in by Baek Si-on to stand in a corner meant she knew him. Knowing him to this degree meant —

Kim Eun-sook elected not to finish the deduction.

She was a screenwriter, not a paparazzo.

The three took their seats.

The cooperation framework agreed by phone was simple. Baek Si-on’s cosmetics brand would become ‘Descendants of the Sun’’s designated lip-product sponsor: the female lead wearing the brand’s gloss on screen, the production cooperating on styling exposure and promo tie-ins, initial placement fee in the neighborhood of two hundred million won. As one of the exchange conditions, Baek Si-on would provide one personally-sung OST for the drama — fee and rights negotiated separately.

Kim Eun-sook was willing. The production company was willing. The network was willing.

But Song Hye-kyo was not.

The Song Hye-kyo route, as costed by production

Breach compensation — or a category exception — against her Laneige competitor agreement.

Short-term color-cosmetics commercial licensing from her agency, UAA.

Portrait and promotional-material usage rights, six months minimum.

The drama’s standard PPL placement fee.

Downstream add-ons: offline materials, promo shoots, launch-event appearances.

Total: roughly 1.2 billion won — against a planned 200 million. Sixfold.

❧ ❧ ❧

When Kim Eun-sook finished laying it out, she pushed an organized cost breakdown across the table.

Baek Si-on opened it and read it page by page.

The arithmetic, as run

Could he pay 1.2 billion won? Easily. Company cash sat above eight million dollars, with publishing income around three million a month — and once ‘See You Again’ entered its settlement period, four to five.

The problem: at Cosmax’s manufacturing quote, one billion won produced roughly five hundred thousand finished glosses. Five hundred thousand units — for an unlaunched brand with no public name, that was enough ammunition to fight a nationwide sales campaign outright.

He didn’t lack the money, and he’d gladly pay a premium for a key resource. But paying a premium was one thing; footing a mature brand’s competitor-exit bill and a star agency’s licensing process was another.

Measured as won-per-won-reaching-a-consumer, this route lost, catastrophically, to spending the same money on product and channels.

Kim Eun-sook was, as advertised, one of the best writers of human nature in Korea.

The moment Baek Si-on closed the breakdown, she already knew the Song Hye-kyo road was functionally dead.

“If you’re not prepared to spend that,” she offered, “the expectation can come down a step.”

“Down to what?” Baek Si-on looked up.

“The second female lead.”

“Kim Ji-won-ssi?”

“Her.”

Kim Eun-sook sketched the case: young, beautiful, could act. The key point — almost no commercial bindings at present. A drama-internal lip sponsorship plus short promo tie-in would come in under one hundred million won.

With the original two hundred million placement fee, the total sat under three hundred million.

From 1.2 billion to three hundred million.

The price had turned friendly all at once.

Baek Si-on knew Kim Ji-won’s potential. Give the girl seven, eight years, and she really could carry an entire product line on one face and one role.

But the problem was the seven or eight years standing in the middle. The market would not wait for a rookie actress to grow into her explosion, and consumers would not open their wallets today because an actress might be famous later.

A cosmetics brand’s first product needed to detonate immediately.

“Understood. Let me think it over.”

In the corner, Irene had been upgraded from circles to squares.

Kim Eun-sook made an acknowledging noise.

“No rush. We don’t roll cameras until next month — you have time to think.”

Baek Si-on stood, shook hands with Kim Eun-sook and Lee Eung-bok, and walked to the corner.

“Let’s go.”

Irene’s fingers were still resting on her half-finished square.

She turned around. Kim Eun-sook and Lee Eung-bok stood by the table.

Irene straightened her back and bowed to a full ninety degrees.

“Sorry for the interruption.”

Kim Eun-sook reflexively returned “thank you for your hard work” — and heard, only after it left her mouth, that something about the register was wrong.

Lee Eung-bok nodded and said nothing.

He still did not fully understand by what right Baek Si-on could make a sitting KBS MC stand in a corner, obediently, for ten minutes.

Irene straightened up and hurried after Baek Si-on, who was already out the door.

Before leaving, she even remembered to draw the door softly shut behind her, one-handed.

❧ ❧ ❧

The corridor was, at present, empty.

Irene trailed two steps behind him, eyes on the back of his head, trying to read from shoulder angle and stride rhythm whether he was angry.

Conclusion: unreadable.

Without turning his head, Baek Si-on spoke suddenly:

“You like dancing?”

Irene’s steps stuttered a beat.

The aim of the question was blurry.

The question, as parsed

Reading one: the literal profession — dancing, the idol job?

Reading two: sarcasm — the antique cover act just performed in the corridor?

Given that the voice carried neither amusement nor anger, she selected the safest available counter-strategy. Silence.

Baek Si-on walked on a few steps.

Behind him, the quiet stretched like a length of trailing air.

He tilted his head and swept a glance backward.

Irene was studying the fire-evacuation map on the wall with total scholarly absorption.

Baek Si-on withdrew his gaze and took out his phone.

“What’s your email address?”

Irene hadn’t predicted this turn.

But she gave it.

Baek Si-on looked down and typed for half a minute.

Irene’s phone buzzed once in her pocket.

She fished it out and opened the mail.

The body carried a cloud-drive link. She tapped through — archives, video files, and a row of English-named folders, packed dense down the screen.

Her thumb swiped three times. The attachment list did not end.

“What is this?”

“Homework.”

“Whose homework?”

“Mine.”

Irene moved her thumb off the screen and raised her head.

“Then why is it sent to me?”

Baek Si-on’s voice sounded entirely matter-of-course.

“You like dancing, don’t you?”

He tipped his chin at her phone.

“Learn this.”

Irene looked back down and re-audited the contents of the email.

It was true that when she’d imitated the A’ST1 antique stage just now, she had been having a wonderful time.

But that wonderful time rested on one very specific premise: the dance was simple, the moves were abstract, the purpose was mocking Baek Si-on — and none of it required actual training.

What was in this email had left the recreational zone of “liking dancing” far behind.

Irene conducted a fast, serious internal audit.

Audit finding: she might not, in fact, like dancing all that much.

At minimum, not enough to do remote homework on Baek Si-on’s behalf.

“I only dance because it’s the job.” She attempted a rearguard action.

“That just now — also the job?”

Irene: “……”

The sentence had precisely sealed every exit.

She could hardly say that was to watch your face when you found your blacklist being performed — it would only sound worse.

❧ ❧ ❧

They kept walking down the corridor.

Irene’s brain was still engineering her escape from the training-video pile when her stomach produced an unmistakable “gr-o-w-l.”

Baek Si-on stopped.

Irene’s face did not move a single muscle.

“That wasn’t me.”

Baek Si-on’s gaze made a full sweep of the corridor.

Apart from the two of them, it contained no third living thing.

The alibi, as evaluated

The vending machine stood twenty meters behind them.

Distance: disqualifying. The machine was legally incapable of taking the blame.

He withdrew his gaze.

“How long is your break?”

Irene hesitated a beat.

“Two hours. I have to be back with ‘Music Bank’ this afternoon — I can’t vanish too close to the live broadcast.”

Baek Si-on took out his phone and dialed.

It was picked up on the first ring.

Baek Si-on ↔ Baek Eun-ah · voice · one ring · requirements, confirmed

“Book a restaurant near KBS.”

Baek Eun-ah’s voice came faintly through.

“Two.”

After those two words, the other end went quiet. She perhaps wanted to ask with whom — and in the end didn’t. She only confirmed the requirements, one by one: close to KBS, quiet, a private room, fast kitchen, in and out inside two hours.

He hung up.

Irene stood to the side, and a thought abruptly arrived.

Wendy.

And the two lunch trays.

The food she’d packed was still sitting on the table outside the recording studio.

“Not hungry?”

Baek Si-on had stopped at the end of the corridor, angled half around.

Irene looked at him.

She should, by rights, be heading back to the studio to eat tray-to-tray with Wendy. She should, by rights, be returning to the ‘Music Bank’ backstage to discharge the social obligations of a new MC. Above all, she should be thinking hard about why performing one six-year-old dance-festival routine had resulted in the forced acquisition of an entire tap-training curriculum.

But her feet had already gone.

First at a normal stride.

Two steps in, slightly faster.

Approaching his side, deliberately slower again — wearing the expression of someone who was not hurrying.

Baek Si-on pressed the elevator button.

Irene stood at his right, eyes on the brushed metal of the elevator doors.

Two silhouettes floated in the reflection.

One tall. One short.

The elevator doors, as deposed

The short one was working very hard to pretend she was not, in any measurable way, happy.

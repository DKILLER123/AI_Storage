Chapter Fifty-Five

Four Days on the Song Assembly Line

July 26 Evening Hongdae → Hapjeong-dong, by cab A song, a secret, and a studio door

The cab swung off the Hongdae entrance up onto Yanghwa Road.

Baek Eun-ah sat in the back seat, fingers clicking across her screen, mouth never still, typing and muttering at the same time:

“Scooter Braun is a manager, not an agent… that translation is wrong…”

Baek Si-on leaned against the passenger seat and watched the scenery out the window.

Hongdae's streets scrolling by frame by frame. Coffee shops, BBQ places, nail salons, karaoke rooms. Signs crowding against signs, each one shouting "look at me, look at me" in the biggest font available, the pavement full of students out for the summer holiday.

He wasn't looking at any of it.

He was thinking about the song.

Writing for Justin Bieber.

That sentence, put in any Korean musician's mouth, was enough to brag about for a lifetime.

But Baek Si-on's brain skipped every step of sentiment and excitement and landed straight on the most practical question.

Which one do I copy?

On the original timeline, Bieber had released an album in 2015.

That album was the turning point of his career. It flipped him from a globally mocked "problem child" back into a "musical genius."

The album had a few hits in it.

But the single that genuinely defined it — the watershed that pushed tropical house into the mainstream — there was only one.

What Do You Mean?

After it, pop singers around the world all started stuffing steel-drum tones and tropical grooves into their arrangements.

Baek Si-on closed his eyes and began to recall.

The melody came back first.

A bright, bouncing synthetic timbre, very grainy, like someone rapidly plucking a row of wooden xylophone keys with their fingertips.

Then the words.

What were the lyrics about?

A "what do you mean" inside a relationship.

She says yes, then she says no. She says she misses you, then she won't pick up the phone. She says let's keep going, then she suddenly pushes you away.

Every sentence carries two meanings. Every glance points in the opposite direction.

Like a driver seeing a red light and a green light at the same time.

So you ask her.

What do you mean?

That was the core of this song.

Baek Si-on ran that feeling through his head twice, confirming he hadn't gotten it wrong.

The cab turned into a narrow alley.

Eun-ah's muttering drifted up from behind:

“……so is it that he sends it over when it's done, or he flies over to record…”

Baek Si-on ignored her.

He pulled the door open, and the image was still turning in his head.

July 26 Evening Hapjeong-dong, the five-storey old commercial building, 4th floor Four hundred and one, and a roll of a chair

Hapjeong-dong.

The five-storey old commercial-residential building looked just the same as ever. The grey-green exterior paint had shed another layer under the summer sun, and more of the raw cement underneath had shown through than last time.

The tailor's shop on the first floor was still closed, its roller shutter wearing a fresh line of graffiti that nobody could read.

401.

Baek Si-on knocked three times.

From inside came the sound of a chair rolling.

Then footsteps.

The door opened.

Jung Jae-joon stood in the doorway.

His ash-blond hair had grown another stretch, and the black at the roots had taken over more than a third of the territory. The whole hair colour was doing this "can't be bothered to touch up the dye" gradient.

He looked at Baek Si-on, then at Eun-ah behind him.

“Baek boss.”

“Teacher Jung.”

They bumped fists.

Jung Jae-joon stepped aside and nodded toward the room.

“Get in here.”

The room was still a mess. Baek Si-on stepped over the cables on the floor and sat down in the folding chair. Eun-ah, of her own accord, went to find the chair she'd sat in last time.

Jung Jae-joon closed the door. No water, no small talk. He pulled his swivel chair up and sat facing Baek Si-on directly.

“You've got a direction?”

“Yeah.”

“Concept?”

“The push-and-pull inside a relationship. Every sentence she says has two meanings, every move points the wrong way. So you ask her — what do you mean?”

“What's the emotional core?”

“Addiction. The more you don't understand, the more you want to. The more you want to, the less you can leave. A sweet confusion.”

Jung Jae-joon nodded, asked no more, and tilted his chin toward the booth.

“Let's start.”

“Mm.”

In the corner, Eun-ah sat in her folding chair, notes app open, not one word written down.

She was thinking about something else.

Half an hour before, in Kim Jae-kyung's dorm, she'd heard Baek Si-on say with her own ears — "remember, chasing after a thing doesn't make a deal."

He'd delivered it with such effortless calm.

And then, not even five minutes out the door, he'd cabbed straight over to Hapjeong-dong, sat down, and before his back was warm, the whole direction and concept for the song was laid out.

Two-faced.

Expressionless, she typed two characters into her notes, then deleted them.

If anyone ever saw that, it'd be over.

July 26 → July 30 Four days Hapjeong-dong, studio 401 The skeleton, the flesh, the voice, the final mix

Humming it through once, they started building the skeleton.

Chord progression, section structure, BPM.

Baek Si-on described the picture; Jung Jae-joon translated the picture into sound.

The way the two of them worked was the same as last time they'd made Way Back Home. Intuition, meeting intuition.

Eun-ah recorded it all in her folding chair, her notes swinging wildly from "what emotion for the verse" to "delivery — fried chicken or tteokbokki?"

Day one · the skeleton

BPM~120 · mid-tempo tropical groove

StructureIntro → Verse → Pre → Chorus → Verse 2 → Chorus → Bridge → Final

Keyopening up — bright, bouncing, a lot of air

Direction: “A question somebody keeps asking, and a beat that won't let them stop.”

Day two, filling in the flesh.

Arrangement details began stacking up layer by layer.

Kick, snare, hi-hat, bass, pad. Every time a track came in, Baek Si-on would close his eyes and listen for ten-odd seconds, then give the most precise direction in the most layman's language:

“This beat's too obedient. Like a good student. I want the feeling of someone who's late to class and still has the nerve to act like he's got every right to be.”

Jung Jae-joon paused for a second, then changed the hi-hat pattern from straight to syncopated.

Right.

Day three, recording the vocal.

Last time recording Way Back Home had taken ages. This time it was much faster.

Baek Si-on already had control over his voice. He knew which register to push, where to hold back, how much breathiness to let in.

Jung Jae-joon listened to the first complete take in the control room, leaned back in his chair, looked at Baek Si-on behind the mic.

He said nothing.

Just gave a thumbs-up.

Day four.

Mixing, mastering, final tweaks.

Jung Jae-joon did that part alone.

Baek Si-on and Eun-ah arrived at noon, bringing jjajangmyeon, two packs of kimbap, and fried chicken.

Day 4 — mix + master · demo

Rule the session found: chase a deal and you lose it; make the thing first and the deal finds you.

Jung Jae-joon played the demo from the top all the way through.

Three minutes twenty-eight seconds.

The synth pluck tone bounced out of the very first note, like a rubber ball hitting a glass table eight times in a row. The kick pushed low and dry, the bass line moving along the very bottom, not stealing the show but holding up the whole weight of the space.

When the chorus came in, Baek Si-on's voice climbed a fourth from the low register and landed on "What do you mean."

He didn't hold the tail note. Just left it there to let the reverb dissolve on its own.

Like a man who really was asking a question.

It ended.

Jung Jae-joon got up from the mixing chair, walked to the fridge, took out three cans of cola, tossed one to Baek Si-on, one to Eun-ah.

He cracked his own and took a sip.

“This one was harder than the last,” he said.

Baek Si-on caught the cola.

“But?”

“But better.”

“So we can put it out?”

“Yeah.”

Four days.

The skeleton, the flesh, and the demo vocal of a song, all of it finished.

What was left now was up to that Canadian on the other side of the ocean to decide.

“Good work.”

Jung Jae-joon didn't turn around, his fingers still on the keyboard.

“Just moving a finger or two.”

The exact same thing he'd said last time.

Chapter Ninety-One

The Reason She Fell Asleep on Your Shoulder

September 12 7:40 pm Hapjeong-dong · a black Kia Carnival A passenger seat, a “whatever,” and a treasure shop with a wind-bell

The black Kia Carnival.

Choi Jin-ri sat in the passenger seat, pulled the belt across and clicked it, then rubbed both feet on the mat twice until she found a position that would do.

“What do you want to eat? Hotpot? Barbecue? Japanese?”

“Whatever.”

Baek Si-on’s “whatever” was a genuine whatever.

On the matter of food, his only filter was whether there was meat. If there was meat, every other condition could be compromised.

“Ummm…”

Choi Jin-ri’s fingers tapped twice on her knee.

Five seconds of thinking.

“Then how about I take you to a treasure shop? Hara eonni and I go there a lot.”

“Navigate.”

She fished a phone from her pocket, opened the map, typed an address, and slotted the phone into the mount above the dash.

The blue arrow started to blink.

“In five hundred metres, turn left.”

Baek Si-on followed the voice.

Out of Hapjeong, through a few narrow lanes in Seongbuk-gu, up onto the inner loop, then down a street he had not used much.

About twenty minutes.

When the map said they were near the destination, he glanced out the window.

A Chinese restaurant.

The front was not large. The sign was red with gold characters, a line of small Hangul translation stuck underneath.

Two pots of pothos by the door, leaves grown fat enough to trail onto the step.

“Here?”

“Mm!”

He parked at the kerb, pulled the handbrake, killed the engine.

They got out.

The wind-bell on the door went once, a cheap bright ding.

From the kitchen came the smell of wok oil, and inside it the particular sting of Sichuan pepper and dried chilli after they had hit hot fat — numb, fragrant, a little violent.

Baek Si-on’s nasal cavity took the signal.

The rating was: promising.

✿ ✿ ✿

“Aigoo! Sulli’s here!”

Behind the counter stood a woman of about fifty, Chinese, a full smile, Korean with a heavy Dongbei accent.

“Ajumma!”

Choi Jin-ri went to the counter, smiling.

“You’ve lost weight. Dieting again? How many times have I told you, you can’t not eat—”

Halfway through the scolding, the woman’s gaze went over Choi Jin-ri’s shoulder and landed on the man standing in front of the menu wall, head tipped back, studying the dishes with great seriousness.

“Boyfriend?”

Choi Jin-ri looked back once.

He was frowning, sunk in a long think between the pictures of boiled beef in chilli oil and chilli chicken. The radar, in all likelihood, was not even on.

She turned back and shook her head at the auntie.

“Not yet.”

The auntie, old in the trade, caught the “yet” in the sentence. Her eyes narrowed to a line.

“Oh~~~~ Then fighting.”

Choi Jin-ri’s ear-tips went red, a short, fast stretch of it.

It was close to the red of that night in Venice, when Baek Si-on had pointed at her from a buffet table and sung “Oh pretty baby.”

Lighter, though.

About seventy percent.

“Ajumma! Is there a room free?”

She switched topics at speed.

“Yes yes yes. The inside one.”

The auntie grabbed two menus and led them in.

Frosted glass. A four-top. Plastic floral cloth. A toothpick pot and a bottle of Zhenjiang vinegar pinning down a corner.

Ice water for each of them.

Choi Jin-ri sat opposite, both hands around the cup, watching him turn the pages.

“Boiled beef in chilli oil, chilli chicken, sweet-and-sour pork, twice-cooked pork, hot-and-sour soup.”

He closed the menu. Less than ten seconds, total.

Choi Jin-ri blinked once.

“You’re not going to look at the vegetable side?”

“You order.”

She flipped.

“Then dry-fried green beans, and a potato shred with vinegar.”

She closed the menu and only then realised.

Seven dishes for two people seemed a little much.

Then she thought of Baek Si-on’s capacity, and the small guilt about wasting food let go of her.

Treasure shop · the table for two

Seongbuk-gu · red sign, gold letters, Dongbei Korean

Boiled beef in chilli oilthe bomb that rewrites the air

Chilli chickena sea-bowl, later a pile of shells

Sweet-and-sour pork · twice-cooked porkthe plates that would not survive him

Hot-and-sour soup · dry-fried beans · vinegar potatoher share, in theory

His filter: is there meat. If there is meat, the rest can be compromised.

Seven dishes. Two people. The auntie would later add rice, and still not understand the logic of “too salty.”

The auntie wrote it down in a rush of pen, and went out.

Baek Si-on took a sip of ice water.

“How’s Goo Hara lately?”

“Hara eonni? The group’s on a Japan tour. She sent me a rehearsal video a few days ago.”

Choi Jin-ri pulled her phone, scrolled the album, looking for it.

Baek Si-on waved it off.

“Don’t. As long as she’s doing well.”

She put the phone away.

“Are you still in touch with Kim Jae-kyung?”

He asked it as a second question, not as small talk. Choi Jin-ri nodded.

“We talked last night.”

“How is she?”

“A bit busy.”

There was a brightness in her voice, the kind you get when you are glad for a friend.

“Apparently because a piece ran saying she designed our red-carpet clothes, she got some exposure. OnStyle asked her to be a regular MC on Get It Beauty, opposite Yoo In-na eonni.”

She counted on her fingers.

“And she’s in an MBC one-act, Turning Point, a supporting part. They say she’ll cameo in It’s Okay, That’s Love too. And she’s still doing university festivals with the group.”

“Oh.”

Baek Si-on set the ice water down.

He had meant, after Venice, to call Kim Jae-kyung and ask her to come on full-time as the team’s visual director.

The handmade red-carpet work had already proved the level. Eye, fabric, execution — all of it held.

But looking at it now, her career had caught the red-carpet wind and started to come back. He put down the thought of cutting someone else’s road.

✿ ✿ ✿

The door opened.

The boiled beef came first.

A full sheet of red oil. Beef slices ghosting underneath, steam, a scatter of garlic and chilli that had been hit with hot oil, Sichuan peppercorns set into the surface one by one like studs.

The smell was a bomb. From the second the plate hit the cloth, the air in the room was rewritten.

Baek Si-on picked up chopsticks.

Fished a slice from under the oil.

Thin, large, edges slightly curled, a coat of red oil and garlic.

Into the mouth.

The numb of the pepper, the heat of the chilli, the sweetness of the beef, the salt of the sauce — four tastes going off at once, then fusing into a single thing that could not be taken apart, which was simply: good.

The chopsticks sped up.

From tasting to eating.

Choi Jin-ri put her chin in one hand and leaned on the table-edge, watching him.

When the chilli chicken arrived, the huge sea-bowl of boiled beef had nothing left but the bean sprouts and cabbage at the bottom.

When the sweet-and-sour pork landed, the chilli chicken that had been a full plate was a heap of precisely sorted chilli husks and peppercorns.

The auntie looked at Baek Si-on.

Looked at Choi Jin-ri’s untouched chopsticks.

Looked at Baek Si-on again.

A little disordered.

She took the empties in silence, set the sweet-and-sour down, and on the way out glanced back once.

Shut the door.

From outside, faintly, she shouted something toward the kitchen. Probably “Lao Zhang, extra.”

✿ ✿ ✿

When the auntie came back with the potato shred, Baek Si-on spoke.

“Ajumma. Two bowls of rice.”

He paused.

“The dishes are a bit salty.”

She looked at the table.

Six dishes.

Four already gone.

If food were truly too salty, a normal person’s reaction was to eat less, drink water, put the chopsticks down.

Not to finish four plates and then add two bowls of rice to balance the salt.

This logic…

She turned it once in her head.

Did not get through.

“All right. Two bowls, coming.”

Choi Jin-ri finally could not hold it, and laughed out.

“What are you laughing at?”

His chopsticks did not stop. His head did not lift.

“Nothing.”

She picked up chopsticks at last, took a piece of twice-cooked pork that had not yet been eliminated, and chewed twice.

Good.

She took another.

“Leave me some.”

His chopsticks hung in the air for a tenth of a second.

“No guarantees.”

She glared.

Sped up.

Could not outrun him.

She took one. He took three.

She took two. He took five.

The twice-cooked pork went toward its end at a speed you could watch.

They cleaned the table.

Strictly speaking, five and a half plates were Baek Si-on. Choi Jin-ri contributed three slices of twice-cooked pork, four green beans, two chopsticks of potato, and two spoons of soup.

At the counter the auntie was already hammering a calculator.

“Forty-eight thousand won, total.”

Choi Jin-ri passed a card. The auntie slotted it, and while the slip came, her mouth did not rest.

“Good eye, Sulli.”

Her gaze went past the counter to the back outside, the man looking at his phone, and she dropped her voice.

“A man who can eat has a thick foundation.”

Choi Jin-ri kept the smile. She had a faint sense that the topic was sliding toward a place she could not steer.

“Thick foundation means a strong fire. A man with a strong fire has endless stamina at night.”

Choi Jin-ri was not some first-year who understood nothing.

On the contrary.

She understood every word.

The machine spat the slip. She snatched slip and card, did not even sign, and bowed at the auntie with extreme perfunctoriness.

“Thank you, ajumma, bye, ajumma!”

Then she turned, jogged, pushed the door. The wind-bell went once.

Baek Si-on was on the step, phone already in his pocket. He watched her come out as if something were chasing her.

“What is it?”

“Nothing, let’s go, let’s go.”

She went around him, pulled the passenger door, got in, shut it, belted — one motion.

He stood on the step and looked at her for two seconds.

Looked back at the restaurant door.

Behind it, the auntie was on the counter, waving at him, smiling.

He did not think it through. Driver’s side. Belt. Ignition.

Choi Jin-ri sat with both hands on her knees, face to the window, not looking at him.

The back of her neck was still red.

He pulled off the kerb onto the main road.

“How much was dinner?”

“Ah?”

She finally turned. The flush had not fully gone.

“You paid. How much?”

“Why?”

“My mom said, when you eat with a girl, you pay.”

She looked at the side of his face.

Streetlights going over it in cuts, light, dark, light.

A few seconds.

“Then take me to a movie.”

“Now?”

“Now.”

He glanced at the clock on the dash.

Eight forty-five p.m.

One hand on the wheel, the other on the nav, he searched nearby cinemas.

The nearest was two kilometres.

September 12 9:10 pm Yongsan · CGV, screen 4, row G Lucy, a travel toothbrush, and a head that kept tipping left

The Carnival went into the mall’s underground.

Lift to the top. CGV.

Choi Jin-ri stood in front of the ticket screen, hands folded, watching him scroll the list.

“What do you want to see?”

“I’m easy.”

His finger stopped.

Lucy.

The 9:10.

“This.”

She tipped her head at the poster.

Scarlett Johansson’s face took most of the screen. Short gold hair, a cold eye, black leather. The whole frame said she could tear physics with her hands.

“You like this kind?”

“Choi Min-sik sunbae is in it.”

Her mouth moved.

Fine.

She was not sure what the point of her question had been. His answer had taken it somewhere extremely safe.

Tickets done.

He scanned the lobby. Toilets at the end of the left corridor. At the corner, a white vending machine labelled “life convenience,” toothbrushes, gum, mouthwash, plasters, hair ties.

He bought two travel brush kits.

The kind with mint already on them, short handle, hard head, the feel of brushing with chopsticks. This was not a time to be particular.

He tore the plastic. One each.

He took his into the men’s.

Two middle-aged men at the mirror, done washing. He waited, then went to the basin, a soybean of paste, two minutes, three rinses, a breath into his palm.

Acceptable.

When he came out she had the mask and cap on, waiting by the machine.

They looked at each other’s disguise in the corridor.

“Go.”

At the concession he turned in for an ice cola. She took a water from the fridge. He paid on the phone before she could say she would.

“Go.”

“…Oh.”

Side by side to the ticket-tear. A part-time boy of eighteen or nineteen, a streak of yellow in his hair, phone down. Baek Si-on held out the tickets. The boy glanced, tore, did not lift his head.

“Screen four, left, all the way.”

“Thanks.”

Dark carpet. Poster lightboxes. Screen four’s door open, ads on the glass.

He stood aside to let her in first.

Under thirty percent full. Their seats mid-back, row G, empty on both sides, nearest neighbour five seats off.

He sat, slotted the cola. She sat on his right, hands on her knees, quiet for the start.

Lights down.

Three trailers.

Then the picture.

Luc Besson’s camera came in hard. Fast cut, clean. Scarlett Johansson’s Lucy kidnapped in Taipei, a packet of blue synthetic in the belly.

Baek Si-on watched properly. The first thirty minutes he barely moved. He forgot the cola.

When Choi Min-sik came on, Si-on’s body tipped a little forward.

Professional instinct.

He was watching how a Korean actor in a Hollywood system switched the emotional state between English and Korean, how the smallest unit of face carried threat and layer.

Choi Min-sik’s role was a Taipei gang boss.

Baek Si-on’s eyebrow moved on that fact.

A Taipei gang boss who spoke Korean?

Had Luc Besson failed to tell East Asian faces apart? Had casting gone by craft and not nationality? Or was a French director’s idea of Asian geopolitics just a pot of porridge?

Three seconds.

Not important.

If Choi Min-sik was good, that was enough.

And he was good. Every scene pulled to the limit. Especially the interrogation — calm to loss of control in under two seconds, the eye-change as precise as a scalpel.

Baek Si-on put a note on Choi Min-sik sunbae, in his head.

If there was a chance, he would ask in person.

Mid-film. The drug in Lucy leaking, brain use climbing off ten percent. The picture more abstract, montage denser, big-bang effects cutting with cell division. Besson was not content with a sci-fi actioner; he wanted time, life, inheritance, being.

Baek Si-on found it interesting.

Told slower, it might have been a masterpiece. The commercial frame limited the philosophy, like putting five litres in a two-litre bottle; the overflow just confused people.

Not important.

If it was good to watch, that was enough.

He lifted the cola and drank.

Choi Jin-ri beside him was not so in.

The first half-hour she followed. By the kidnapping her lids had started to fight. A dark room, a kind temperature, a continuous bed of sound — the body’s sleep system starts itself. A cinema is all of that.

Her lids closed once.

Opened.

On the screen Lucy was waking, particles spreading from her body.

Closed again. Longer by two seconds.

Open.

Shut.

Open.

Shut.

The frequency slowing.

Her head began to tip left, tip left, and at last came to rest, lightly, on Baek Si-on’s shoulder.

He turned.

In the weak cold light off the screen, her face was fitted into the hollow of his shoulder, seam to seam.

He looked at her for a few seconds, and put his eyes back on the glass.

The numbers still jumping.

Twenty percent.

Thirty.

Forty.

Ninety-nine.

A hundred.

Lucy vanished.

Became everything. Time, space, matter, mind. She was everywhere.

Credits.

Lights.

On Baek Si-on’s right shoulder, Choi Jin-ri was still asleep, a shallow curve at the corner of her mouth.

He did not know what she was dreaming.

He did not call her.

He waited a while.

Until the cleaners came in with brooms, and then he moved the right shoulder, lightly.

“The film’s over.”

Her lid jumped.

Opened slowly.

Three seconds to know where she was.

Then she knew her face was on his shoulder, and sat up hard.

“I fell asleep?”

“From when Lucy started using one percent of her brain.”

“…That early?”

“To be exact, the third minute of the scene where Scarlett Johansson is tied to the chair.”

“You remember that clearly?”

“Because your hair was poking my neck. It itched.”

She touched her hair on instinct, tucked the loose bits behind an ear.

“Was it good?”

“Who?”

“…The film.”

“It was good.”

“What did I miss that was good?”

He stood, took the empty cup from the holder.

“You missed Choi Min-sik sunbae’s performance.”

She pressed her mouth, a little ashamed.

“Next time I meet Choi Min-sik sunbae, I’ll tell him an actor named Choi Jin-ri fell asleep watching a film he was in.”

Her eyes went wide at once.

“And I’ll add, she started sleeping three minutes before you came on, so strictly speaking she didn’t fall asleep watching you. She gave up while waiting for you.”

“Don’t you dare!”

“That way sunbae should feel a bit better. It wasn’t his scenes that hypnotised her. It was Luc Besson’s rhythm.”

“Baek Si-on!”

“Of course, if sunbae asks who this Choi Jin-ri is, I’ll answer honestly: the actress who said at the Venice press conference, ‘Stand opposite him and catch it.’ On a set she can catch my emotion. In a cinema she couldn’t catch Luc Besson.”

“…”

It had been a romantic beat.

She had slept on his shoulder for a whole film.

He had not woken her, had not even changed position, and had only moved when the broom came in.

In any Korean drama, that was the kind of thing the heroine later remembered face-down in a duvet, rolling.

After his mouth had worked it — hair poking the neck, asleep from one percent, I’m telling Choi Min-sik sunbae — the romance had been taken apart, diluted, and wrapped in a humour you could not answer except by standing there blank.

Was this man doing it on purpose, or was he born like this?

Choi Jin-ri leaned toward the former.

She had no proof.

“Are you going to sit a second screening?”

He was at the stair-mouth, looking back.

She came back to herself, stood, took the water, walked up fast.

Behind him, her step slowed.

“If you say those things to Choi Min-sik sunbae.”

His feet did not stop.

“I’ll tell people that song of yours was written for yourself.”

His feet stopped.

He turned.

She stood half a step behind him.

Just-woken eyes still a little fogged, the content in them not fogged at all.

A threat.

Naked.

I will become a legend. My name will be passed down through the ages.

If those lines were made public, and confirmed as Baek Si-on’s midnight love-letter to himself rather than some blood-warming collective fight-song, the social death would be about the size of throwing the Volpi Cup into the Han.

He looked at her.

She looked at him.

Five seconds under the corridor lights.

“Mutual secrecy?”

The corner of her mouth bent.

“Mutual secrecy.”

She put out her right hand.

Little finger up.

He looked down at that finger, then up at her face.

“Childish.”

“Mm. Childish.”

The little finger did not come back.

It stayed there, hooked in the air between them.

“…”

He put out his right hand.

Little finger hooked.

Her thumb came over his, and pressed once.

“Stamp.”

Chapter Two Hundred Thirty-Four

Legends Never Die

The schedule negotiation between Baek Si-on and Baek Jeong-hoon failed, without even a moment of suspense.

The former arrived holding ‘Sado’ publicity dates and asked for time next week for the internal screening and the distribution meetings.

The latter held the ‘The Three Evils’ shooting plan and asked exactly one question:

“You leave, and who kills the two hundred believers in the church?”

Baek Si-on tried to compress his scenes instead.

Baek Jeong-hoon countered:

“You plan to kill all two hundred in a single day?”

“In the movie, that works.”

“On this crew, it doesn’t.”

“......”

At that point, the uncle-nephew talks had basically exhausted their room to develop.

In the end, Baek Si-on did not get his full leave.

The single concession he extracted: video meetings during the lunch break, from the nanny van.

The condition: be back in the makeup trailer before the afternoon call.

❧ ❧ ❧

Lunch break · the blinds drawn

One laptop on a fold-down tray · one believer’s white robe

The nanny van · the video conference

Lunch break.

The van’s blackout curtains fully closed.

Baek Si-on propped his laptop on the fold-down tray as the conferencing software connected.

He still wore the believer’s white robe of ‘The Three Evils’ church line — that morning’s scene had been the male lead newly arrived at the church, led by the faithful into the collective life.

The feed connected.

Several windows arranged on the screen.

Director Lee Joon-ik sat dead center.

The distribution chief occupied another pane; the publicity team and the overseas-release lead filled the rest.

Lee Joon-ik took one look at Baek Si-on’s costume.

“You look worse off than Sado did dead.”

Baek Si-on adjusted the camera angle slightly.

“Uncles do tend to express their art at the nephew’s expense.”

Laughter rolled through the windows.

Lee Joon-ik was amused too — the line could be charged to Baek Jeong-hoon just as fairly as to Yeongjo in ‘Sado’.

Korean cinema’s use of its young Best Actor had always run on the principle of full utilization.

Lee Joon-ik didn’t linger on the costume.

“Postproduction is locked. Sound, color, subtitles — all final. Distribution is ready to start the publicity cycle.”

The distributor pushed the draft plan onto the shared screen.

The campaign, as tabled

July 3 — first concept poster and teaser reveal.

July 10 — full publicity cycle begins.

Late July — press conference, media screenings, core long-form interviews.

Around August 2 — nationwide theatrical release.

First week: Seoul, Gyeonggi, Busan, Daegu — twelve stage greetings in total.

The distribution chief spoke first.

“We’d like actor Baek at every key first-week event.”

Baek Si-on turned a page of the schedule.

“Press conference: yes.”

“Media screenings: yes.”

“Two or three long-form interviews can be arranged, and the twelve stage greetings stay on the current route.”

The publicity lead followed up:

“And variety?”

Baek Si-on skimmed the show proposals. ‘Infinite Challenge’. Talk shows. ‘Running Man’.

“Song Kang-ho-sunbae and I will do interviews — film-channel deep-cuts are fine.”

“But we will not be playing games, guessing songs, or dancing in Sado costumes on a variety stage.”

Lee Joon-ik nodded from his window.

“Cut that category.”

The distribution chief still mourned the ratings reach.

Lee Joon-ik added:

“If you can get Song Kang-ho in Yeongjo robes dancing girl-group choreography, I have no objection.”

The distribution chief quietly withdrew the variety list.

That project’s risk profile had been judged too high.

The remaining nodes settled fast — and then came a knock on the van door, the assistant’s voice through the metal:

“Actor Baek — the director says: is the meeting finished?”

Baek Si-on glanced at the clock in the corner of the screen.

“How much of the lunch break is left?”

“The director says it ended five minutes ago.”

Lee Joon-ik, hurrying him with a smile:

“Go on. Your uncle’s film matters.”

“Director Lee, your film matters too.”

“Which is why you’re only down for twelve.”

“So the twenty-seven was your idea?”

“Signal’s bad.”

Lee Joon-ik’s window closed on the spot.

The rest went black in a hurry.

Whether the distribution side was any good at handling press remained unverified — but at escaping a lead’s accountability, they were already highly trained.

❧ ❧ ❧

June wore down toward its end, and ‘The Three Evils’ moved into the church stage of the shoot.

The male lead, a wanted man, had fled to the south of the country, believing he had finally found a place where he could wait for death in peace.

He put on the white robe. Ate the temple food. Prayed with the believers.

Briefly believed the world really did contain a place that could wash every sin away.

Baek Jeong-hoon shot the passage with unbearable gentleness. Sunlight falling through stained glass. Hymns soft as cotton. Nobody telling the male lead that he had walked into the greatest evil in the film.

Off camera, Baek Si-on had assumed the next things to reach him would be LAVUE’s restock figures, the ‘Sado’ teaser poster, or the first cut of Lee Joon-ik’s preview.

None of the three came.

What came first was a transatlantic call from Scooter Braun, in Los Angeles.

Baek Si-on’s first assumption: he was being pushed to return to America.

“I haven’t wrapped.”

Scooter audibly froze.

“I know.”

“Then what are you chasing me for?”

“...I’m calling about a license.”

“Which song?”

“‘Jumpshot’.”

Baek Si-on → Scooter Braun · voice · Los Angeles on the line

‘Jumpshot’ had not replicated the chart-sprinting speed of his earlier singles. At release it lived as Curry’s private celebration music at Oracle Arena, its circulation locked to the NBA’s orbit. What changed the song’s fate was the Finals themselves — the Warriors took the title, the household rose with the champion, and after three weeks of compounding, ‘Jumpshot’ climbed to № 7 on the Billboard Hot 100 in the final June chart.

Scooter laid out the whole chain of the offer. Universal Pictures was about to release the animated film ‘Minions’. The production team had noticed that the processed female voice in the ‘Jumpshot’ chorus — high, sharp, and just beyond intelligibility — sat remarkably close to the Minions’ own babble. Four chorus lines, practically engineered for a crowd of yellow capsule bodies in dungarees to bounce to. Universal wanted a three-month global promotional license: web shorts, social tie-ins, TV spots in select territories, premiere warm-ups, and official animation shorts. For Universal: an NBA champion and a hot Billboard track pulling audiences toward the film. For ‘Jumpshot’: the Minions’ global release carrying the song to millions who had never watched a possession in their lives. Mutual win.

“Done.”

“Do you need to ask the IRENE lady’s opinion first?”

“I’ll tell her myself.”

The Minions license closed, Scooter rolled straight into the second item.

“There’s also an invitation.”

“Who?”

“Riot Games.”

“S5?”

“Mm.”

The previous collaboration had carried a host-country’s color — the finals in Seoul, Baek Si-on the Korean artist with the sharpest international climb. Riot’s choice then had balanced the home market against global reach. Season 5 was different: the circuit stretched across Europe, the finals bound for Berlin. And Riot still sent the invitation to him. Geography had left the core of the equation; what they wanted to borrow now was the name itself.

Scooter opened Riot’s term sheet and read the core conditions aloud.

The Riot term sheet, as read

Songwriting and master rights: Baek Si-on, in full.

Riot receives exclusive worldwide promotional use for the S5 cycle — game client, tournament broadcasts, documentary, official social accounts.

After the event: perpetual archive retention; no standalone sale or re-licensing of the master outside the S5 project.

Offer: three million — covering production, event rights, and the finals-stage performance. First demo due before mid-July.

The call ended; the email alert popped instantly.

Baek Si-on opened the attachment and read from the rights scope to the price page.

Three million dollars.

“Dollars?” Baek Si-on was a little surprised.

Production, event rights, and the finals performance — all inside one number.

He screenshotted the price page.

Opened Kim Eun-sook’s chat window.

Image, sent.

One line under it:

“When I last quoted you — the production buyout for one song was five hundred thousand dollars. In hindsight, that quote of mine was rather conservative.”

Sent.

Chat closed.

As for when Kim Eun-sook would discover that her two hundred million won had bought a friendship price whose value was still climbing — that was her affair. A great writer processes the fates of that many characters every day; she could surely process one invoice’s worth of feelings too.

❧ ❧ ❧

Baek Si-on reopened Riot’s project folder.

Page one: the visual concept of the Season 5 world finals.

Reading, he found himself back at another image from the previous year’s finals.

Lee Sang-hyeok, the S3 champion captain, had come to the S4 opening ceremony — stood there staring at a stage that should have been his own, both eyes full of hunger.

And then Kobe’s voice surfaced from memory.

“This league doesn’t belong to old-school shooting guards like us anymore.”

“After next season — retirement.”

One man temporarily off the throne, waiting to climb back.

One man at the end of the road, preparing to say goodbye to his era.

Esports and basketball. A competitor barely into his twenties, and a veteran of a thousand battles. Two figures with nothing in common, pulled together by the same thing.

❝

Legends lose. Legends age, fall, sit in the gold rain that belongs to someone else — and admit, when the young shoot the deep three they don’t believe in, that the era has changed. But a legend does not lose his name to one loss, one farewell ceremony, or one statement.

— the idea, arriving all at once: Legends Never Die

Baek Si-on’s fingers settled on the tabletop, tapping softly along a rhythm only he could hear.

The line fit the world championship. It fit Kobe’s farewell tour just as well.

He could even picture it: Kobe’s last game finished, the Staples Center lights going out, the crowd refusing to leave. The man who had worn Lakers purple and gold for twenty years standing at center court. Music rising out of the dark. Baek Si-on stepping onto the floor and singing the first line.

❝

The old man who had been too proud to cry his whole life would very possibly cry uglier than he had on the night he lifted his fifth trophy.

— the fantasy reel, filed under creative side benefits

A picture with real entertainment value.

Though the song’s principal purpose remained the celebration of legends. Observing Kobe’s tears would count strictly as a creation-adjacent perk.

Same week · ‘Descendants of the Sun’ on set

One flashback before a wedding · one counter, two tubes

The makeup-boutique interior · the built set

On the ‘Descendants of the Sun’ set, the day’s shoot was the flashback before Yoon Myung-joo and Seo Dae-young attend his ex-girlfriend’s wedding.

The location: a cosmetics boutique interior built by the art department.

Bae Joo-hyun wore the red-and-black beauty-counter uniform, hair up, a silver name tag at her chest with the brand mark pressed under the name. Two gloss tubes waited at her station.

Kim Ji-won, playing Yoon Myung-joo, was already seated at the counter.

They walked the lines and the business once, confirming camera positions and the angles for the hand close-ups.

The slate fell.

“Action.”

Kim Ji-won tapped the counter once, lightly, checking her mirror.

Bae Joo-hyun came over from the display shelf.

“Hello — would you like help with a shade?”

“Mm.”

“A date?”

“A wedding.”

Bae Joo-hyun picked up No. 01, First-Love Peach.

“Then this one suits you better.”

“Natural. It won’t look like you tried.”

The tube turned once under the light — the peach pink-orange, clear and soft.

Kim Ji-won added, toward the mirror:

“The bride is my boyfriend’s ex-girlfriend.”

Bae Joo-hyun’s hand stopped mid-twist.

No. 01 went quietly back on the display stand.

She reached for the neighboring No. 02, Vampire Cherry.

Kim Ji-won tilted her face.

“Didn’t you say something natural?”

“...Circumstances have changed.”

“Cut.”

Lee Eung-bok sat behind the monitor checking the replay, while the two stayed at the counter waiting for the adjustment notes.

Kim Eun-sook sat in a folding chair nearby, script spread across her lap, reworking the emotional beats of Yoon Myung-joo’s entrance at the wedding in a later scene.

Her phone buzzed on the table.

She picked it up without thinking.

The message was from Baek Si-on.

An image and one line — the image was the price page of Riot Games’ term sheet, the boldest number occupying half the row: three million dollars.

KakaoTalk · Baek Si-on

Baek Si-on

[screenshot — Riot Games term sheet, price page: 3,000,000 USD]

Baek Si-on

When I last quoted you — the production buyout for one song was five hundred thousand dollars. In hindsight, that quote of mine was rather conservative.

Kim Eun-sook

Noted, philanthropist Baek.

Kim Eun-sook

The contract’s signed — no raising the price after the fact, hm.

Kim Eun-sook counted the zeros on the quote once more. Ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten-thousands — all the way to three million. Confirmed she hadn’t miscounted, she opened a currency app and keyed the figure in. The conversion surfaced: over three point three billion won — and the number would keep climbing with the rate.

When she’d first heard five hundred thousand dollars for a buyout, Baek Si-on had seemed to be robbing Korean drama production budgets in broad daylight. Now Riot had lifted the same ceiling to three million dollars.

Lee Eung-bok noticed her expression and assumed the script had a problem.

“What’s wrong?”

She turned the screen toward him.

He read the cooperation terms first, then the price.

“This is... one song?”

Kim Eun-sook defended the last shred of her dignity.

“It’s not all song. It includes the global promotional license for the tournament cycle, the finals opening-stage performance, and the usage rights for promos and the documentary.”

Lee Eung-bok nodded, seriously.

“So netting those out — what’s the song worth?”

Kim Eun-sook: “......”

However the project was divided, five hundred thousand dollars no longer sounded the least bit exaggerated. Two hundred million won, set against a three-million-dollar frame, suddenly didn’t read as a friendship price at all — more like a modest act of cultural aid.

She muttered at the unread badge, quietly:

“He sent this purely to prove he never swindled me...”

Lee Eung-bok went back to the replay controls.

“He’s afraid you don’t know how good a deal you got.”

“......”

❧ ❧ ❧

The second take.

Bae Joo-hyun was visibly looser than the first, her counter tone carrying that wordless understanding between a beauty consultant and a customer’s romantic predicament. Kim Ji-won received it beautifully. Neither of them chased the products with the camera — every action followed the characters’ feelings, and from “something natural” to “circumstances have changed,” the two glosses’ logic of use had been sewn into the personalities themselves.

Lee Eung-bok watched the full replay.

“Passed.”

Movement returned to the set; the crew began striking the close-up camera.

Bae Joo-hyun bowed to Kim Ji-won first.

“Thank you for your work, sunbae.”

Kim Ji-won hurried to return it, waving both hands:

“No, no — you’re the one who worked hard.”

Bae Joo-hyun then thanked Lee Eung-bok, Kim Eun-sook, and the crew in turn.

This was her first real drama shoot. The part was tiny; her lines, all told, fewer than five.

Kim Eun-sook tossed out an easy compliment:

“Good camera sense. You could try proper acting someday.”

Bae Joo-hyun thanked her politely, and did not let the compliment go to her head.

Because she knew perfectly well what lay under it: three hundred million won of Baek Si-on’s money.

❧ ❧ ❧

Before leaving the set she made her farewell rounds, her manager already urging from outside — ‘Weekly Idol’ waited, where the other four would join midway.

The nanny van pulled out, and once Kang Seul-gi, Son Seung-wan, Park Soo-young, and Kim Yerim climbed in, the space turned lively at once.

Kim Yerim asked first:

“Unnie, how was the acting?”

“Fine.”

Park Soo-young followed up:

“Did you have lines?”

“Yes.”

“How many?”

“Enough that you’ll sit still and listen.”

Park Soo-young understood: plenty of lines — just not enough to sustain a younger sister’s in-depth interview.

The van entered central Seoul.

Passing a bus stop, Kim Yerim suddenly slapped the window.

“Joohyun-unnie!”

Four heads swiveled to the right window at once.

In the bus-stop light box hung a double-sided poster.

The poster, as lit

Left: black. Her lips in the deep Vampire Cherry, the gaze cold and sharp.

Right: soft pale pink. The same face, lips in the translucent First-Love Peach — many degrees gentler.

Center copy: “Take a bite. Become the color you want to be seen in.” — LAVUE.

The bus stop slid out of sight. Bae Joo-hyun’s surface stayed calm, but the corners of her mouth were climbing without her permission.

The van pressed on and soon overtook a city bus wearing the same ad — her face enlarged to the scale of an entire vehicle side.

Park Soo-young glanced at the real person, then at the bus.

“Unnie, you’re in several places across Seoul at once today.”

Son Seung-wan added:

“And every one of them is taller than you.”

Bae Joo-hyun turned her head.

Son Seung-wan looked down immediately:

“I mean the ad is.”

The van stopped at a red light. Across the intersection: an LG Electronics showroom, and behind its floor-to-ceiling glass, dozens of display televisions playing the same commercial in loop.

LG showroom · the display wall · ad on loop

Dark background. Bae Joo-hyun lifts a cherry between two fingers and sends it between her lips.

Dozens of mouths turn Vampire Cherry at the same instant — then the whole wall cuts to peach, and her face takes over every screen from edge to edge.

Forty screens. One face. No escape for anyone at this crosswalk.

Park Soo-young said, drily:

“This many unnies eating at once — it’s a little frightening.”

“Shut up.”

Kang Seul-gi, though, gave it serious consideration:

“Unnie, it really is beautifully shot.”

Bae Joo-hyun made a small “mm.”

This time, no modesty. She thought so too.

❧ ❧ ❧

While the van was still dissecting the commercial, five phones lit up with the news push at almost the same moment.

Naver · Entertainment

Naver · Entertainment

Naver · Entertainment

“Baek Si-on-sunbae’s new movie!”

At the name, the phone in Bae Joo-hyun’s hand had already unlocked itself.

Kim Yerim crowded in beside Park Soo-young, and the two of them opened the teaser link on one shared screen.

Teaser · ‘Sado’ · first reveal

Black screen. Sound first — Song Kang-ho’s Yeongjo, low and cold:

“You are first the Crown Prince of this nation. Only then are you my son.”

Light. The child Sado kneels at the center of the hall, reciting the classics. Cut: the adult still kneeling in the same spot. Yeongjo’s expression slides from a smile to ice:

“Why is there nothing you do that satisfies me?”

The montage breaks loose. Sado alone inside a forest of ministers, absorbing the censure. The robes stripped off, kneeling on a bridge, rain running down his hair. Yeongjo cradling the young royal grandson, teaching emperor’s craft stroke by painted stroke — the old hand steering the small one through the characters.

The music turns violent. Drums slam into strings. The long blade comes off the rack. Palace gates swing open in a night of rain; guards draw steel. Baek Si-on moves through the corridor blade-first, water sliding off every inch of the steel. Soaked robes. Wet stone spraying under his feet. Moon Geun-young, as the Crown Princess, rushes from the side gate to bar his way — he lifts his leg past her, and she falls hard. The blade shears through the lanterns before the gate. The royal guard closes from both wings.

The last shot slows without warning: Sado outside the hall, rain sliding down the blade as it rises — pointing, far off, at one lit window. Two shadows on the paper, one large, one small. Yeongjo’s head bowed. The grandson’s face lifted.

Freeze between the blade and the shadows. Black. Title: ‘Sado’. Below it, the date — August 3.

The teaser ended. The van stayed quiet for a long while.

Kang Seul-gi broke the silence first:

“Didn’t Crown Prince Sado end up locked in the rice chest? The teaser looks like he’s about to kill Yeongjo.”

Park Soo-young had been thoroughly hooked:

“Maybe it’s alternate history? Maybe he really does fight his way in?”

Son Seung-wan considered, then ruled:

“Then the second half has to reshoot all of Joseon history.”

Bae Joo-hyun took no part in the historical-revision debate.

She backed out of the teaser and opened her chat with Baek Si-on.

KakaoTalk · Baek Si-on

Bae Joo-hyun

I watched the ‘Sado’ teaser.

Bae Joo-hyun

So in the end — did you really take up the blade to kill Yeongjo?

Baek Si-on

Aren’t you Korean?

Bae Joo-hyun

I’m asking about the movie. In the teaser you clearly look about to storm the palace.

Baek Si-on

Then the teaser edit works.

Bae Joo-hyun

So — the ending?

Baek Si-on

No spoilers.

Bae Joo-hyun

Will there be seats for us at the premiere?

Baek Si-on

Five. Held for you.

Bae Joo-hyun

Good.

She looked up once, at the three sisters still arguing over whether the Crown Prince made it into the inner hall, then looked back down and typed the reply.

The van came out of the tunnel, and sunlight poured back into the cabin.

On premiere day, she would get to see him again.

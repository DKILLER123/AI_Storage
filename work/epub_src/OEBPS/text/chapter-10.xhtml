Chapter Ten

The Poster Debate

Mid-April Edge of the Banwol industrial complex The poster shoot set

Mid-April. An old district at the edge of the Banwol industrial complex in Ansan, Gyeonggi-do.

The sky was overcast, the wind carrying a smell of rust and damp. The pre-shoot ritual was simple. Baek Jeong-hun lit a stick of incense; the crew filed past and bowed in turn. On the offering table sat a pig’s head and a few pieces of fruit. Nobody spoke — just the wind rattling the paper.

Beyond the “entire crew,” there were barely a dozen people in total. Camera, lighting, sound, script continuity, art-and-props in one body, make-up, two production assistants, plus Baek Jeong-hun and two actors.

Too broke to hire a single line producer. Everyone hauled equipment in turns.

But Baek Jeong-hun’s eyes were bright. The script was rewritten; the year moved to 2002; Sang-hun and Yeon-hui’s storyline redrawn and tightened. Every scene had more solid logic than before.

After the ritual, Baek Jeong-hun clapped his hands.

“Alright. First, the poster.”

The poster shoot was set in an old alley nearby. The art director had scouted ahead, picking a dead-end with peeling plaster at the base, standing water on the ground, moss at the foot of the low walls, and a tangle of wires overhead. The raw texture of 2002 Seongnam’s underclass came free of charge.

Baek Jeong-hun spread the storyboard sketch across a folding chair and waved Si-on over. The drawing wasn’t fine, but the composition was plain enough: Sang-hun crouched at the mouth of the alley, back against the wall, one hand cradling a cigarette whose smoke covered half his face, eyes lifting toward the lens.

The standard indie-film poster framing. Safe. Fail-safe. And flat.

Si-on looked at it for a few seconds and said nothing. Baek Jeong-hun assumed he was gathering his thoughts and didn’t rush him.

After a while, Si-on spoke.

“Samchon, let’s try another direction.”

“What?”

“Set me sinking in the water.”

Si-on crouched down and picked up a fallen branch, dragging a rough composition in the dirt: water past the chest, past the chin, past the lips, until only a pair of eyes surfaced above the line. He drew a horizontal stroke at the waterline.

“I stretch one hand toward the bank. Not thrashing, not flailing — reaching. Reaching for something and not getting it.”

He drew a row of stick figures above the line.

“People stand on the bank. Lots of them. Arms folded, standing, watching. Not one of them reaches down.”

Baek Jeong-hun stared at the crude sketch in the dirt for a long time without a word.

“No.”

Si-on looked up. From moving the year to replacing the lead, his uncle had bowed to every proposal he’d put forward. He’d grown used to that rhythm. So this “no” landed oddly.

Baek Jeong-hun pointed at the crowd of impassive stick figures on the bank.

“This composition is too big. A crowd of cold onlookers on the bank, one struggling man sinking below. That’s social-portrait criticism. That’s a tragedy of the age.”

“But Si-on, my film isn’t a portrait of a group. My film is deeply private.”

“Sang-hun and Yeon-hui are two flies gnawing at each other in a gutter and licking each other’s wounds. They don’t need the passing crowd, because this world has never once cared about them.”

The alley went quiet. The wind lifted the unburnt ash from the offering table, sent it circling, and let it fall.

Si-on looked down at the sketch he’d drawn. He thought back over every line he’d spoken to his uncle since discharge — the script has to change; which one do you choose; let’s try another direction.

Every time it had been him driving. Every time he’d taken it for granted. Because he had a past life, professional judgment, and the confidence that comes from knowing more.

But he’d forgotten one thing. This film was called Green Bottle Fly. The writer was Baek Jeong-hun. The director was Baek Jeong-hun. From the first word to the last frame, it belonged to Baek Jeong-hun.

An actor may offer suggestions, discuss character, even nudge a director’s judgment at certain moments. But he cannot make a director’s decision for him. That was the rule.

He’d leaned on the cleverness of two lifetimes and forgotten that rule.

“I’m sorry, Samchon.”

His shoe sole ground over the lines in the dirt, blurring the sketch to nothing.

“I overstepped. It’s your film. The poster is yours to decide.”

Baek Jeong-hun studied him. Truth be told, when he’d spoken, he’d braced for an argument. His nephew was young, had ideas, had a temper, and would surely push back at being overruled in public. He hadn’t needed it. Si-on accepted the correction faster than he turned a page. No defense, no “but I think,” no “give it another thought” — just one “I’m sorry,” clean and done.

Most of Baek Jeong-hun’s flare had gone out.

“But—”

He cleared his throat, picked up the branch Si-on had dropped, crouched, and re-drew in the smoothed earth.

“Your core image is good. The suffocation under the water fits Sang-hun’s situation exactly. The problem is only the bank.”

He drew a waterline. Below it, a human shape. But on the bank he drew only two figures.

“Take the crowd out. Leave only Yeon-hui on the bank. She reaches into the water, trying to pull him out. But behind her stands her rotten little brother, dragging her back with all his weight.”

The branch scraped softly across the ground.

“Sang-hun sinks, Yeon-hui is hauled back. Two people both struggling, both trying to save the other, neither reaching.”

He drew a short stretch of emptiness between the two outstretched hands. Nothing long. A few centimetres. But in the frame, it was a distance that could never be crossed.

Si-on crouched beside him and watched for a long time.

“Samchon, you draw beautifully.”

It wasn’t flattery. Faced with the same image — a man struggling in water — his own version pushed outward: add the crowd, add the society, add the criticism, cram a whole era into a single poster. Baek Jeong-hun’s version pulled inward: strip away everything extra, leaving two people, two hands, and the unmade distance between them.

One was addition. One was subtraction.

And subtraction was always harder than addition.

Baek Jeong-hun, rarely praised sincerely by his nephew, could no longer hold the corners of his mouth. He tried to deflect.

“This framing may be too artsy for a domestic theatrical poster. Audiences won’t bite.”

“It’ll be fine, Director.”

Baek Jeong-hun blinked. From discharge to now, this boy had called him “Samchon.” This was the first “Director.”

He said nothing. The smile pressed down twice and failed, and finally he let it go, turning toward the equipment truck, all the while raising a hand to wave them off.

“Get ready to work.”

The production assistant answered and began hauling a tripod down from the truck. The cinematographer crouched at the alley mouth adjusting the aperture. The make-up artist ran over with her kit, nearly stepping into the puddle at the wall’s foot. The alley had suddenly come alive.

Only Choi Jin-ri, at the base of the wall, had not moved. She held the script clutched to her chest and had not said a single word the whole time.

She wasn’t sure what she was watching. It was only when she turned a page that she realised she was still thinking about the look on his face when he ground out the drawing.

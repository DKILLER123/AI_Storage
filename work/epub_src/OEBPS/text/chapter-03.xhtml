Chapter Three

A Method Actor’s Training

The day after casting A loan-shark office in Guro District First day of research

On the day after he was confirmed for the lead role, Baek Si-on walked into a loan-shark office in Guro District and applied for a job on the spot, on the grounds that he was a freshly-discharged soldier with nothing to do.

He was hired that day.

The first time he went out on a collection with a veteran, Si-on made an effort.

Hawaiian shirt, white slacks, pointy shoes.

The veteran guiding him was a man named Kim, in his forties, with a scar across one cheek that made him look formidable. Kim looked Si-on up and down and laughed.

“Watched too many movies, huh?”

Si-on didn’t argue.

Noon. Mealtime. The two of them reached an old residential building and climbed to the fourth floor. Si-on rolled his shoulders and cracked his neck, thinking he was finally going to see something real.

The door opened on a middle-aged man, thin, with heavy dark circles under his eyes.

Si-on was about to square his shoulders.

Kim shoved him aside without even taking his shoes off and walked straight in. One room, one hall. The furniture was old but clean. Two dishes and a soup sat on the table, chopsticks just set down.

Kim didn’t flip the table. He didn’t shout. He went straight into the kitchen and ladled out a bowl of rice, and on his way back he thrust an empty bowl into Si-on’s hands.

“What are you standing there for? Go get rice.”

He plopped down, took up his chopsticks, and shoved a fat piece of fish belly into his mouth, chewing and talking at once.

“Oooh, this fish is fried just right…”

Si-on stood there holding the empty bowl.

So where are the machetes and the baseball bats?

But his best quality was that he adapted. If the veteran said eat, then he wasn’t going to stand there like a statue. He went to the kitchen, filled his own bowl, sat cross-legged, and went at it. Fresh out of the service, his appetite was at its peak. Three minutes and the first bowl was gone. He got up for a second.

Five minutes later, Si-on decided the back-and-forth was too much trouble. He carried the whole rice cooker — the one with the chipped inner pan — to the table and started eating straight from the scoop.

Kim’s chopsticks stopped in mid-air. The debtor stopped eating too.

Si-on looked up at them.

“You two not eating?”

As he spoke he scooped the last of the kimchi from the dish into the rice cooker and mixed it in.

The debtor stared for a full five seconds. Then he stood up, walked to the back room, and came out in under two minutes carrying a paper packet wrapped in old newspaper, which he put in Kim’s hand with a hand that was trembling.

“Please. Just… eat somewhere else.”

Kim looked at the packet, then at Si-on, still scraping the last grains of rice out of the cooker. He silently tucked the packet into his briefcase.

On the way downstairs, Kim looked at Si-on differently, entirely.

First collection, closed, in the most absurd way possible.

❧ ❧ ❧

The next day, Kim’s desk was empty.

“Where’s the hyung?”

The boss took a drag of his cigarette and pointed at a man so thin he was like a bamboo pole, eyes darting around the room.

“Hit his quota. Took the day off. Today you go with old Park.”

If old Kim played the “eat like a hurricane” school, then this old Park was pure “be a pest.” They went to the door of another debtor. This one wasn’t refusing out of stubbornness — they genuinely had nothing left. Park didn’t curse. He didn’t demand dinner. He just took off his shoes, lay down on the broken sofa in the living room, grabbed the remote, turned the TV to max volume.

“Hyung-nim, I get that you’ve got no money, but I’ve got to eat too.”

Park picked at his toes as he watched a variety show.

“I’ll just live here. Money arrives, I leave. Don’t worry, I don’t snore.”

Si-on copied him, found a stool, sat, arms crossed, and closed his eyes.

Day one: the debtor endured. Day two: the debtor’s wife, unable to take two grown men camped out in her house, took the kid and went to her mother’s. Day three, at noon: the debtor showed up with enormous dark circles, having somehow patched one hole with another, borrowing from a lender with even higher interest to settle Park’s account. Collection successful.

❧ ❧ ❧

The next day, Park’s desk was empty too.

“Off day as well?”

The boss nodded.

Si-on stood there for three seconds and was then assigned to a third veteran. Surname Choi. Early forties. Bald, with tattoos across his neck, and he was sitting with his legs crossed, clipping his nails.

Si-on started toward him. The boss called him back.

“Hold on. Something first.”

The boss offered him a cigarette. When Si-on waved it off, he lit it himself.

“Choi’s different from the other two. Kim and Park grind people down. Choi doesn’t grind.”

“A while back there was a hard case. Name of Park, went bankrupt in the building-materials business, owed fifty million. Choi took two cans of red paint straight to his door and splashed them all over the steel security gate. Wrote big characters on the wall. Pinned the IOU up and down the stairwell — name, ID number, how much owed, all of it. Every resident knew this man owed money and wouldn’t pay.”

Si-on said nothing.

“That man had thin skin. He lasted three days. The third night he jumped from Mapo Bridge into the Han River.”

The boss flicked his ash.

“The man’s dead. But a death clears the account only with the bank; not in our line of work. Today old Choi is at the funeral home settling the balance — negotiating with the dead man’s family. Go along and watch.”

He clapped Si-on on the shoulder.

“Learn what real debt collection looks like.”

Si-on looked over. Choi had blown his nail clippings onto the floor, stood up slowly, shrugged into a jacket, and gave him a tilt of the head.

“Let’s go.”

❧ ❧ ❧
The funeral home Near the Jogye Temple district The seventh day before the burial

The deceased man’s head memorial was still in progress. When Choi walked in, he didn’t even offer incense. He dragged a chair to the entrance of the mourning hall and sat down with his legs wide, lighting a cigarette.

“Sister-in-law, my condolences. But old Park’s gone, and the money still has to be sorted. Fifty million principal, and with these months of interest, seventy million.”

The dead man’s widow stood in her mourning clothes, eyes swollen like walnuts, the whole of her swaying on the verge of collapse.

“He… he was driven to death. We’re a widow and an orphan, where are we supposed to get money?”

“Now that’s not right.”

Choi flicked his ash.

“A father’s debt is the son’s to pay. It’s only natural. If you’ve no money, then old Park’s urn may not be leaving this building today. My men down below don’t have the best tempers; if the ashes were accidentally scattered, that would be terribly unlucky.”

The words had barely landed.

“SSI-BAL! I’ll fight you!”

The dead man’s son had somehow got hold of a fruit knife and charged at him, eyes bloodshot.

Choi didn’t dodge. Didn’t even rise from the chair. Because he knew the knife wouldn’t land.

It didn’t. The woman threw herself on her son, clutched him with everything she had, sobbing so hard it tore.

“Please, Mother is begging you — let it go…”

The boy shook all over, eyes fixed on Choi, teeth grinding. The knife hung in the air.

It hung there a long time.

Then, with the knife still in his hand, he sank to his knees in front of his father’s portrait, tears and snot streaming over his face.

The scene was over. The woman mortgaged the only old house the family had to the lending company at a rock-bottom price and scraped together seventy million.

Walking out of the funeral home with the transfer slip, Choi was in a good mood. He even hummed a little tune. He glanced back at Si-on, who had been silent the whole time, and patted his shoulder.

“See that? That’s collection. Don’t copy the soft-handed tricks of Kim and Park. Against these kinds of poor wretches, you get harder than they are — hard enough that they lose even the courage to die, and then the money comes out on its own.”

Si-on said nothing.

The early-spring sun fell warm on his shoulders, and yet he felt the cold crawling up from inside his bones.

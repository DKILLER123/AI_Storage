Chapter Ninety-Four

September in a Diary, and a FaceTime from Los Angeles

September 13 2:10 pm Hapjeong · Choi Jin-ri’s apartment, the desk by the window A pink cover, a black pen, and a day that had to be written down before it became someone else’s

Choi Jin-ri sat at the desk with her knees up on the chair and the diary open to a blank September page.

The cover was pink. Not the loud pink. The kind that had faded at the corners because she had been carrying it since spring, in bags, in plane pockets, once in a hospital drawer. The pen was black. Uniball. The same one she had used to write the Venice flight in June, and the BBQ in July, and the eight minutes she had not been in the hall for, because she had been in the hall, and had not been able to write until the plane.

She dated the page.

12 September. Then, because the 12th had run into the 13th and the 13th was already eating the 12th, she wrote the 13th underneath, smaller.

The apartment was quiet. Goo Hara was still in Japan. The group chat had a photograph of a convenience-store rice ball and the caption “fighting,” which was Hara eonni’s way of saying she was alive and eating and not to be worried about, which meant Choi Jin-ri worried anyway, and then stopped, because the diary was open and the night was still in her shoulder.

She wrote.

Choi Jin-ri · September diary · the 12th, running into the 13th

He said whatever. I took him to the treasure shop. Ajumma asked if he was my boyfriend. I said not yet. I don’t know why I said yet. The word came out before I could catch it.

He ate four plates before the rice. He said the dishes were a bit salty. I laughed. I couldn’t not.

He paid. No — I paid, and then he asked how much, because his mom said when you eat with a girl you pay. I told him to take me to a movie instead.

Lucy. Choi Min-sik sunbae. I fell asleep on his shoulder from about one percent. He didn’t move. He only woke me when the broom came in. Then he threatened to tell Choi Min-sik sunbae. I threatened to tell people the song was about himself. We pinky-swore. He said childish. He still hooked the finger.

In the car park I felt a lens. I always feel it. He didn’t ask if I was sure. He treated it as true. He made two calls. I said I was sorry for the movie. He put a finger to his lips.

Then too many cars. Then we left. Then this morning the whole country had a screenshot of a page that isn’t there anymore, and his four lines, and an apology with my name in it.

I should be angry that my name was in it.

I am. A little.

Mostly I keep thinking about the yet.

And the finger.

And the fact that he did not move until the broom.

She sat with the pen above the last line and did not add another.

The yet was already too much. The finger was already too much. If she wrote the thing underneath those, the diary would become a person, and a person can be stolen, and she had been stolen enough.

She closed it.

Put it in the drawer. Not under the mattress. She was not fifteen. The drawer had a key. She turned it.

Her phone, face-down on the desk, buzzed.

KakaoTalk.

Baek Si-on.

KakaoTalk · Baek Si-on

Baek Si-on

The piece is gone. If anyone asks, it was a film. If they keep asking, don’t answer. I’ll handle it.

14:18

Choi Jin-ri

I know. I saw. Thank you.

14:19

Baek Si-on

Did you sleep?

14:19

Choi Jin-ri

A bit. Did you?

14:20

Baek Si-on

Four hours. Enough. Don’t go out today.

14:20

Choi Jin-ri

I wasn’t going to. I’m writing.

14:21

Baek Si-on

Good.

14:21

She looked at the last Good for longer than a Good deserved.

Then she put the phone face-down again, because if she left it face-up she would wait for a bubble that was not coming, and she had decided, at the drawer, not to be a person who waited for bubbles.

The diary was locked.

The yet was in it.

That would have to be enough for a thirteenth of September.

September 13 11:40 pm KST · 7:40 am PDT Hapjeong 401 · a laptop, a cousin, and a number that did not look Korean Hwang Soo-ah, Rich Paul, LeBron, and then a face the whole world already had a word for

Baek Eun-ah was on the floor with the Hyundai folder and a bowl of cereal that had gone to soup. Seo Eun-ju’s retainer sat on the second page, signed, a number Baek Eun-ah could live with and Kim & Chang could live with, which was, she had been told, the only overlap that mattered at eleven over tofu.

Baek Si-on was on the sofa with a script he was not reading. The Green Fly domestic press kit. Dates. Quotes. A still of Choi Jin-ri in the alley that he had already looked at too long this afternoon, after sending a Good.

Park Ji-hun’s phone, charging on the kitchen counter, rang first. Unknown. He glanced, declined, because unknown at 11:40 was usually a shop or a journalist or a wrong number, and he had learned, in three weeks, that the people who mattered already had his Kakao.

Then Baek Eun-ah’s laptop chimed.

FaceTime.

The number was American. +1. Los Angeles area code. She stared at it as if an area code could be a person.

“Oppa.”

“What?”

“Hwang Soo-ah-ssi said she might put someone on. I thought she meant an e-mail.”

Hwang Soo-ah was the woman in Los Angeles who did the kind of introductions that did not go through LOEN and did not go through SM and did not go through Kim & Chang. A Korean who had stayed. A name Baek Eun-ah had written in the margin of a page and had not highlighted, because highlighting made things look like money, and this had not yet been money.

Baek Si-on sat up.

“Answer it.”

She answered it.

The screen opened on a kitchen that was not Hapjeong. White. A lot of white. A man in a black shirt, sitting, the camera a little too close, as if he had been told to call and had not adjusted the distance.

Not Hwang Soo-ah.

FaceTime · live · 07:40 PDT

Rich Paul · Los Angeles · incoming on Baek Eun-ah’s laptop

The man looked at them. Looked at Baek Si-on. Nodded once, the way American sports people nod when they have decided you are in the room.

“Baek? Rich Paul. I handle LeBron. Hwang Soo-ah said I should see your face before I see a contract, so here is the face. LeBron watched the Venice clip. He said the kid doesn’t blink. That’s a compliment. He doesn’t give many. I’m not calling to sign you. I’m calling because Kobe asked who the Korean was, and when Kobe asks, I don’t send an e-mail.”

Baek Eun-ah’s cereal spoon hung. Baek Si-on did not hang. He had been a person who collected debts. He had been a person who stood in a hall in Venice and counted eight minutes. He had been a person who posted four lines at 12:31 and made a shop kneel. A man in a white kitchen in Los Angeles saying LeBron and Kobe in the same breath was a large sentence. It was not, for him, an impossible one.

“This is Baek Si-on.”

“Yeah. I got that. Hold on. He’s coming on.”

The picture jostled. A hand. A chair. Then another face filled the laptop, closer than the first, the way a face fills a screen when the person on it has never learned to sit back from a camera because the camera has always sat back from him.

Shaved close. Eyes that did not do small talk. A mouth that had been in a thousand stills and still looked like a mouth, not a logo.

Kobe Bryant

Guard · Los Angeles Lakers · five-time NBA champion

Real-name counterpart · the Mamba on a Saturday morning in L.A.

The face the world already had a word for, filling a laptop in Hapjeong at 11:40 p.m. because he had asked who the Korean was, and because Rich Paul does not send an e-mail when that question has been asked. He does not blink much either. He is calling from a kitchen that is too white, at an hour that is morning for him and not for anyone in this room.

For a second nobody spoke.

Park Ji-hun, in the kitchen, put down the phone he had declined. He did not come closer. He stood with a charging cable in his hand as if the cable were a fact he could hold onto.

FaceTime · live · 07:41 PDT

Kobe Bryant · Los Angeles · Baek Si-on & Baek Eun-ah, Hapjeong 401

Kobe Bryant looked at Baek Si-on. The connection lagged by half a beat, which made the look longer than it was.

“You’re the actor.”

Not a question. A placing.

“I am.”

“I watched the clip. The award. The speech. You talked about your mother and then you talked about money they owed you. That’s not how those speeches usually go.”

“It was owed.”

Kobe’s mouth moved. Not quite a smile. The thing a mouth does when it has heard a sentence it respects and has not decided to say so yet.

“I asked Rich who you were. He asked Hwang. Hwang said Korean-American, L.A. as a kid, army, then this. I grew up with that kind of map. Different sport. Same map.”

Baek Si-on sat forward on the sofa. The script slid. He let it.

“I lived in L.A. until I was twelve. Then Seoul. Then the army. Then a debt-collection office, because the film needed it. Then Venice. That’s the short version.”

“The short version is always a lie. I know that. I’m not asking for the long one at 7 a.m. I’m asking if you work.”

The word work came out like a noun that had weight. Not job. Work.

“I work.”

“Good. People who win something young start thinking the winning is the work. It isn’t. The work is what you do at 5 a.m. when nobody is clapping. You look like you already know that. If you don’t, you’ll find out. Everybody does.”

Baek Eun-ah had not moved. The cereal had completed its journey to soup. She was not going to eat it. Kobe turned his head, said something off-camera that the laptop did not fully catch — a name, a time, a gym — then came back.

“When you’re in L.A., come to the facility. Not a photoshoot. You sit, you watch, you don’t talk through the first hour. Rich will set it. LeBron’s people will pretend they thought of it. I thought of it. That’s enough for a Saturday.”

“I’ll come.”

“Don’t say I’ll come if you mean I’ll see. I don’t do I’ll see. I do show up or don’t.”

“I’ll show up.”

Kobe looked at him for another second, as if checking whether the sentence had a back to it. Then he nodded, once, the same nod Rich Paul had used, except this one had a career behind it.

“Tell your mother I said the speech was the right length. Most of them aren’t. And tell the girl in the film — the one they tried to throw away — she didn’t look thrown away. That’s harder than a cup. All right. I’m going to the gym. You should sleep. It’s late there. I can see it in the room.”

The picture cut.

The laptop went to the FaceTime call-ended screen, which is a very small, very stupid grave.

Hapjeong 401 was quiet in the way rooms are quiet after a large voice has left them.

Park Ji-hun said, from the kitchen, with the cable still in his hand:

“Was that—?”

“Yes.”

“Should I have picked up the unknown number?”

“No. That was a journalist, or a shop, or a wrong number. This came through Hwang Soo-ah-ssi. Different door.”

Baek Eun-ah closed the laptop. Not hard. As if a hard close might restart the call, and she was not ready to be a person who had been in a room with Kobe Bryant twice in one night.

“Oppa.”

“Mm.”

“I am going to put this in a spreadsheet, and then I am going to delete the spreadsheet, because there is no cell for that.”

“Don’t put it in a spreadsheet.”

“I just said I would delete it.”

He picked the script up off the floor.

Choi Jin-ri in the alley. The still.

Kobe had said: the girl in the film, the one they tried to throw away, she didn’t look thrown away.

That was harder than a cup.

Baek Si-on sat with that for a while, in a room that still had the white kitchen in it, faintly, the way a bright light stays on the inside of an eyelid.

He did not text Choi Jin-ri.

She was writing. He had said Good. A Good, left alone, is sometimes the correct last bubble.

He put the script on the table, face down.

Park Ji-hun plugged the cable back in, as if the universe might need charging.

Baek Eun-ah took the cereal to the sink.

Outside, Hapjeong at midnight was ordinary. A scooter. A convenience-store door. Someone laughing too loud, then not.

In Los Angeles it was morning, and a man was going to the gym, and the work, as he had said, was what you did at 5 a.m. when nobody was clapping.

Baek Si-on set an alarm for five.

Then he turned it to five-thirty, because he was not a basketball player, and because honesty, even with an alarm, was a kind of work.

But what really sent the situation spiralling out of control was not the James–Kobe fan fight. It was the sports fans of every other discipline getting provoked. Fans from every corner of sport piled in, trading numbers, dragging each other down, and starting to tag their own era’s superstars at scale. The sports world worships strength with a single-mindedness no other field matches. If an NBA wing player could do it, how could the best athlete in any other game possibly admit they couldn’t — especially when their own fans had already been mocked at their own front door by another fan base?

The first to follow was Cristiano Ronaldo. The backing track, naturally, was Legend. Real Madrid and Manchester United fans went at each other in the comments. Then Neymar. Then Usain Bolt. Then the gymnasts. Then NFL wide receivers. Then rugby players. Then even a baseball pitcher who turned the challenge into leaping from the water and catching-and-throwing in a single motion.

Not every attempt succeeded. But every single one was set to Legend.

October 22 Los Angeles · afternoon SB Projects · Scooter Braun’s office A number he did not bet on, a phone call from a country code that rhymes with money, and a door that opens without a knock.

Los Angeles. SB Projects. Scooter Braun sat in his office staring at the freshly refreshed Billboard page on his monitor for a full two minutes and still found it hard to believe.

By his original estimate, riding the tailwind of the S4 World Championship and the release of the Kobe-starring music video, the song should have reached somewhere between No. 10 and No. 20. The audience base was clear-cut: Kobe’s die-hard fans, the film crowd Bong Joon-ho had pulled in, and the esports world that the opening ceremony had blown apart. Top twenty was the most optimistic projection a top-tier operator could make.

What he had not calculated was LeBron James’s competitiveness. And he had not calculated that LeBron’s attempt to hijack the traffic would set off a wave of copycats across the entire sports world, physically shoving the song into a dimension of total breakout.

“Honestly…”

Scooter shook his head and laughed. Fate worked like that sometimes. The best marketing is never the thing you design. It is the thing somebody else cannot resist stealing the spotlight for. He watched the chart for a while, and the amusement slowly faded from his face — because a thought had arrived. On October 28, Baek Si-on would sing Legend at the Lakers’ season opener. Kobe, on his own home floor, would walk the tunnel to a song written about him, with twenty thousand people singing the chorus behind him.

And with Rich Paul’s instincts, he would almost certainly commission a songwriter to make LeBron an anthem of his own — a song bound to his name, played at every one of his big moments. The moment it came out, there would be another round of James-versus-Kobe war. People get compared. Songs get compared. Whose theme is hotter? Whose narrative is loftier? Who is the real legend?

That kind of comparison was a good thing. Because whichever side won, Legend would keep being mentioned. What Scooter feared was not comparison. What he feared was the uncontrollable. Like this time: a change of opener by Riot had started as a controversy and ended as a Bones demo counter-attack, a live coronation at Worlds, a Kobe MV explosion, a LeBron challenge, the global athletes piling in, and a Billboard No. 3 — each link snapping onto the next. Satisfying, yes. But dangerous. What if the next uncontrollable thing was negative? What if the songwriter LeBron commissioned turned out to have some open rivalry with Baek Si-on? What if the sports world’s tribal culture dragged Baek Si-on into a century-defining “Kobe vs. LeBron” debate he had never asked to join?

So. Rather than wait for LeBron to commission somebody himself, intercept early. Offer LeBron a song before he asks. Kobe has Legend. LeBron gets a song sung by Baek Si-on too. Both sides bound to Baek Si-on. And whichever way the competition resolved — the winner was always Baek Si-on.

Scooter had no intention, though, of making Baek Si-on write it. Not from any doubt about his talent. From fear that he would die of exhaustion. The roadside nap in the car, the cop rapping on the window — Scooter still remembered that one. He planned to solicit songs within the industry instead: call in a few of the best producers and writers and have demos built around the keywords “the king’s return home,” “Cleveland,” “the best player in the league,” “the crown,” “a promise kept.” If LeBron really wanted a personal anthem, Scooter would already hold the direction. If LeBron never asked, the song went into Baek Si-on’s album instead. One more piece of inventory. No loss either way.

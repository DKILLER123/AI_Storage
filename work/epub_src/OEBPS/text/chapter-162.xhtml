Chapter One Hundred Sixty Two

Being Short Is Not Entirely Without Advantages

December 13 · afternoon — into the evening A medical room · a hospital she didn’t queue for · a dorm interrogation after dark Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium → Asan Medical Center → the Red Velvet dorm One ankle, one phone call with no sentences wasted, and a confession extracted by the least gentle interrogation unit in Korea: three roommates and a cushion.

The medical room sat at the end of the temporary corridor behind the main stage: a white folding bed, a portable ice fridge, a first-aid kit, and a few laminated flow charts taped to the wall.

Baek Si-on carried Bae Joo-hyun in and set her down on the examination bed.

Her arms were still half-looped around his neck. By the time her brain caught up with the geometry, she snatched them back, and her ears went red again.

The medical staff closed in around the ankle at once. Baek Si-on stepped back one pace and turned to the three girls who had crowded in behind him like a tail.

“Go call your manager.”

“Okay…”

Kang Seul-gi, Son Seung-wan, and Park Soo-young exchanged a look, retreated obediently, and jogged off to find him.

The room quieted. The medic pressed along the outside of Bae Joo-hyun’s ankle, rotated the joint, watched her face. Her shoulders flinched; she kept her lip between her teeth and made no sound.

The medic looked up.

“Mild sprain. Nothing serious.”

“Ice it for the next couple of days, keep weight off it, and with three to five days of rest it should settle.”

Bae Joo-hyun had just started to exhale when the medic added:

“But to be safe, we’d still recommend a hospital imaging scan.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Understood.”

“It’s really not necess—” Bae Joo-hyun started, very small.

Baek Si-on looked at her. She shut the sentence off mid-syllable.

His gaze moved off her face and down to her other foot — the one with the performance shoe still hanging off it, stiletto thin, absurdly high.

“Company make you wear these?”

Bae Joo-hyun looked down at her own toes.

“…I didn’t exactly refuse.”

“Why seven centimeters?”

Silence.

The question hurt worse than the ankle. What was she supposed to say? That she remembered secretly rising onto her toes in the last group photo, and that Baek Eun-ah had seen through it in one glance? That she had been at war with her own height ever since? That three centimeters of stubbornness had proved insufficient, so she had escalated to seven?

None of it could be said. Too embarrassing. Far too much like the private, ridiculous inner life of a rookie idol, which was not a genre the public was supposed to know existed.

After a good while, she said, quietly:

“I didn’t want to look too small on camera.”

Small was easier to overlook. Small was easier to crop out of the frame.

Urgent footsteps arrived at the door — the manager. He came in, saw the ankle first, and his brow collapsed immediately. Then he turned and bowed to Baek Si-on.

“We’re sorry for the trouble we’ve caused you.”

“I didn’t call you in here to receive bows.”

The manager straightened, visibly off balance.

Baek Si-on said:

“I spoke with the broadcast director earlier.”

The manager snapped to full attention.

“Yes.”

“On how your group gets the best possible exposure at this concert.”

The manager’s eyes widened a degree.

“I’m sorry — what?”

Baek Si-on looked at the girl on the examination bed.

“For this performance — big-screen close shots and close-ups, seventy percent on her.”

Bae Joo-hyun’s first reaction was not joy.

It was alarm.

“Then what about the other members?”

She was the leader. She knew perfectly well this was a once-in-a-lifetime kind of opening for her — and she still couldn’t let herself be the only thought in the first second. The younger ones ran promo events in the cold wind too. They drilled choreography in the practice room past midnight too. They needed to be seen just as much. If the lenses went only to her, would they be hurt? Would the company use it as an excuse to push resources her way alone? Would fans say she was hogging the frame?

The thoughts churned into each other.

Baek Si-on didn’t answer her question. He looked at the manager instead.

“Did you understand?”

The manager, this time, was fast. He understood completely. Baek Si-on was not raising somebody’s group, and he was not designing anyone’s career path — he was only responsible for making this concert’s exposure maximally efficient. Whether the group developed internal gaps over it, how the members were settled emotionally, how the company later split screen time and talking points — that was the manager’s desk. You could not have Baek Si-on spoon-feed the food to their mouths and then also be responsible for wiping their chins.

The manager bowed his head again — lower this time, and with a different weight behind it.

“Thank you.”

“I’ll coordinate everything.”

Bae Joo-hyun looked at him.

“Oppa…”

The manager turned back to her, more serious than usual.

“Joo-hyun-ah. Leave this one alone for now.”

Baek Si-on didn’t continue the subject. He walked out of the medical room and took out his phone. Baek Eun-ah followed and lowered her voice.

“Cousin — calling a hospital?”

“Mm.”

“A regular one, or…?”

“Asan.”

Baek Eun-ah blinked.

Asan Medical Center, Seoul. To ordinary Korean ears, those two syllables carried real weight: one of the country’s largest general hospitals, permanently pinned to the top tier of the national rankings — cancer center, heart center, imaging, emergency — a reputation that ran clear across Asia. It was not a place where walk-ins got same-day scans. Without connections, you traded your life for queue numbers. An “ankle sprain, imaging to be safe” case could fail to schedule promptly even at a regular hospital — Asan was several tax brackets above that.

Baek Si-on dialed. It connected after a few rings. He was brief.

Baek Si-on → Asan Medical Center · December 13 · four sentences, no minutes taken

It’s me.

My concert guest has hurt her ankle.

We’re coming over now.

Thank you.

The call ended. Four sentences. Somewhere on the other end, an entire reception apparatus rearranged itself for the evening.

Baek Eun-ah looked at him.

“The Hyundai side?”

“Mm.”

Behind Asan stood the Chung family of Hyundai. Baek Si-on disliked owing favors — but he wasn’t precious about using one when using one was correct. If a single phone call could save an injured rookie half a day of corridors, there was no percentage in performing aloofness.

A little later, he came back into the medical room. Bae Joo-hyun lay on the bed mentally rehearsing how she would handle the resource tilts and the fine member-cracks her company might produce next — still deep in the weeds of it — when Baek Si-on’s voice dropped her straight back into reality.

“You’re taking her to Asan for an MRI.”

The manager startled.

“Asan Medical Center?”

“Mm.”

“It’s arranged already.”

The manager was still processing the sentence.

“But if we go right now—”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

“I’ve made the call. You just take her there.”

With the conversation at that point, there was nothing left to say. The manager bowed fast.

“Thank you. Really — thank you.”

Bae Joo-hyun stared at Baek Si-on, stunned. She had assumed, at most, that the company would take her to a neighborhood clinic. And if the manager judged it minor — ice first, hospital later, maybe never. That was the standard rookie treatment path. Not out of malice. Just that everyone was too busy, the schedules too tight, the resources too thin. An ankle sprain, in company logic, got triaged as “can she still dance?” — never as “will this leave something behind?”

And Baek Si-on had sent her to Asan with one sentence.

The difference was too specific. Specific enough that, for a moment, she didn’t know what to say at all.

The medics strapped her to the stretcher. Her hand snagged reflexively at the jacket lying over her. Baek Si-on saw it, and turned.

“Get a blanket.”

Baek Eun-ah went for one immediately. As they wheeled Bae Joo-hyun through the door, she looked back at him.

“Sunbaenim.”

“Mm.”

“Thank you.”

Baek Si-on glanced at her ankle.

“Wear lower heels from now on.”

Bae Joo-hyun: “……”

Moving. And also extremely literal.

She nodded, gently.

“I know.”

The manager followed the medics out and got her into the van. Doors shut. The van pulled out of Jamsil and pointed itself at Asan.

…

Only when the van stopped at the hospital entrance did Bae Joo-hyun truly understand what “it’s arranged already” meant.

The Asan lobby was full. The waiting chairs were packed with patients and families — some queuing with paperwork, some asleep against the backrests, some leaning over the information desk. On the big screen, names and departments rolled past in an endless list.

the Asan main hall · the rolling board · late afternoon

IMAGING — RADIOLOGY — WAIT: 47

ORTHOPEDICS — WAIT: 31

INTERNAL MEDICINE — WAIT: 58

Antiseptic in the air, footsteps, queue-call chimes — the specific mixture that tells you instantly: this is not a pop-up clinic on an idol’s schedule. This is one of Korea’s top hospitals, and also the place where countless ordinary people trade away their afternoons, their queue numbers, and their hope, one wait counter at a time.

She didn’t join any of those counters.

The moment she came through the door, a dedicated staff member came to meet them.

“Bae Joo-hyun-ssi?”

The manager nodded quickly.

“Yes.”

“This way, please.”

The staff led them down a different corridor entirely. Sitting in the wheelchair, watching the lobby full of people still waiting for their numbers, Bae Joo-hyun felt an uncomfortable feeling she couldn’t name. She was inside today because of Baek Si-on’s phone call. This convenience was not something she had earned. But the ankle kept reminding her that she did, factually, need the scan.

The two feelings tangled together and kept her quiet the whole way.

The scan moved fast. MRI. Wait for the films. The doctor read the results — and his verdict landed within shouting distance of the Jamsil medics’.

Mild sprain. No fracture. Ligaments intact.

The doctor set the films down.

“Three days or so and it should improve visibly.”

“Rest these few days. Ice. Keep weight off it.”

“No heels.”

“No dancing.”

“If the pain hasn’t eased in three days, come back for a review.”

The manager nodded along to every line.

“Of course. We’ll be careful.”

“Thank you, doctor,” Bae Joo-hyun added, small.

The doctor gave her a look — he clearly knew who she was — and added one more sentence anyway.

“The stage matters. So does the ankle.”

“When you’re young, it feels like you can push through anything. Leave yourself a habit-sprain, though, and it’s the kind of guest that never checks out.”

Bae Joo-hyun looked down.

“I understand.”

…

By the time they came out of the hospital, the sky had gone over to dusk.

The manager helped her into the van. Back to the dorm, this time. The car was quieter than it had been on the way in.

After a good while, Bae Joo-hyun spoke.

“Oppa.”

“Mm?”

“So I really can’t practice these next few days?”

The manager watched her through the rearview mirror. His first draft was we’ll see how it goes. But the words got as far as his teeth and he remembered Baek Si-on’s face in the medical room, and the doctor’s voice: no dancing.

“Rest properly. That’s the whole assignment.”

“But the concert…”

“You can only have a concert if you can stand on the stage.”

He paused, then added:

“Also — the performance heels are getting swapped for something lower.”

“If the close-ups are going to you anyway, then height isn’t really the point anymore, is it.”

Bae Joo-hyun: “……”

She made a small acknowledging sound, and something in her chest went odd and unnameable. Being chased by cameras was, of course, a good feeling. But someone having already thought through how you should walk, before you’d even asked — that felt, somehow, better.

…

Evening. The Red Velvet dorm.

When Kang Seul-gi pushed the door open, Bae Joo-hyun was on the living room sofa, ankle propped up on the coffee table.

“You’re back.”

Kang Seul-gi swapped into slippers, came over, and crouched beside the ankle for an inspection.

“How is it? Does it hurt?”

“Doesn’t hurt anymore. Doctor says a few days’ rest and it’ll be fine.”

“Thank goodness…”

The sentence had barely landed when the door opened again. Park Soo-young and Son Seung-wan came in one behind the other.

“Princess-nim! How’s the foot?”

Bae Joo-hyun: “……”

“Ya!”

Park Soo-young retreated a step, mouth unbothered.

“Don’t look at me — eonni said it herself.”

She cleared her throat and delivered the impression with tremendous seriousness.

“‘At home… Mom and Dad call me that.’”

“AHAHAHAHA—”

Son Seung-wan finally broke. Kang Seul-gi’s shoulders started shaking and would not stop.

Bae Joo-hyun: “……”

“So every single one of you heard that?”

Three heads nodded in perfect unison.

Bae Joo-hyun closed her eyes.

Wonderful. Today she had been hauled off the stage like a sack by Baek Si-on-sunbaenim, and confessed, with her own mouth, that she was a princess at home. The career was barely out of the gate and the blackmail archive was already shipping in bulk.

“Eonni.”

“Mm?” Bae Joo-hyun opened one eye.

“Was Baek Si-on-sunbaenim’s arms warm?”

“…I don’t remember.”

Park Soo-young stretched the syllables out like taffy.

“You——don’t——remem——ber——”

Bae Joo-hyun grabbed a cushion and threw it.

The cushion did not fly far — with her ankle up on the table, her throwing mechanics were severely compromised — and Park Soo-young caught it one-handed and hugged it, delighted.

“Honestly, eonni, there’s no need to be embarrassed.”

Bae Joo-hyun looked at her.

Park Soo-young kept going.

“A man like Baek Si-on-sunbaenim — him walking over himself, asking himself whether you can stand, carrying you off himself — you might be the only person in all of Korea that’s ever happened to.”

Bae Joo-hyun blinked.

“…Is that so?”

“Mm.”

Park Soo-young came back and sat down close, cushion still in her arms.

“There’s a few days before the concert. You heal up properly — the camera’s yours, and we’ll be your green leaves.”

Bae Joo-hyun looked down at her ankle, and her voice got smaller.

“You really won’t feel bad about it?”

“About what?”

“Everyone practiced for so long…”

Park Soo-young settled in beside her, cushion and all.

“But if the audience remembers eonni first, and then finds Red Velvet by following eonni — isn’t that good too?”

Son Seung-wan nodded.

“It’s not scene-stealing. It’s an entrance.”

Kang Seul-gi said, quietly:

“When eonni gets remembered, we get remembered too.”

Bae Joo-hyun looked at the three of them, and the corner of her mouth bent up.

“Thank you.”

Park Soo-young blinked at her.

“For what?”

“We’re Red Velvet.”

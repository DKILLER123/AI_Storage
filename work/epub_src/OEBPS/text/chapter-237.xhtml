Chapter Two Hundred Thirty-Seven

One Guitar for Twenty-Six Meetings

Same night · the doorbell’s long aftermath

One intercom · two boxes of chocolate · zero appointment

Lee Ji-eun’s apartment · the studio corner

The intercom finished its ring, and the upstairs stayed silent.

Baek Si-on pressed it again.

Slippers crossed a floor somewhere above, and then came a sentence whose impatience survived the speaker entirely.

“Who is it?”

“Baek Si-on.”

Silence on the line.

The lock popped open fast.

He climbed the stairs carrying a laptop and chocolate. The door opened, and Lee Ji-eun’s attention dropped straight to the paper bag in his hand.

“What are you doing here?”

Baek Si-on held the chocolate out to her.

“Recruiting you for overtime.”

Lee Ji-eun took the bag, weighed it in her hand, and glanced at the laptop bag in his other hand.

“You recruit overtime, and this is the wage?”

“Didn’t it work well last time?”

She opened the bag — it was exactly her favorite kind.

“Who told you it worked?”

“Jeong Han-teuk.”

Lee Ji-eun exhaled one short hum through her nose and stepped sideways to clear the doorway.

“Come in, then.”

Baek Si-on swapped his shoes at the entry and went inside.

Before closing the door she added terms:

“Let’s be clear — this buys you half an hour.”

“If that’s not enough, I’ll add more.”

“Do I look like a vending machine to you?”

“The current fee structure is very close to one.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Lee Ji-eun carried the chocolate toward the living room. Manuscript paper and lyric sheets lay spread across the coffee table; one page wore a full coat of revision marks, and the title circled in its top-right corner read — ‘Leon’.

Baek Si-on sat down across from her.

“Heard you’ve been in a mood lately?”

Lee Ji-eun’s hand stopped on the box lid mid-unwrapping. She raised her face.

“Is Jeong Han-teuk my agent, or yours?”

“Yours.”

“Then why does he tell you everything?”

“Possibly because I treat him like a person.”

She gave him a flat look, tore the foil, and put a piece of chocolate in her mouth. The sweetness spread; she sank back into the sofa and gave the edge of the rug an irritated kick with her toe.

“It’s not a mood. It’s that the thing I want to make isn’t the thing he wants to make.”

“With who?”

“Park Myung-soo-sunbae.”

She pointed at the lyric sheet on the table. The ‘Infinite Challenge’ music festival pairs the show’s hosts with professional singers; Lee Ji-eun’s partner was Park Myung-soo. The two had already settled on the French film ‘Léon: The Professional’ as their stage concept — she would play Mathilda, Park Myung-soo would take Léon, and the song would orbit the off-kilter, half-lit relationship between the two characters.

The problem was wedged in the musical direction.

The ‘Leon’ disagreement, as tabled

Her line — retro texture: guitar and acoustic instruments, a clean melodic line, the two of them answering each other in character. The song may ride light, but the aftertaste has to hold. Her home turf: ‘Meet Me on Friday’ and ‘Besides Spring, Love, and Cherry Blossoms’ had long since proved her command of light melodies and long-tail feeling.

His line — heavy electronics: midsummer, outdoors, tens of thousands in the crowd, the air too loud for most of them to hear a single lyric. If the drums sit too light and the melody too slow, the festival heat evaporates the song on the spot. So: a simple, flagrant, brainwashing chorus — ideally a crowd bouncing on the very first listen.

The verdict nobody had spoken yet: neither read was amateur. The question that actually mattered was how to give a retro narrative a festival groove.

“He wants me to push the drum machines and synths even harder.”

Lee Ji-eun opened a second piece of chocolate.

“Keep adding like that, and a French classic turns into a nightclub commercial.”

Baek Si-on picked up the melodic draft.

“You told him that?”

“I told him.”

“What did he say?”

“That a music festival holds tens of thousands of people — it’s not an audience sitting in a concert hall with their eyes closed.”

“He has a point.”

The foil-tearing immediately turned violent.

“So you brought chocolate all the way over here to keep persuading me on his behalf?”

“When did I ever say you were wrong?”

“You just said he has a point.”

“Between has-a-point and is-right, there’s still a whole song in the way.”

Her fingers slowed.

Baek Si-on set the draft back on the coffee table. Objectively, he did side more with Park Myung-soo’s read of a live crowd. A big outdoor festival needs motion — an audience that has waited for hours in the heat unifies fastest under a hard drum pulse and a simple chorus, and every edition’s breakout hits tended to carry hip-hop, disco, or EDM in their blood. But handing Lee Ji-eun a flat “sunbae is right” would only add a second opponent to the disagreement. Park Myung-soo needed live impact; Lee Ji-eun needed the work to hold up. The valuable question was the one that served both.

“Stop thinking about ‘Leon’ for a while,” Baek Si-on said.

Lee Ji-eun frowned.

“If I stop thinking about it, the song won’t finish itself.”

“If you sit here and stew, it’ll finish itself even less.”

“I am creating.”

Baek Si-on surveyed the coffee table.

“Where is the work, then?”

“......”

The table currently held half a page of lyrics and one opened box of chocolate. Judged on evidence, the latter was the more complete work.

Lee Ji-eun reached for a third piece.

Baek Si-on set the laptop bag on the coffee table.

“Switch projects first. Wash your ears.”

“And then?”

“Then you go back to ‘Leon’.”

“You’re sure this will help?”

“It beats more chocolate.”

Lee Ji-eun looked down. The third piece was still unwrapped.

She put it back in the box.

“Fine.”

“Show me what you brought.”

❧ ❧ ❧

The laptop opened. Baek Si-on pulled the project up on the screen.

Lee Ji-eun tilted her head at the filename.

“Legends?”

“Mm.”

“Another love letter to Kobe?”

“...Just listen to the track.”

Kobe genuinely was the source behind a great many of Baek Si-on’s songs, but the phrase love letter departed severely from his definition of that relationship. Lee Ji-eun gave him one suspicious sideways sweep — he looked, she thought, vaguely guilty — and then the melody arrived and carried her attention off entirely.

The current version had no formal vocals. Piano, bass, violin section, and drums marked out the melody’s spine, and a few strips of Baek Si-on’s own hummed guide vocal were scattered through the session — rough, but unambiguous about intent.

The moment the chorus arrived, drums and strings detonated together, and the whole mood was shoved from sunken to summit.

The demo ran start to finish. Lee Ji-eun dragged the playhead back to the chorus entrance and heard the climb out of the verse once more.

“The backdrop is huge.”

“Riot’s world-championship theme this year.”

“No wonder.”

She soloed the drums ahead of the chorus and played them alone.

“This part is already finished. What do you need me for?”

“Record me.”

Lee Ji-eun sat up straight.

“I’m singing?”

“I’m singing.”

Half of the freshly raised expectation fell back down.

“Then what do I do?”

“Sit in the control room. Arm tracks. Monitor. Give notes.”

The other half fell too.

Lee Ji-eun sank slowly back into the sofa.

“That’s it?”

She had just heard a world-championship theme and assumed that a man arriving with chocolate intended to ask her to sing on it. It turned out this person’s core need was a hand to press the record button.

Baek Si-on read the disappointment straight off her.

“There’s something else, too.”

She asked at once:

“What?”

“Finish the demo first.”

Lee Ji-eun’s eyes narrowed.

“You had better not have invented a ‘something else’ on the spot, just to trick me into working.”

“A few boxes of chocolate aren’t worth an elaborate lie.”

“Are you calling me cheap?”

“You quoted half an hour earlier.”

“Prices have gone up.”

“Work first.”

Lee Ji-eun took the console chair and learned the session first. The track naming was clean — verse, low end, strings, drums, scratch melody, and vocals-to-record already separated. It saved her no small amount of time.

Low end — bass holding the floor under the verse.

Drums — pressed back to center, waiting on the chorus.

Scratch melody + vocals-to-record — his hums marked intent; the real takes were all still missing.

Clean naming had saved her close to an hour of orientation.

Baek Si-on went into the booth. The sealed door shut, Lee Ji-eun put on the monitors and opened the mic channel.

“Can you hear me?”

The answer came through the headphones.

“Loud and clear.”

“First verse, then.”

“Okay.”

The record light came on; the backing track entered her ears; Baek Si-on opened his mouth.

The first line had barely ended when Lee Ji-eun hit the talkback.

“Again.”

Baek Si-on glanced at her through the glass, said nothing, and sang it again.

“Again.”

“......”

Third time through, he asked:

“What’s the problem?”

“Too much emotion.”

The first verse, as taken

Take one — recalled on arrival.

Take two — recalled without comment.

Take three — halted for questioning. Diagnosis delivered: too full.

Take four — the voice pressed lower, the first lines no longer chasing full resonance, weariness living between the words; strength permitted to arrive only as the melody climbs. Verdict, one finger raised to stop playback before the chorus: this one holds.

Outstanding: one more pass for the verse’s second half — the last word had closed too fast.

The verse wrapped quickly. Once the chorus entered the session, Lee Ji-eun began to nitpick in earnest, and Baek Si-on absorbed her thickening volleys of corrections through the headphones.

“You talk a lot today.”

“No talking back to your producer during recording.”

“You’re a temporary engineer.”

“Then press it yourself.”

Baek Si-on: “......”

If he could have pressed it himself, he would never have come all this way to be scolded.

Watching him stung into silence, Lee Ji-eun finally laughed out loud.

“Feeling better now?”

She knew exactly what he meant. An hour ago, on that sofa, she had been coated head to toe in the ‘Leon’ mess. Now the funk was gone. Finding fault with Baek Si-on, it turned out, possessed formidable therapeutic value.

Her finger settled on the new-track button, the corner of her mouth rising:

“One more time.”

❧ ❧ ❧

After a number of passes nobody was counting anymore, Lee Ji-eun finally keyed the talkback.

“That works.”

Baek Si-on pulled off the headphones and walked out of the booth, picked up the water on the desk, and drank half the bottle in one go. He had genuinely lost count of his own takes.

Lee Ji-eun, of course, remembered every one. An opportunity like this — the man delivering himself to be found fault with — was not to be wasted.

Water finished, Baek Si-on sat down at the console and played her saved demo top to tail. The vocal had reached the completeness a demo requires. Some arrangement details were still raw, and the drums would wait for Jung Jae-joon’s return — but for judging melody, vocal structure, and overall direction, it was enough.

The outro faded. He took off the headphones and exported the audio. Lyric concept, production notes, and the first demo went into a single email — addressee, Riot’s project team; cc, Scooter.

The delivery, as sent

To: Riot Games · project team — cc: Scooter

Attachments: lyric concept · production notes · first full demo — upload complete. Send.

A page pops up: Sent successfully. The first complete demo, formally delivered. Fine arrangement, official vocals, live-instrument passes, and mastering all wait on Riot’s feedback.

Lee Ji-eun waited until he had finished the whole operation before speaking.

“You can tell me now, right?”

Baek Si-on closed the mail client.

“Tell you what?”

Her eyebrows came down degree by degree.

“You said — besides recording you, there was something else.”

Only now did Baek Si-on remember the bait he’d dangled to get his temporary labor in the door.

“Do you know Kim Eun-sook’s new drama?”

“‘Descendants of the Sun’?”

“Mm.”

‘The Producers’ and ‘Descendants of the Sun’ were both flagship KBS projects — one fresh off principal photography, one already in production. The two crews had no public overlap, but inside one broadcaster, news never quite quarantines. Lee Ji-eun had of course heard of it. What interested her was why Baek Si-on was suddenly raising that drama. With ‘The Producers’ barely wrapped, him bringing up Kim Eun-sook’s new project — a faintly absurd guess took shape fast.

Was he about to recommend her for the cast now?

Was this man planning to corner the market on her future drama roles?

The thought made a full lap of her head without surfacing; she only tested the water:

“That drama has already started filming, hasn’t it?”

“It has.”

Baek Si-on offered nothing further. He looked down and tidied his project files as if the one sentence fully explained his purpose here. Lee Ji-eun waited out the folder closing, and her patience closed with it.

“So?”

Baek Si-on finally looked up.

“I took the OST for that drama.”

The answer had veered clean away from her guess. Lee Ji-eun blinked once, and instinct seized the microphone.

“How much?”

“Two hundred million won.”

“Are you robbing them?!”

“I already gave them the friendship price.”

Lee Ji-eun studied him, openly suspecting that this man labored under a severe misunderstanding of the word friendship. The Korean drama market prices an OST against singer tier, rights ownership, production involvement, and project level. Established regulars of Kim Tae-yeon’s and Baek Ji-young’s caliber typically land between twenty and forty million won for pure vocal work; writing or composing pushes it higher. A full commercial collaboration at Lee Ji-eun’s current streaming gravity could already touch one hundred million won. And this man had opened at two hundred million.

The rate card, as it stood

Top OST regulars, vocal only: twenty to forty million won a song.

Writing or composing attached, or a demanding brief: the number climbs.

Lee Ji-eun, full commercial collaboration, at current gravity: touching one hundred million won.

Baek Si-on’s opening bid for one drama OST: two hundred million — the friendship price, as quoted.

“If the friendship price is two hundred million, what does it cost without the friendship?”

Baek Si-on reopened the mail client instead of answering. Riot’s letter of intent still sat in the attachment list. He pulled up the quote page and turned the laptop toward her — and the number in the center of the page let Lee Ji-eun’s expression set slowly, like concrete.

The page, as turned toward her

First she confirmed the currency unit. Then she counted the digits again, left to right.

$3,000,000

“Three million dollars?”

“Mm.”

“One song??”

Baek Si-on corrected the scope: the price was not for one song — it also covered the Worlds finals performance, the event-cycle promotional licensing, and part of the visual creative collaboration.

“That’s outrageous too!”

She typed the exchange rate into her phone. The conversion came out past three point three billion won. She checked it a second time; the number refused to change.

“You came here specially to show me this, didn’t you?”

“Primarily to correct your mistaken view of market prices.”

“Say it in human words.”

Baek Si-on accepted the interrogation.

“Showing off.”

“I knew it.”

❧ ❧ ❧

The shock of the three-million-dollar screenshot was still lodged in her head. She went back through the contract items once more and couldn’t help sighing: no wonder everyone wanted to go to America now. The ceiling of the Korean market was simply too legible — even a song that topped every domestic chart could hardly match, in long-term commercial value, the global distribution system. Cross genuinely into the North American mainstream, and quotes, rights income, and brand deals all multiplied.

Baek Si-on declined to join the chorus for America.

“It’s not that simple.”

Lee Ji-eun waited for the rest.

“My luck was good. And the road I came in on — nobody can copy it step for step.”

It was true. A Korean singer can release English singles, work with American producers, appear on North American shows — and still never duplicate the single path formed by film awards, sports culture, esports, global business, and music all braided together.

Hearing it, Lee Ji-eun concluded that this man managed to be infuriating even when he was being modest — because the content of his modesty was still: the road I walked is unlearnable.

She pointed at the console.

“So a producer who charges three million dollars a song pays me off with two boxes of chocolate?”

She counted on her fingers:

“I just worked three hours as your engineer. Two boxes covers, generously, one.”

“The rest was voluntary overtime.”

“......”

She finally understood why employees needed labor contracts.

“Is that guitar still with you?” Baek Si-on asked.

Lee Ji-eun’s hand stopped mid-air. She knew exactly which one he meant — the Gibson J-160E that had belonged to John Lennon, lost for more than fifty years and then surfaced again, currently resting in the display cabinet at her home. Baek Si-on had told her back then to keep it safe. And she had genuinely, conscientiously kept it safe ever since.

“It’s here.”

“Then we’ll fold it in against the production fees.”

Lee Ji-eun: “......”

She had only been trying to prove that two boxes of chocolate could not buy three hours. Now an obscenely valuable piece of collateral had joined the settlement, and the labor dispute upgraded on the spot from snack subsidy to international auction. She took out her phone and searched the guitar’s last public sale price.

The debt, as computed

Last public sale: $2,400,000 — at the current rate, approaching 2.7 billion won. She stared at the screen in silence.

Her side of the ledger, full production credit at one hundred million won a song: one song. Two songs. Ten songs. Twenty songs — the fingers ran out before the number did.

Running total: at today’s rate, the number still refused to zero out.

At tonight’s labor intensity, clearing one guitar would take fifty-two more evenings like this one.

“Twenty-six songs?”

Baek Si-on reminded her: at the moving exchange rate, possibly half a song more.

“How does half a song work?”

He nodded at the screen.

“Just now.”

Yesterday, passing that display cabinet, she had found the guitar romantic. Now that she thought about it — how was that a gift? This was a music labor contract with no stated term, a staggering workload, and interest indexed to the exchange rate!

Lee Ji-eun finally raised her head.

“Back then you said to keep it safe. You never said it would offset production fees.”

“If you don’t want it, you can give it back.”

“......”

One nod, and the absurd debt of twenty-six songs would zero out instantly. Lee Ji-eun did not feel relieved by the offer — she felt a thin, specific irritation. She locked the phone screen.

“Who said I don’t want it?”

Returning it now would amount to declaring she couldn’t afford the gift. That answer was never coming out of her mouth.

Baek Si-on leaned back in the chair and answered as if it were obvious.

“Want it? Then trade work for it.”

“Fine.”

She said it without a gram of shame. Baek Si-on didn’t fight over half a song. From the day the guitar left his hands, he had never planned to take it back. The production debt was, at most, a reusable reason for two people to keep working together — whatever ledger Lee Ji-eun wanted to keep, she could keep.

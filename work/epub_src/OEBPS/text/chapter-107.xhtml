Riot: Change of Plan — Imagine Dragons Out, Baek Si-on In

October 2 Evening Nonhyeon-dong · Big Hit · 4F The first billion is on the book. The first spend is not the boys. It is the sentence the search engine still wants to keep.

The night the first billion won confirmed on the account, Bang Si-hyuk did not add budget to Bangtan. He hired press.

Girl group was girl group. Boy group was boy group. The member in the case was the member in the case. Company duty was company duty. Bangtan Sonyeondan had debuted a year ago and was working. They were not a scandal’s extra. That logic had to be said until it was boring. Different papers. Different angles. Different headlines. Until the related search began to loosen. Until a user opening a piece did not meet, first line, Big Hit girl-group extortion drags Bangtan down.

He called entertainment desks, board runners, a few of the newer net papers. Insight was one of the outlets that took money and did the work. When Baek Si-on heard it from Son Nam-won he sat with it a while. He had not expected his own money to leave the left hand, walk a circle, and come back in the right.

Second thing. Bring the boys home from America. Dark & Wild had already fallen down. That was a fact. Handing flyers out on a Los Angeles street would not suddenly teach America that K-pop had a life after Gangnam Style. That was not promotion. That was spending the children’s legs, heart, time, and a company that did not have much left to spend. The rest of the money would go into the next album.

No more of the thing that sat in the middle and pleased nobody. There had to be a concept. One box that could hold their youth, their hurt, their ambition, their lostness, their no. The prettiest age. The least steady age. The age that thought it could do anything and got its head opened by a wall.

He picked up a pen. Four characters on the draft paper.

Bang Si-hyuk · draft

The Most Beautiful Moment in Life

He looked at the line a long time. A knock.

“Daepyonim. The kids are here.”

“Practice room.”

“Yes.”

He closed the paper. Stood. Stomach met the desk edge. He looked down. Tch. Still had to eat less chicken.

The practice room. Seven boys in two rows. Home from America less than four days. The clock in the body not yet turned. He had not given them a turning. Printed lyric, printed chart, on the floor. One song, a little over two hours. He sat at the back and did not talk much. Interrupted when he had to. Nam-joon, don’t push that line. Ji-min, the shoulder can’t drop. Jung-kook, leave the breath before the chorus. Daytime he had been living with PR, counsel’s letters, GLAM’s exit fights. Night, in a practice room, he could still hear which beat had the wrong feeling. That was the frightening part of him. Fat was fat. Chicken was chicken. The ear was real. While everyone else thought Big Hit should first stay alive, he was already thinking what they would sing after they were alive.

October 2 11:47 pm A room that was called a dorm because seven people slept in it Clothes, shoe boxes, delivery bags, practice kits, charge cables. Sogyeok-dong on a phone. Then Billboard, which did not ask permission to enter a Korean night.

“Let people know who we are first.”

Kim Nam-joon was not in that talk. He was on a phone that was not Korea. After America nobody had opened the subject of what the trip had been. They papered it with dead-tired, jet-lag is hard, American burgers are huge. He had a knot. So at night he looked at that market, at the songs that actually entered American ears. Then he saw Baek Si-on. Legend. Billboard Hot 100. Forty-eight.

He stared. First time he thought he had misread. Refresh. Load. Still forty-eight. Artist: Baek Si-on. Title: Legend. Forty-eight. First week.

PSY’s Gangnam Style had come in at sixty-four, and a large part of that sixty-four had been YouTube, a horse dance, the world’s curiosity spilling sideways — not only people buying a song. Legend had Spotify editorial, radio, iTunes money. It had gone into the front fifty on the song. Clean in a way that was frightening.

“Daebak…”

“What?”

“Come here. Look at this.”

He turned the phone. Six heads made a knot around a five-and-a-half-inch glass.

October 4 Late morning PDT Los Angeles · hotel suite · a table of printouts Itineraries, tickets, brand letters. A manager who had not had to plead in a long time. A man who would not buy out a Korean paper with two million dollars.

Half a curtain. California light on the low table. Scooter Braun sat opposite, face serious enough that Baek Eun-ah zipped the case quieter without deciding to.

“I don’t understand.”

The impatience was already in the voice.

“Why do you have to go back to Korea?”

Si-on, in the single chair, had a Korean schedule confirmation in his hand. He did not fill the air. Scooter took a breath. It had been a long time since he had had to talk an artist into staying, especially one whose ink was still new. His plan after a first-week forty-eight was two more weeks of gold: radio, late night, sports desks, music blogs, then Bong’s Kobe cut as a second bomb. The song could see the twenties. The twenties would change the name. Not the Korean Venice actor who also sang. The first Korean man to walk a non-idol road into Billboard’s front twenty. The tag was worth seven later machines in Scooter’s head without being asked. A small North American tour. A sports open. A brand mash. Film-music rooms. A second late-night. And the man said he was going home.

“Baek. Do you know what you’re shortest on right now?”

“Sleep.”

“No.”

He almost followed the joke into a ditch.

“Continuity. A market remembers a bombing, not a visit. You let Americans learn the name and then you vanish.”

“You leave now, you come back, half of it may already have gone.”

Si-on put the paper down.

“I know.”

“You know and you’re still going?”

“Those papers were signed before yours. I have to shoot. The Green Fly has a Korean opening to stand in. KB, Hyundai, SK — the days in those contracts have to be walked.”

Scooter’s brow moved.

“How much is the break fee. Two million enough?”

Eun-ah’s hands stopped in a half-folded shirt. Two million. More than two billion won. For most Korean artists a number that could punch a hole through an obligation. Si-on’s face did not do the number the courtesy of changing.

“Contract isn’t a thing you keep when it’s convenient and buy out when it isn’t.”

“If I can walk away from a Korean partner today because a bigger number showed up, what makes you think I won’t use the same logic on you tomorrow.”

Scooter had nothing that would sit on that. He started a sentence, ate the rest of it, stood.

“Ten minutes.”

Phone. Door. Closed. Eun-ah looked up.

“Oppa.”

“Mm.”

“What do we do?”

He went back into the chair.

“What we were going to do.”

“Oh.”

The half-folded hoodie went into the case. Passport, contract copies, laptop, cables, a few changes, the drive with the sessions, the scans Seo Eun-ju had said did not leave a bag. While she packed she remembered, from a thinner year, him talking about the rules of the road. Then he had been a man still climbing, still giving a CP face, still placing stones in a Korean net. Now a North American manager sat in a hotel living room and pleaded. He was still talking rules. The word had only gotten harder. Credit.

Ten minutes. The door. Scooter came back looking like a manager again.

“Baek.”

Si-on looked up.

“You have to be back the twenty-eighth.”

“Why?”

“Kobe’s first game back. You’re singing it live.”

The zipper in Eun-ah’s hand stopped again. Kobe. First of the season. Live. Not a paid stage. Sport narrative welded to a song.

“All right.”

“You say yes fast.”

“That’s one you say yes to.”

Scooter made a sound in the nose. Phone.

“And October nineteenth. Seoul World Cup Stadium, Sangam.”

“To do what?”

“League of Legends Season 4 World Championship. Opening.”

Si-on looked at him. First thought: he had misheard. Second: no. If he remembered right, the opening had been Imagine Dragons. A global bracket did not wait until October to find a singer. Which meant Scooter had just pushed him into a hole that had already had a name in it.

“You’re sure.”

“I’m sure.”

Scooter sat, went back into the sofa.

“Riot had a plan. My argument was simple. A global event in Korea should be opened by a Korean star who is in the middle of taking the globe. Legend sits on a competitive floor. The song and the game want the same weather.”

“…Thank you.”

He did not say thank you often. In the short time Scooter had known him the range had been mm and all right. Thank you meant the thing had actually landed. Scooter nodded, took it, then looked past him at Eun-ah, who was holding clothes and had forgotten to fold them.

“Sweetheart.”

She turned.

“Every dollar this stage makes sits with me.”

“OK.”

Clean. The building was in Seoul. On paper it was hers. She did not have Riot’s global events mailbox. She could not, in ten minutes, talk an American game company into swapping an opening that was already carved. Scooter watched her, checking whether the OK was pride covering a bruise. She said it again.

“Commercial take from this stage is yours. No problem. You did it.”

He smiled, a small one.

“Sweetheart. I’m starting to like you.”

The look she gave him came up fast.

“Work only.”

“Work only.”

Back to Si-on.

“When you go home, do not walk yourself into a political hole.”

“Your news is fast.”

“I’m a manager. The expensive kind.”

Si-on smiled once. Scooter’s shoulder did a thing.

“Go. See you at the end of the month.”

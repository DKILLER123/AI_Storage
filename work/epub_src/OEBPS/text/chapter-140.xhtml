Chapter One Hundred Forty

Thank You, Madam President, for Making Time

November 8 · night The world premiere of a commercial film Seoul · CGV · the Fashion King premiere screening A full house watching a comic revenge scene, and two people in the back row who cannot stop talking about a man in another building.

The house lights were down.

On the big screen, the story had reached the scene where the male lead, to prove himself in front of a room full of people, pulls off some very comic-book-style counterattack.

By the logic of the scene, this was where the audience’s emotions were supposed to be rising.

But Choi Jin-ri, seated in the guest row at the front, heard a low murmur start up somewhere behind her.

“Ya — did you see the news?”

“What news?”

“Baek Si-on wore court robes to the awards ceremony!”

“What court robes?”

“The period costume! The crown prince one! They say he flew straight from the Sado set by helicopter!”

“No way.”

“An SK helicopter, a Hyundai convoy, police escorts — it’s already trending!”

“……”

Choi Jin-ri’s ears pricked up.

They caught keywords automatically.

Baek Si-on.

On an ordinary day, that name put her in a good mood. Such a good mood that an ordinary iced americano could taste like honey.

This was not an ordinary day.

An audience at her own film’s premiere was spending the running time discussing Baek Si-on.

Which meant, in plain terms, that Fashion King as a finished film was already being beaten by a man who was not even in the building.

Choi Jin-ri lowered her eyes and looked at her own hands.

She could not be angry.

She did not have the right to be angry.

Because if she were not sitting here in the premiere audience but at home scrolling her phone, she would probably have clicked open the live broadcast of Baek Si-on in court robes faster than either of those two people in the back row.

Probably faster.

The thought almost made her laugh.

She really was no better.

The film went on.

Every so often a laugh broke out somewhere in the auditorium.

It was not a cold audience.

But it was not exactly on fire either.

Choi Jin-ri put herself back in her place as an actor and watched the second half properly.

At least this was her work.

Even if she knew, somewhere very honest inside herself, that the roles she truly wanted to be remembered for were not these pretty, bright, weightless ones.

✿ ✿ ✿

The film ended.

The lights came up.

Applause ran through the auditorium.

The cast came up on stage, the host began the introductions, and Choi Jin-ri stood there smiling, bowing, and answering questions.

Someone asked how it felt to take on her first leading role in a commercial film.

She said she was honoured, and thanked the director and her seniors for looking after her.

Someone in the audience told her she was very pretty.

She smiled and said thank you.

And someone asked whether she worried that Fashion King and The Green Bottle Fly had come out so close together that the contrast between the two images might be too sharp.

Choi Jin-ri held the microphone and thought about it.

“I think an actor should be allowed more than one face.”

She smiled.

“I’m not good enough at it yet. But I’ll keep working on it.”

It was the standard answer.

It was also the true one.

When the Q&A ended and the cast came off stage, Choi Jin-ri had barely reached the backstage hallway before her phone was in her hand.

Her manager noticed her head bent over the screen and called out a reminder:

“Sulli — the media photos are soon.”

“Mm. Just one quick look.”

She opened the live stream.

The picture took two seconds to load.

And came up.

November 8 · the same night Live, from the National Theater of Korea On the phone screen · the tail end of the Korean Popular Culture and Arts Awards The encouraging citations are done. What is left is the part nobody in the building expected.

By now the ceremony was almost over.

The warm-up honours — the Minister of Culture, Sports and Tourism’s citation, the Prime Minister’s citation — had all been handed out.

On the live picture, the host stood at the centre of the National Theater stage.

His voice rang out with ceremony:

“And now we come to the Cultural Medal of this year’s Korean Popular Culture and Arts Awards.”

“The Silver Crown Cultural Medal goes to —”

“Actor, Baek Si-on!”

Applause went up at once.

On the great screen behind the stage, a video of his career highlights began to play.

Clips from The Green Bottle Fly.

The Venice red carpet.

The whole of the Sala Grande rising to its feet.

Baek Si-on standing on a Venice stage holding the Volpi Cup.

The Legend performance at the world championship.

A screenshot of the Billboard Hot 100 official site.

The Lakers season opener, Baek Si-on and Kobe slapping hands.

The sea of people at Incheon International Airport.

Down at the bottom of the live picture, the comments were racing past so fast that Choi Jin-ri had to squint to read them.

Choi Jin-ri watched for a few seconds, then reached out and switched them off.

The picture came clear.

The camera had settled on Baek Si-on.

The blue gonryongpo. The winged coronet. The jade belt catching the light at his waist. The cloud-pattern boots standing on the carpet of the theater.

He looked as though he had walked straight out of one of those “Portrait of a Joseon Crown Prince” paintings hanging in the National Museum of Korea.

“He really did come in this…”

Choi Jin-ri had assumed, until now, that the internet was exaggerating.

Netizens were always doing that — calling “very good-looking” a descent of the gods, calling “dressed formally” a prince’s procession.

This time they had not exaggerated.

If anything, they had undersold it.

The host’s voice-over continued.

“Actor Baek Si-on shone at the 71st Venice International Film Festival with The Green Bottle Fly, winning the Volpi Cup for Best Actor and proving the formidable power of Korean screen actors to the world!”

“Since then, Actor Baek Si-on has held the number-one spot on the American Billboard Hot 100 singles chart for two consecutive weeks with the English single Legend — an achievement that stands as a milestone in the history of Korean popular music!”

“Crossing film, music and global popular culture, with outstanding artistic achievement and worldwide influence, he has raised the standing of Korean culture in the world to a remarkable degree.”

“In recognition of his outstanding contributions to the fields of culture and the arts—”

The host turned the card in his hand, and his voice grew heavier.

“The Silver Crown Cultural Medal will be presented to Mr. Baek Si-on personally by the President of the Republic of Korea.”

✿ ✿ ✿

On the little screen, the broadcast cut to the entrance at the back of the stage.

The Korean Popular Culture and Arts Awards · live · the National Theater of Korea

The red curtain is drawn back by staff, and a figure in a navy suit steps slowly into the frame.

Every person in the theater is on their feet. The applause breaks out a second time.

Through the applause, Baek Si-on rises from the front row, the gonryongpo settling around him as he moves. He gives a small bow to the seniors and officials on either side of him, then starts up the centre steps.

Step. Step. Step. The applause does not fade as he climbs. If anything, the picture — absurd and solemn at once — seems to set it burning again.

Centre stage. He bows to the President first; she inclines her head in return. The attendant comes up carrying the medal — the Silver Crown lying quietly on a dark tray — and the camera pushes in for the close-up.

The President lifts the medal, one of the highest honours the state can give, and pins it to his chest, over the gonryongpo. The instant the silver crown settles against the blue fabric, the absurdity of the whole scene climbs another notch.

Applause rolls through the hall a third time.

“Your Highness the Crown Prince — thank you for conquering the American music charts for your country.”

He looks down at the medal on his chest, then up again.

“And thank you, Madam President, for finding time in your busy schedule to present it to your subject.”

The line is perfectly courteous — and just a little bit clever. On the feed, the President’s smile pauses, only for a fraction of a second.

In truth, by every reasonable precedent, she should not have attended in person today.

But the record was too pretty to ignore. A Venice Volpi Cup. Two straight weeks at number one on the Billboard Hot 100. This was no longer an ordinary celebrity bringing honour to the country; it was a dish big enough to set on the table of the national narrative.

You must attend and present it yourself.

Besides, relations between the Blue House and the more left-leaning film community had soured badly lately. The blacklist was still only a murmur — not yet blown wide open — but people in the cultural world had already begun to smell it. For the President to appear at the Popular Culture and Arts Awards in person would do two things at once: it would absorb some of the resentment building in the cultural sector, and it would let the public, for a while, see a Blue House that was warm, open, and embracing of popular culture.

Political theatre was nothing to be ashamed of.

What was shameful was doing it badly.

And yet, at this exact moment, the President found herself wondering whether “finding time in your busy schedule” had carried a second meaning.

She could not blame herself for the suspicion.

But the young man’s face was too sincere. His bow had been too respectful. There he stood, in his crown prince’s court robes, with the Silver Crown medal she had just pinned on with her own hands, his manners so complete that there was not a single flaw to pick at.

She set the faint unease aside.

She held out her hand.

Baek Si-on took it.

The shutter sounds came thick and fast.

Click.

Click.

Click.

This time the press area was genuinely losing its mind. They all knew that this photograph would be on the front page of every newspaper, portal, news channel and forum homepage tomorrow.

Holding his hand, the President offered a word of encouragement.

“I hope that Actor Baek Si-on will continue to contribute to bringing Korean culture to the world.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“I’ll do my best.”

The President smiled, patted the back of his hand, and turned to leave.

Applause rose once more.

The stage was handed back to Baek Si-on.

✿ ✿ ✿

He walked to the standing microphone, reached out, and adjusted its height — unhurried, in no particular rush.

Then he raised his head and looked out at the sea of faces below the stage.

Officials. Seniors. Directors. Singers. Idols. Media. And, beyond all of them, the countless viewers watching the live broadcast on television and on their phones.

“I’m sorry for showing up like this.”

That first line got a ripple of laughter through the whole hall.

The comments surged along with it.

November 8 · the same night One look, and then some Seoul · CGV · the small lounge behind the premiere stage A phone propped against a water bottle, a manager knocking three times, and a road that has just become clear.

Choi Jin-ri watched the rest of the broadcast from the lounge.

She had come backstage to take one look at the news.

One look had become five minutes.

Her manager knocked on the door three times.

Choi Jin-ri pretended not to hear.

Then, on the screen, Baek Si-on stepped down off the stage, and the picture froze on the frame of the whole theater on its feet.

And something rose in her chest that she could not quite put a name to.

Pride.

And, along with it, a faint ache.

The ache was not jealousy.

It was closer to this: she understood, again, and very clearly, that Baek Si-on had already walked to a place very high up, very bright, and very much in view of a great many people.

And she was still sitting in a backstage lounge, at the end of the premiere Q&A for one commercial film.

Choi Jin-ri looked down at her own hands.

Then back at the screen.

And smiled, very lightly.

It was all right.

She would get there too.

Even if it took a little longer.

At least now she knew which road she wanted to take.

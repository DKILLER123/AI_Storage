Chapter One Hundred Thirty Seven

The King Who Kept the Crown

November 2 Late night · after the evening shoot Jeonju · the Sado cast hotel · basement parking A man scolded all day by Yeongjo comes home to find a car waiting for him. Cars, it turns out, apologise better than mouths.

Jeonju.

The underground parking of the hotel where the Sado company was staying.

By the time Baek Si-on came back from the night shoot, it was nearly eleven.

The most punishing part of a period drama was not the long dialogue. Not the intricate etiquette.

It was that, after being shouted at by Yeongjo all day, you still had to spend another age at the end of it taking off the costume and unpicking the headpiece.

The moment Baek Si-on stepped down from his van, Baek Eun-ah stopped walking.

“Oppa.”

“Mm?”

“Over there.”

Baek Si-on followed her gaze.

In the most prominent bay of the parking lot sat a black van.

Its roof ran a good deal higher than an ordinary nanny van’s. Dark, shatter-resistant film on the windows. And on the nose, the badge of Hyundai Motor.

Three people stood beside it.

The one at the front was Hyundai’s marketing director himself.

Seeing Baek Si-on appear, the marketing director smiled and came forward.

“Baek Si-on-ssi. Hard work on the shoot.”

Baek Si-on inclined his head toward him.

“Director-nim. You didn’t have to come yourself.”

“I did.”

The director gestured at the tall-roofed vehicle behind him.

“This is a small token from inside the company — a Grand Starex in high-roof luxury custom trim, prepared specially for you.”

Baek Eun-ah’s eyes moved, very slightly.

Grand Starex.

High-roof.

Luxury custom trim.

She had already started converting that into a price in her head.

Halfway through, she decided to stop.

Lately, for the sake of her mental health, she should not be in frequent contact with high-priced vehicles, Patek Philippes, or gifts from Gulf royalty.

The marketing director went on.

“Given how often you’ve been moving lately — shooting here in Jeonju, promotions in Seoul, back and forth to the airport, overseas schedules — the group would like this vehicle to let you and your team rest a little more comfortably on the road.”

The door was electric.

His assistant pressed a catch by the handle, and the door slid smoothly open to the side.

Warm-white ambient light came up inside the rear cabin.

Baek Eun-ah leaned in for one look, and her eyes lit up on the spot.

Platinum-toned leather. Individual airline-style seats. A screen. An on-board fridge. A rotating business seat. Even a small hanging rail for clothes.

Baek Si-on looked too. Then he turned to the marketing director.

“What does this mean?”

“This vehicle is a celebration gift for the Aslan passing ten thousand units — the brand’s recognition of its ambassador’s contribution. It sits outside the gift clauses of the endorsement contract.”

His assistant handed over a folder.

“The registration, insurance and user’s manual are all in here. If anything is inconvenient later, Hyundai will assign someone to handle it with you directly.”

Baek Eun-ah reached out and took it.

Too fast.

She realised only after she had taken it how eager that had looked.

She cleared her throat, and turned her head toward Baek Si-on.

Baek Si-on understood at once.

This was not simply a car.

This was a mending of the relationship.

After the Lakers season opener, SK Telecom had been the first to throw its weight behind him — they had even joked that if he ever needed to step up for a friend again, he could wear one of their global-roaming tees while doing it.

Hyundai Motor, by contrast, had been noticeably more cautious.

They had not ended the contract.

But they had not come out publicly in his corner either.

Watching. Waiting.

In business, that word — “wait and see” — was normal.

In human terms, it did not sound good.

Especially not when Baek Si-on had just opened the Aslan’s launch with four thousand first-day orders, and driven Hyundai Motor’s share price up into something people were calling the Baek Si-on effect.

Hyundai had clearly realised it too.

So this vehicle had come.

A car, it turned out, was more sincere than an apology made in words.

“Thank you, Hyundai.”

The marketing director let out a small breath of relief, and hurried to say:

“And thank you, Baek Si-on-ssi.”

The two of them bowed to each other, and that was that — the matter settled.

Baek Eun-ah, folder in her arms, looked at the van, then at her cousin.

She felt something.

A few months ago they had been agonising over whether a second-hand Kia Carnival could survive their schedule.

Now Hyundai Motor was handing over a high-roof luxury custom nanny van, unprompted, purely to smooth over a crack that might have existed.

The snobbery of the entertainment world was real.

But if you stood high enough, even snobbery came dressed as politeness.

November 5 Morning · between takes Jeonju · the Sado set One call from across the ocean, and a schedule that was already full quietly dies.

Three days later. November the fifth.

The Jeonju set.

After finishing an interior scene that morning, Baek Si-on sat in a chair in the rest area, the Sado script open across his knees.

Park Ji-hun crouched in front of him, small mirror and wax in hand, preparing the look for the afternoon’s scene.

The Grand Starex in high-roof luxury custom trim was parked a little way off.

Inside it, Baek Eun-ah sat working through S.W Studio’s mail — the tax papers Seo Eun-ju had sent, the album pre-order settlement tables, the Hyundai handover documents — stacked all around her.

Outside the tent, the assistant director was working out camera positions for the next scene with the director of photography.

Everything in order.

And then Baek Si-on’s phone buzzed.

The screen lit up.

Incoming call:

Scooter Braun.

For Baek Si-on, that name was no longer an alarm clock ringing across an ocean.

These days, a call from Scooter usually meant his calendar was about to be rewritten again.

Baek Si-on looked at it for two seconds.

He picked up.

Baek Si-on ← Scooter Braun · Jeonju set · incoming

“Hello.”

“Baek! Massive news!”

Baek Si-on turned a page of the script.

“What?”

“Taylor Swift has pulled her entire catalogue off Spotify.”

Baek Si-on’s finger stopped at the edge of the page. Scooter was talking fast. He had clearly been excited for a while.

“The whole of 1989. Her entire catalogue from when she debuted at sixteen to now. Her team’s stated reason is opposition to Spotify’s free tier — that free streaming cheapens the value of music and hurts creators and the record industry.”

Baek Si-on’s first thought, once he had taken it in, was simple. Legend was very likely to hold the Billboard number one next week. Because his biggest rival had just voluntarily walked off the streaming field. It really was a piece of massive good news — however absurd the way it had arrived. Other people climbed the charts with radio play, with sales, with fan pushes. His side kept winning because the opposition kept dismantling one of its own legs.

“So the point of this call is to tell me not to comment to the media about her decision?”

That was very much the kind of call Scooter would make in advance — keeping him from being led by reporters, from becoming a headline reading “Baek Si-on comments on Taylor Swift leaving Spotify,” from turning a chart race into a fan war.

“No.”

“Mm?”

“I’m calling to tell you that Spotify is about to pick you up and throw you into the sky like a pig on a rising wind.”

“????”

“A pig?”

“Figure of speech, Baek. Don’t fuss over the metaphor. Fuss over the content.”

“……”

For the next three minutes, Scooter talked. Baek Si-on did not interrupt. He was absorbing everything the news implied.

Taylor’s decision, in November 2014, was not “one artist fewer” for Spotify.

It was a systemic crisis.

Because Taylor did not represent only herself.

She stood for the head-on collision between the old record industry and the new streaming age.

To survive, Spotify had to answer.

It could not simply issue a statement saying “we respect artists.” That was too soft. Too much like a man who has been punched and stands there saying he’s fine.

It needed a counter-punch.

Immediately. Now. In an overwhelming posture, telling the whole world:

the streaming age does not need any one particular artist. The platform itself has the power to make gods.

And the most effective way to prove that was not to beg Taylor to stay.

It was to manufacture a new god.

Baek Si-on happened to stand exactly on that point in time.

By every ordinary rule of thumb, Spotify should never have picked him first.

The Weeknd could have been chosen. Ed Sheeran could have. Bruno Mars could have.

But the problem was that those people were already top-tier. Spotify or no Spotify, they sat at the apex of the pyramid.

If Spotify lifted them up, at best it proved the platform could serve superstars.

Icing on the cake.

Not enough.

What Spotify needed was the coal-in-the-snow kind of story — the one that changes a person’s entire fate, that lets the whole world see “the platform made him.”

And yet, even by the normal course of business decision-making, Baek Si-on still would not have been chosen.

Because Spotify’s internal marketing, catalogue and A&R departments would all instinctively pick the safer Western homegrown act.

A Korean? An Asian face? A cultural barrier? The approval of the middle-aged white male North American mainstream?

Every one of those was a question mark.

And every question mark could kill a promotion plan.

The only person inside Spotify who could force the name “Baek Si-on” onto the boardroom agenda was Scooter Braun.

He was a Spotify shareholder.

A small one, yes.

But he could make himself heard in a boardroom.

And his gift of the gab no longer needed any proof.

Once he had digested Scooter’s three minutes, Baek Si-on finally spoke.

Baek Si-on ← Scooter Braun · the same call, continued

“What do I need to do?”

“Record those three songs and send them over, obviously.”

On the other end of the line, Scooter did not hesitate for a second.

Baek Si-on looked down at the Sado script on his knee. The Korean film industry and the American music industry had, through his body, completed a cross-border conspiracy at last. Its verdict: his sleep was sentenced to death.

“Three days.”

“What?”

“I’ll record all three songs and send them to you within three days.”

There was half a second of quiet on the line. Then Scooter laughed.

“This is what I like about you, Baek. Other people hear a workload like that and start complaining first. You just give me a delivery date.”

Baek Si-on said, flatly:

“Complaining wouldn’t record the songs for me.”

“Very correct.”

Scooter’s voice brightened again.

“One more thing. LeBron still hasn’t reached out about a song. I’m a little surprised.”

“Didn’t you say he’d probably come to you?”

“By the usual logic, yes. Maybe Rich Paul’s still watching from the sideline. Maybe LeBron’s team feels that coming to you first, right now, doesn’t look good on their posture.”

Scooter snorted lightly.

“Doesn’t matter. The King narrative doesn’t have to wait for him anymore.”

“Meaning?”

“Sing it about yourself.”

Scooter said it bluntly.

“You’re a Billboard champion. The number-one name in Korean pop. A historical moment in Asian pop music. A Venice Best Actor. The centre of the NBA narrative. You singing a song called King — no problem at all.”

“Don’t be shy, Baek. Your record right now earns you the right to be arrogant.”

“I’m not shy.”

“Even better.”

Scooter laughed.

“Right. If Legend really does hold number one tomorrow, remember to look a little happy about it.”

“Why?”

“Because the whole world is losing its mind for you. If you stay too calm, we’re the ones who end up looking like a bunch of lunatics who’ve never seen anything.”

Baek Si-on thought about it.

“You do look like that.”

“……”

The call ended.

The truth, of course, was that Baek Si-on’s rivals kept falling over for no reason of his doing. But who cared?

November 6 · the same night A night shoot in progress Jeonju · the Sado set · the side hall A bird in a cage, drawn in ink. A phone lighting up at the edge of the set. And a coronation nobody can attend.

The night shoot was still running.

Palace lanterns had been dimmed almost to nothing. Firelight pressed against the paper window screens, throwing a layer of amber shadow across the room.

Baek Si-on sat in the side hall.

He wore the Crown Prince Sado’s everyday robes, hair bound neatly, a sheet of drawing paper spread in front of him.

This scene had no dialogue.

After the court audience, the Crown Prince Sado sits alone in the night, and paints what he feels.

At this point in the story, Sado has not yet fully broken.

He is more like an artistic young man whose ambitions go nowhere — a man with dreams, with talent, and with a belly full of grievances no one understands.

Baek Si-on bent his head over the brush.

The tip touched the paper.

Ink bloomed, slow.

The camera tracked in from his profile and stopped on his eyes.

Lee Joon-ik sat behind the monitor, watching the frame.

The set was very quiet.

At the far edge, Baek Eun-ah stood hugging a thick coat, and her phone screen lit up.

She looked down.

Her whole body froze on the spot.

The screen was lit with the refresh she had been telling herself not to keep checking.

Billboard update · on the phone · 11:00 p.m., Korea time

Legend

Baek Si-on

1

Hot 100. Number one. A second straight week — glowing in the dark at the edge of a silent Joseon set.

Baek Eun-ah very nearly shouted out loud.

Then she saw that the camera was still rolling, and forced the sound back down her throat.

The effort of holding it in was enormous.

Bigger than the first time she had sat down and worked out that three months of her cousin’s income, filed under the top individual-tax bracket, meant ₩1.6 billion in tax.

On the monitor, the Crown Prince Sado set down his last stroke.

On the paper: a bird shut inside a cage.

The bird was not struggling.

It simply had its head lowered — as if it already knew how far away the sky was.

Lee Joon-ik looked at the picture for two seconds.

“Cut.”

The word dropped.

Only then did the set begin to breathe again.

Baek Eun-ah trotted over at once and draped the thick coat over Baek Si-on’s shoulders.

“Oppa!”

Baek Si-on looked up.

“Mm?”

Baek Eun-ah’s voice was kept very low, but its tail was already trying to fly off.

“You kept the Billboard crown! Two weeks!”

“Mm.”

“Just ‘mm’?”

“I’m pleased.”

Baek Eun-ah stared at the thoroughly unbothered face.

“Oppa, you don’t look pleased.”

“Should I pick you up and spin you around, shouting ‘I am a legend’?”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

No. No thank you.

The picture was already forming in her head.

That would not be a celebration.

That would be the whole crew calling the police.

“Number one again?”

Just then Song Kang-ho walked over, also wrapped in a thick coat.

He had evidently heard the news from one of the staff.

Baek Si-on rose and inclined his head to him.

“Mm.”

Song Kang-ho looked at him and shook his head in wonder.

“Our Crown Prince here has been scolded so hard by his father that the American chart doesn’t dare let him go.”

Lee Joon-ik laughed out loud from behind the monitor.

Baek Eun-ah could not hold back a laugh either.

Song Kang-ho played Yeongjo in Sado, and for days now, nearly every single scene had him reducing the Crown Prince Sado, played by Baek Si-on, to a man who could not lift his head.

And now Legend had sat two straight weeks at the top of the American chart.

The line sounded absurd.

And yet, somehow, it held together.

Lee Joon-ik, thermos in hand, added:

“Sunbae, if you scold him another two days, maybe we’ll get three weeks.”

Song Kang-ho blinked.

Then roared with laughter.

Around them, the crew dissolved into laughter as well.

“Director-nim — in that case, should we add a few more scenes of His Majesty scolding the Crown Prince?”

“Can’t. The Crown Prince hasn’t cracked yet. The chart will crack first.”

“Billboard’s afraid of him.”

“Your Majesty, please keep up the pressure.”

“……”

The laughter carried out of the side hall and into the night.

The wind passed beneath the old eaves of the Jeonju buildings.

In the distance, the Grand Starex that Hyundai had delivered sat at the edge of the set, its warm white interior light glowing.

And then —

Baek Eun-ah’s phone buzzed once in her pocket.

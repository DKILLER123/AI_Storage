Chapter Two Hundred Thirty-Nine

The Premiere Invitation

That same night · after the last equipment case

One studio gone quiet · one crew herded out the door

Lee Ji-eun’s studio · the console

Baek Si-on’s earlier “switch projects, wash your ears” had genuinely worked.

Lee Ji-eun had stopped trying to prove who was right and started seriously considering how to fit a retro narrative and an electronic groove into the same song. The overall direction of ‘Leon’ was provisionally settled that night, and the crew captured more than enough footage besides.

Park Myung-soo, for his part, remained firm in his belief that an international superstar was waiting downstairs for his girlfriend to finish her overtime, so the date could continue.

He stood at the door personally rushing the strike, with an energy that suggested the studio lease was about to expire.

“Cables — faster.”

“That machine goes first.”

“Don’t wipe down the tripods one by one before leaving. You can wipe them back at the office.”

The cameraman passed him hugging equipment and couldn’t help asking:

“Sunbae, do you have another schedule today?”

“No.”

“Then why the rush?”

Park Myung-soo’s face went serious.

“A musician’s time is precious.”

Lee Ji-eun stood by the console, certain the sentence pointed at something else. She didn’t dig. What mattered more right now was whether the man downstairs was still there.

The last equipment case rolled into the elevator. The doors shut. The studio went quiet again.

Lee Ji-eun pulled out her phone and scrolled her contacts to the very top.

Lee Ji-eun → Baek Si-on · voice · past midnight

The call connected.

“Where are you?”

“Downstairs.”

Short, from his side. Two syllables of answer.

Lee Ji-eun had prepared herself for two possibilities. One: he had already gone home. Two: he had waited a while, gotten bored, and told his driver to take him somewhere else. There had been no third option.

She packed fast — console power off, bag, lock, elevator. The floor numbers jumped downward. The doors opened, and night air poured in through the lobby entrance. The nearby shops were going dark one by one; only the convenience store and the billboard at the intersection were still lit.

Baek Si-on’s nanny van sat in an unremarkable spot at the curb, its dark privacy film melting the windows into the night.

Lee Ji-eun pulled the door open. Cold air and the warm glow of the reading light spilled out together.

The van, as she inspected it

Baek Si-on reclined in the aviation seat, ‘La La Land’ prep materials open across his knees; the tablet beside him was playing a piano-fingering demonstration, and the only other thing in reach was a half-finished bottle of water.

The folded tray table was clean. No shopping bags. No takeout containers.

She sat down and checked the table one more time before she could help it.

“You really didn’t eat first?”

“Someone forbade it.”

The blanket of late-overtime irritation sitting on her chest was, by that one sentence, gently pressed flat. This man — who since his wrap had crammed two free days full of three songs and Hollywood prep — had genuinely taken her spoiled, half-teasing “no eating first” to heart.

“Since when are you this obedient?”

Baek Si-on closed the tablet.

“Mainly, I’d rather the twenty-three-song debt not keep accruing interest.”

“Cheapskate.”

“Debtors usually describe their creditors that way.”

Lee Ji-eun tugged her seatbelt into place and asked, as casually as she could manufacture:

“And if I record until tomorrow morning?”

“Then that counts as willful default.”

“And then?”

“One song added.”

Her head snapped around.

“Those are two separate things!”

Baek Si-on motioned the driver to go.

“Debtors say that a lot, too.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Chungjeong-ro · one sign still lit

One private reopening · one corporate membership

‘Good Days’ · the aunt’s restaurant

Evening. Chungjeong-ro.

The ‘Good Days’ sign was still lit. Inside, the other customers were long gone; tables and chairs had been wiped down spotless, and a single warm-yellow lamp burned near the kitchen. The driver stopped at the door, and Baek Si-on and Lee Ji-eun pulled on cap and mask and went in. The wind chime above the door gave one soft ring.

Footsteps came quickly from the kitchen. Lee Ji-eun’s aunt walked out in her apron, and at the sight of Baek Si-on her smile went warm at once.

“Si-on’s here? Sit, sit — food’s almost ready.”

Baek Si-on took off his cap and bowed, properly.

“Making you fire the kitchen again this late just for a meal — I’m truly sorry.”

“Sorry about what?”

She waved a hand, laughing.

“You coming to look after my business? I couldn’t be happier.”

Lee Ji-eun protested immediately:

“Auntie, I worked all day — why do you only care about him?”

Her aunt showed no mercy.

“Didn’t he spend all day working alongside you?”

“He’s the one who recruited ME for overtime.”

“And you still left him waiting down there hungry?”

“......”

Baek Si-on stayed out of the family debate and, very naturally, pulled out Lee Ji-eun’s chair for her. The motion was so unforced it barely counted as courtesy — which is exactly why she gave him an extra look.

As her aunt turned back toward the kitchen, Baek Si-on stopped her.

“Auntie — one more thing to trouble you with.”

“What is it?”

“I have four employees in Seongsu, and the company doesn’t have a canteen yet.”

“All young ladies, I take it?”

“Mm.”

She agreed without a second’s hesitation.

“Just send them over. If they eat here regularly, I’ll give them a bit of a discount.”

“No discount. Regular prices.”

He took a bank card out of his wallet — and before she could get another word in, he had laid out the whole scheme.

The staff-meal plan, as tabled

Deposit posted up front: ten million won.

Coverage: one meal every working day, a second on overtime nights — at ten to fifteen thousand won a head per meal, with the occasional extra order, roughly a year’s worth.

Settlement: whether the four actually come, and what they actually spend, reconciles line by line against the consumption records; the restaurant issues compliant tax invoices, and the company books it as employee welfare or work-meal expense.

The prepay itself was pure convenience. Her aunt still found the number steep: “Put in one or two million first. If the girls don’t like the food, you can’t have them trudging here daily just to burn down the balance.” His elder-friendly explanation: “It’s company spending. It offsets tax.” Strictly speaking the deposit could not be deducted in one gulp — it entered the welfare ledger in installments, as the meals were actually eaten and invoiced. But delivering a corporate tax lecture at the door of her own restaurant was unnecessary.

Lee Ji-eun undercut him on the spot:

“Your company has lawyers. And accountants.”

“If the tax office ever audits you, don’t you dare say my auntie taught you tax evasion.”

Her aunt laughed at the two of them.

“Fine — I’ll set up a separate corporate membership card. But let’s be clear: the girls come if they want to. No boss’s orders forcing them to eat the same dish every day.”

“Of course.”

She took the bank card to the back to process it. Only the two of them remained in the restaurant; the warm lamp lay soft across the table. Lee Ji-eun rested her chin on the backs of her hands.

“So — is this the real reason you wanted to eat here?”

Baek Si-on turned to her.

“How would you like me to answer?”

“The truth.”

“Half of it is that the company genuinely needs it.”

“And the other half?”

“The food here is good.”

Her expectation was stopped at the source. She refused to give up.

“A foodie like you must have a whole list of hidden-gem restaurants.”

“Then why did you insist I wait, so we could eat together?”

The counter-question landed back on her. Baek Si-on lifted his water glass.

“The answer’s probably about the same.”

Her lips moved and produced nothing. She lowered her head and picked up her own glass — the rim conveniently shielding the small, uncontrolled curve at the corner of her mouth.

Her aunt came back out with the finished card and caught Lee Ji-eun head-down smiling and Baek Si-on leaned back watching her. Her aunt’s step paused, almost imperceptibly.

Then she walked over as if nothing had happened and set the card and the receipt slip in front of Baek Si-on.

“All done.”

The card, as printed

LAVUE Staff Meal Account

Appendix: daily spending allowances for the four staffers; alcohol and private meals paid personally.

At each month’s end, the itemized statement and invoices go to the company in one packet. Baek Si-on read it through once. “Thank you for the trouble, auntie.” — “Stop saying trouble. Just come eat a couple more times from now on.”

❧ ❧ ❧

The dishes started arriving fast. Her aunt had seen Baek Si-on’s appetite before, and tonight’s prep clearly ran past any normal dinner for two. Even so, she had underestimated how hungry he was. Lee Ji-eun had just wound her second forkful of pasta when the risotto in front of Baek Si-on was already down to a thin lining, and the roast beef had lost half its mass.

She threw a protective arm over the chicken leg nearest her.

“Leave me some!”

Baek Si-on moved her hand aside.

“Hey!”

Lee Ji-eun began speeding up too — beef stuffed in, pasta wound with uncharacteristic sloppiness — until a few bites in, she realized she was doing something profoundly stupid. This was her aunt’s restaurant. Run out of food, they make more. Why on earth was she in a survival competition against a man whose intake equaled two or three grown males?

She slowed back down to her native pace of one hundred chews per mouthful.

“You almost dragged me off course.”

“Competition improves efficiency.”

“Why would eating need efficiency?”

“Because somebody eats too slowly.”

“I’m savoring properly.”

“The chef would be deeply moved to hear it.”

Lee Ji-eun speared a roast chicken leg.

“Then what are you?”

“Action-based feedback.”

“Expressing approval by emptying plates?”

“Direct.”

Her aunt came out with the last dish and found the table mostly cleared. No surprise on her face — just a beaming remark aimed at Baek Si-on:

“Si-on-ah, if the entertainment business ever turns sour, come do mukbang at your auntie’s.”

“The footage might put other restaurants out of business.”

“Even better. Clear out the competition.”

She set the last plate down.

“Tell me when you’ve finished whether it’s enough. There’s more in the kitchen.”

“Okay.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Dinner over, Baek Si-on stood to settle the bill. Her aunt flatly refused to take anything.

“You just deposited ten million, and tonight still gets billed separately?”

“The ten million is the staff meal fund.”

He held the bank card out to her.

“Tonight is private consumption.”

“Then call it auntie’s treat.”

“If you don’t take it tonight, I won’t dare come back.”

She tried once more to wave it off. His hand didn’t retreat. He did not take advantage — not even of Lee Ji-eun’s family. In the end her aunt accepted the card.

“Fine. But next time, none of this politeness.”

“We’ll discuss next time.”

Past midnight · the long way through Seoul

One reading light left on · one question asked at a red light

The nanny van · westbound

It was nearly dawn when they left ‘Good Days’. Her aunt saw them off at the door, the wind chime swaying behind the two of them. Before they got in the car she said to Baek Si-on:

“Come again when you have time.”

He bowed his farewell.

“I will.”

Lee Ji-eun waved.

“Go in and close up.”

Only after the taillights merged into the road did her aunt turn back inside — wearing a smile whose implications, she probably knew herself, exceeded the normal bounds of hospitality.

In the van, the reading light was still on. Baek Si-on picked the ‘La La Land’ materials back up and turned to the piano setlist page. Fed and warm, Lee Ji-eun was getting drowsy; she sank into the aviation seat, eyelids sagging. She turned her face toward him.

“Aren’t you tired?”

“Tired.”

“Then why keep reading?”

“If I don’t do it now, there’ll be even less time.”

She noticed the faint weariness under his eyes — a grayness that didn’t come from today alone, more like something accumulated since before ‘The Three Evils’ wrapped and never fully metabolized.

“You pack yourself this full every single time.”

“Your day wasn’t exactly idle either.”

“At least I know how to strike.”

“That’s a virtue of yours.”

Lee Ji-eun laughed.

“Rare, hearing you compliment me.”

“Occasional praise lowers an engineer’s attrition rate.”

“Did you just promote me off temporary engineering?”

“By the debt-repayment schedule, you’re roughly on a tenure track.”

“......”

The recording engineer, it turned out, had been granted tenure — with the repayment schedule serving as the board’s approval.

— the employment terms, per the creditor

The late-night streets of Seoul flowed slowly past the window, streetlamps stretching tree shadows into long bands across the empty sidewalks.

A cinema slid into view on the right, its exterior LED wall cycling the current movie ads. The frame changed — and the ‘Sado’ concept poster filled the entire screen.

Cinema LED wall · now showing

Song Kang-ho’s Yeongjo sits enthroned in the upper half in his red dragon robe; below him, Baek Si-on’s Crown Prince Sado kneels in plain white.

Lee Ji-eun’s gaze rode the poster along the window glass until the cinema slid away behind them.

“When is the ‘Sado’ premiere?”

“August third.”

Lee Ji-eun took out her phone and opened the calendar app. Her fingertip stopped on that date.

The calendar, as checked

August 3 — empty.

No conflict. She closed the calendar and put on a voice engineered to sound casual: “Send me an invitation.”

Baek Si-on looked up from the materials.

“You have time?”

“A premiere is only a few hours.”

“Alright. I’ll have director Lee Joon-ik’s side send the invitation to Jeong Han-teuk.”

The procedure was correct. The arrangement was solid. It was not what she wanted.

“That’s it?”

Baek Si-on asked:

“Do you need me to write a formal invitation card?”

“You could at least formally invite me.”

“What would count as formal?”

Lee Ji-eun declined to demonstrate.

“Figure it out yourself.”

Baek Si-on closed the materials and turned to her.

“Ji-eun-ssi.”

“Ne.”

“I hope you’ll come support Korean historical cinema.”

“......”

She stared at him. She had waited all this time, and been handed an official line in the house style of the film promotion council.

“Can’t you just say, ‘I hope you’ll come see my film’?”

“Is the meaning different?”

“Of course it is.”

Another intersection drifted past the window. The light turned red. Baek Si-on, per instruction, began again:

“I hope you’ll come see my film.”

This time it was one person saying it to one person.

That sentence was the entire reason she had started this. And having actually heard it, she refused to look too pleased. She lowered her head, opened the calendar, and ceremonially re-confirmed a blank date she had already confirmed.

“We’ll see how the schedule looks.”

Baek Si-on: “......”

The light went green. The van rolled forward. Lee Ji-eun turned her face to the window — her lashes throwing a small shadow in the glass’s reflection — and the smile at the corner of her mouth, in the end, refused to stay hidden.

❝

The acceptance, filed formally as a scheduling matter, was ratified at a red light by a smile that would not sign its own name.

— the minutes of August 3, as kept by the window glass

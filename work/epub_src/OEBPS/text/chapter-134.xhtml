The last time he topped this list it was for winning the chart. Now even the congratulation on the chart win has to push past other news to get here. The ceiling keeps moving.

Number one. The second congratulatory telegram from the Blue House.

Last time, the telegram had been for the Venice Best Actor. This time, the subject was a Billboard Hot 100 singles-chart championship.

the Blue House · congratulatory telegram · No. 2

“Through personal talent and tireless effort, Mr. Baek Si-on has become the first singer in the history of the Republic of Korea to top the American Billboard Hot 100 singles chart, writing a new chapter for our nation in the field of global popular music.”

“The President extends her most sincere congratulations to Mr. Baek Si-on and to his team.”

— the Blue House

Two telegrams, less than two months apart.

For a normal artist, receiving either one even once was enough to sit at the most prominent line of a career resume for life.

Baek Si-on, though, had gone and handed in a fresh achievement while the Blue House congratulation-printers were still warming up.

The forums were in hysterics.

Ten thousand would have sent the director to a temple to kowtow three times. Thirty thousand was a genuine hit. Fifty thousand was a phenomenon. This number may require kowtowing at every temple in Korea.

By the standards of a commercial blockbuster, that figure would have been a flop.

But by the standards of the independent scene, this was not “pretty good.”

This was an explosion.

A normal first day for a Korean independent film: if ten thousand people walked into cinemas, the director should go to a temple and kowtow three times in gratitude. Thirty thousand was a minor smash. Fifty thousand was a phenomenon. A hundred and twenty thousand… Baek Jeong-hun would probably need to kowtow at every temple in Korea.

Of course, how much of that number had come for Baek Si-on, how much for the Venice-award hook, and how much for the “Choi Jin-ri’s breakout role” label was impossible to say.

Most likely: all of it, mixed together.

But who cared.

Box office was box office.

The numbers did not care why you walked into a cinema. They only cared whether you bought a ticket.

Round and round the discussion went, and finally it always circled back to the same question:

Which side was Baek Si-on actually on?

The film world?

The music world?

The fashion world?

Before, this question had only been a joke among fans.

Now it was starting to give genuine headaches to industry insiders.

Because every one of those circles could produce evidence and say:

He’s one of ours.

The same morning The first scene after breakfast Jeonju · the Sado set · a palace hall that used to be a warehouse The man the whole country is discussing sits, bolt upright, on a throne.

And right now, the man being chewed over by hot searches, telegrams, box-office numbers, forums, and the media was sitting bolt upright on a throne in the Jeonju studio lot.

In a gonryongpo — the crimson robe of the crown prince, gold dragons coiled across the shoulders.

An ikseongwan — the black winged coronet — set squarely on his head.

His back was straight. His chin was lifted. Dozens of courtier extras knelt in ranks before him.

He was addressing the court at length — the scene in which Yeongjo summons the Crown Prince to preside over state affairs.

On paper, it was meant to teach the prince how governance was done. In practice, it was the overture to a public execution.

Sado · the court audience · scene twelve · camera two · take one

The Crown Prince presides over the court — before the King takes the floor back

Action

The court sits in ranks. The Crown Prince is bright and full of momentum, laying out reform after reform with total fluency — on the treasury, the grain transport, the army, the provincial magnates.

SADO “If we simply raise taxes on the common people, we are draining the pond to catch the fish.”

SADO “What we truly need to audit is the hidden land registered under the great yangban clans — the provincial powers who shelter from corvée and levy behind the names of their kin — and the acres that sit in the official ledgers, existing on paper, never once paying a grain of tribute.”

SADO “The yangban live beneath the state’s protection and eat of the court’s grace, yet refuse to share the state’s burden. If the nation is in trouble and only commoners are made to give money and labour while the yangban keep their privileges — then what we call court law is no more than a stone laid upon the backs of the people.”

Every sentence sounds as if it was thought out years in advance — because it was. A faint stir runs through the ranks of courtiers; a head rises, a glance is exchanged.

SADO “Therefore, your Highness proposes—”

The King’s voice, weightless, drifts down from behind the throne. The Crown Prince’s spine straightens a fraction; for an instant there is light in his eyes — he believes he is being acknowledged.

YEONGJO · OFF “Crown Prince.”

YEONGJO · OFF “Audit the yangban? Make the yangban pay? Easy for you to say. Then tell me — who do we audit first?”

YEONGJO · OFF “The house of the Chief State Councillor? The house of the Second State Councillor? The great clans whose ancestors rode with King Taejo at the founding of the dynasty and have served this court for generations? Or the houses of the loyalists who risked their entire families to stand behind me when I took the throne?”

YEONGJO · OFF “Are you raising money for the state — or raising enemies for your king?”

At that line the Crown Prince’s face finally changes. Not taxes — power. He understands now: the old accounts of years past, the things a young heir may not be allowed to tear open with a single phrase like “for the sake of the state.” His brightness buries itself under shame. Below, the courtiers lower their heads — some pretending not to have heard, some keeping their faces blank, a few letting a flicker of relish surface.

MINISTER “Since the matter of the yangban land tax cannot be lightly touched — then from where is the shortfall in military provisions to be made up? If I may ask the Crown Prince.”

The Crown Prince who should have answered at once does not answer. His mouth opens — the plans are still in his head: curb the court’s own expenditures, purge the local magistrates, investigate the padded army-cloth returns. He no longer dares say them. The camera creeps in; the cold light of the court hall puts a shallow shadow across his face.

Slowly he turns his head toward the throne. His voice is much lower than before.

SADO “What does Father King believe… should be done?”

YEONGJO “The Crown Prince presides over the court, and when he meets a problem he cannot resolve, he turns and asks his king. Then what use is there in making you my regent?”

The whole court is silent. The Crown Prince’s face drains white, degree by degree, until he bends forward and his forehead nearly touches the floor.

SADO “Your son… accepts his guilt.”

Behind the monitor, Lee Joon-ik does not call cut immediately. Not sudden stupidity. Not sudden timidity — a man ground down once in public, beginning to doubt whether he still has the right to speak at all. The prince who stood bright at the head of the court has been pressed out of existence by his father’s own hand. The take holds.

“Cut.”

The courtier extras slowly came out of their kneeling positions, covertly flexing their knees.

Song Kang-ho looked down at Baek Si-on once. Baek Si-on was still holding the prostrate pose. After two seconds, he straightened, slowly.

Song Kang-ho held out his hand. Baek Si-on took it and used the leverage to stand.

“How was it?”

Baek Si-on asked.

Song Kang-ho gave him an unsparing thumbs-up:

“What time did you finish memorising the lines last night?”

“Three in the morning.”

“That’s dedication.”

Lee Joon-ik got up from behind the monitor.

“Print one.”

He paused, then added:

“That take was very good. But let’s do one more.”

Baek Si-on turned.

“What should I adjust?”

Lee Joon-ik looked at him.

“When you lay out the reforms at the start — be a little brighter.”

Baek Si-on understood.

“So the fall hits harder?”

“Yes.”

“Understood.”

Baek Si-on straightened the hem of the court robe and walked back to kneel in place. The crew re-rigged the lights. The extras reseated themselves. The script supervisor walked back in front of the lens.

A little past eleven, a small commotion rose outside the hall.

A support truck rolled, unhurried, into the crew’s temporary parking area.

From its side hung a banner.

support truck · side banner

Please take good care of our Crown Prince — Actor Baek Si-on

Below the words, a giant print of the Crown Prince in court robes, looking far too handsome for the Joseon tax code.

Once the truck stopped, two staff members jumped out and began unloading.

Passing crew stopped for a second.

the lot · murmurs

“Wow…”

“A support truck?”

“Actor Baek’s?”

“Daebak — the photo on the side is too handsome.”

“That coffee looks expensive.”

The assistant director saw what was happening, walked quickly to Lee Joon-ik’s side, and murmured a few words.

Lee Joon-ik was still watching the monitor.

The court scene was done, one take printed and one in the can. What came next was the scene where the Crown Prince, having descended from the court, finally lets his true feelings spill out in private before his father-in-law.

The two scenes ran together. In the first, he is crushed in front of the full court by Yeongjo. In the second, he finally lets a little real emotion surface in front of an elder he can trust.

If too long an interval passed, if the just-suppressed shame and indignation were allowed to dissipate, the next scene would lose its force.

Lee Joon-ik turned to look at the support truck. Then he looked down at his watch.

Eleven twenty-one.

He turned back to Baek Si-on.

“Sado. Do you want a break?”

“No need. Let’s continue.”

“Fine.”

Lee Joon-ik nodded and waved at the assistant director.

“Anyone who isn’t needed can go get food first. Don’t crowd. Shooting crew stays. Actors, get ready.”

The assistant director turned at once and called out:

“Non-scene staff can head to the support truck! Move quietly! Don’t disturb the sound!”

At that, the edges of the set lightened considerably.

The extras couldn’t move. But the crew without scenes had begun a small-scale migration — props, costume, field crew, assistants — shuffling in small steps toward the support truck.

One of the hardest parts of filming a period piece was hunger.

Especially with winter coming on.

Standing through a whole morning in layer after layer of costume burned calories the way the Joseon fiscal system drained the treasury.

✿ ✿ ✿

Outside the hall, the support truck’s side window had slid open.

Inside, rows of coffee, sandwiches, kimbap, and assorted pastries sat in neat formation. The quantity was generous — clearly prepared on the scale of the whole crew of a hundred-plus.

By the time Song Kang-ho arrived, a short line had already formed in front of the truck.

At the window, a girl in a mask and a cap was working like a diligent little bee.

“Hello — americano or latte?”

“Ham or egg sandwich?”

“Thank you for your hard work. Enjoy.”

Her voice was soft. Her eyes curved. Even with half her face hidden, you could tell she was in an excellent mood.

Song Kang-ho looked once.

Recognised her.

Choi Jin-ri.

“Tsk. The Crown Prince has someone who dotes on him, after all.”

Several crew members nearby laughed.

Baek Eun-ah had just come over from the set. The instant she heard “the Crown Prince has someone who dotes on him,” she laughed too — then trotted over and hopped up into the truck.

“Eonni, let me take over~”

Choi Jin-ri was handing kimbap to a field hand and hadn’t registered it yet.

“Huh?”

Baek Eun-ah had already squeezed in from the side and was pushing her away from the window.

“You go rest. I’ll hand things out.”

“But—”

“Go on, go on. It’s stuffy in here. Eonni, go out and get some air.”

Choi Jin-ri was shoved, stumbling, down out of the back door of the truck.

She stood beside the vehicle, dazed. Then she saw Baek Eun-ah in the window, blinking at her.

The expression on that face spelled it out with total clarity:

Go.

Stop pretending to be staff here.

“……”

Go where?

Choi Jin-ri followed Baek Eun-ah’s gaze toward the middle of the set.

Oh.

To go see the Crown Prince.

Her face warmed.

But she still hugged a hot coffee to her chest and headed toward the centre of the set.

Last night, she had told him that next time he won some number one, she would prepare something else instead.

And this morning — Human was still at number one on Korea’s album chart.

It wasn’t Billboard.

But it was still a number one.

So here she was.

Choi Jin-ri had taken a few steps — she hadn’t even finished drafting what she would say when she saw Baek Si-on — when a fresh burst of noise rose behind her.

“Another one’s coming?”

“What’s going on?”

“Is it somebody’s birthday today?”

Her steps stopped.

She turned around.

Sure enough. Another support truck had rolled, unhurried, into the parking area.

Its banner wasn’t fully unfolded yet; staff had already guided the vehicle into the empty space beside the first one.

The door opened.

Lee Ji-eun jumped out.

She was dressed plainly today. Plaid shirt. Jeans. A cap. A mask over her face.

But the moment her eyes came out, anyone who knew her could tell.

Her staff got out behind her and unrolled the banner.

support truck · side banner · No. 2

Congratulations to Actor Baek Si-on — Billboard Hot 100, Number One

Two trucks, two banners, parked side by side in the November light of a Jeonju lot.

Baek Eun-ah poked her head out of the first truck’s window and looked.

Her expression changed.

“Oh no…”

Then a small-scale detonation swept the lot.

Still holding the hot coffee meant for Baek Si-on.

The wind came through, and the steam off the cup lid drifted once.

She looked at Lee Ji-eun.

Lee Ji-eun looked back at her.

Across two support trucks, several rows of crew, and a crowd of cast members who were absolutely watching while pretending not to watch — their eyes met.

One second.

Two seconds.

Then she raised a hand and smiled in greeting first.

“Peach.”

Both smiles were very pretty.

Both tones were very gentle.

Gentle enough that Baek Eun-ah, watching from the truck window, felt her scalp go tight on the spot.

She glanced toward the centre of the set.

Baek Si-on was still having his makeup touched up.

Completely unaware that two support trucks had just completed an extremely delicate historic convergence in the crew parking area.

Lee Ji-eun walked over.

Choi Jin-ri stayed where she was.

Lee Ji-eun looked at the coffee in Choi Jin-ri’s hand.

“Eonni sent one too?”

“Mm.”

“What a coincidence.”

Inside the window, Baek Eun-ah shut her eyes.

This was not a coincidence.

This was a total failure of intelligence.

She — the head of artist management at S.W Studio — had failed to learn in advance the support-truck deployment plans of two important female friends of her cousin.

Dereliction of duty.

Complete dereliction of duty.

Song Kang-ho stood nearby, watching with relish, an americano in hand. He said, quietly, to the assistant beside him:

“Times have changed. These days the Crown Prince gets wedged between two women before he’s even been locked in the rice chest.”

The assistant very nearly sprayed his coffee.

Over on the other side, Lee Joon-ik had finally registered the delicate commotion beyond the set.

He turned and looked.

Two support trucks.

Two banners.

One Choi Jin-ri.

One Lee Ji-eun.

Then he looked back at Baek Si-on — completely unaware, still standing at the edge of the frame letting the makeup artist dust powder onto his face.

Lee Joon-ik was silent for two seconds.

Then he looked down at the monitor.

“Prepare the next shot.”

The assistant director blinked.

“Director — no rest?”

Lee Joon-ik’s face was expressionless.

“If we wait any longer, the Crown Prince may no longer be able to act aggrieved.”

The assistant director:

“……”

Sound reasoning.

Because in real life, the Crown Prince in question looked far, far too un-aggrieved.

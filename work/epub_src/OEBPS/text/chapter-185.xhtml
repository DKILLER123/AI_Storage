Chapter One Hundred Eighty Five

Chaebols Can’t Picket, but Movies Can Fire

January 3–8 · a farewell at the security line to an opening night One goodbye kept small on purpose · an Asian tyrant running drills · locusts at the ticket gate · thirty-two shows answering with thirty-six · a $2,000 stub in a VIP row · and a toast sealed in plastic LAX departures → the rehearsal hall → the arena, Los Angeles → the backstage lounge, where a chaebol and a singer clink sports drinks A mother fixes a collar and boards; a comedian-in-waiting cracks on cue; scalpers meet the one force scarier than criticism — supply; and in a noisy back room, the quietest deal of the year gets poured over ice: a joint venture, a hit list, and a certain teacher ranked No. 1.

Los Angeles International Airport. The departures hall.

Yoon Hye-ja and Choi Jin-ri were flying back in the morning. Baek Si-on stood outside the security line, seeing them to the last point a person could be seen to.

“Mom — rest well.”

“Mm.”

Yoon Hye-ja reached up and smoothed down a crooked patch of his collar.

“You too.”

“I know.”

She let go, patted his arm, and turned toward the security lane. Two steps in, she looked back once. Choi Jin-ri was standing where she’d been left, not following. Yoon Hye-ja didn’t hurry her; she walked through security and left the two of them standing in the flow of the departures hall.

Choi Jin-ri looked up at Baek Si-on. Baek Si-on looked at her.

Yoon Hye-ja might already be inside, but this was a public space, and public spaces were full of eyes. And Choi Jin-ri knew, besides, that some things didn’t need to be poured out entirely at a farewell.

“Do the tour well.”

“Mm.”

She looked down at the tips of her shoes, then back up.

“Remember to read your messages.”

He nodded at her.

“I’ll answer.”

Her eyes bent, just slightly. It was a small promise. But for someone who used to reread her phone over and over whenever his busy seasons swallowed him, it was enough to keep her warm through an entire flight.

“Go.”

“Okay.”

She pulled her suitcase toward the security lane. A few steps in, she turned and waved anyway. He raised a hand. She faced forward again, and did not look back a second time. Because if she looked back once more, she was afraid she might actually run back.

❧ ❧ ❧

The days that followed, Baek Si-on’s life filled back up with tour preparation.

Human Live 2015: First Light Tour. That was the name that stuck — Scooter’s explanation being that Baek Si-on was currently standing on the dawn line where Asian pop entered the North American mainstream.

The brand sheet · the tour name, as sold to the room

HUMAN LIVE 2015 — FIRST LIGHT TOUR

Rationale, per the manager: the artist is standing on the dawn line — the first light of Asian pop coming over the edge of the North American mainstream. Nobody in the room had a better line, so everybody got to keep it.

Stage rehearsal started proper on January fifth, and the AMAs dance crew reported in full force. These were top-tier American dancers, men and women who had seen every kind of room — they’d backed divas, warmed up rap royalty; one of them had served as human set dressing at a Super Bowl halftime show. Their attitude toward Baek Si-on was, noticeably, different.

Rehearsal efficiency was outrageous. The choreographer would finish describing a formation and the dancers would already be in it. When Baek Si-on said the second chorus needed to be pulled in tighter, the entire next run came back with everyone’s power dialled down in perfect unison.

Adam also brought the Maroon 5 band in for a rehearsal run. He stood at the edge of the stage watching Baek Si-on and the dancers drill the ‘Legend’ chorus three times back-to-back, and finally shook his head.

“You know what, Baek? You don’t look like a singer anymore.”

Baek Si-on took a drink of water.

“What do I look like?”

“Like an Asian tyrant training an army.”

“Thank you.”

“That wasn’t a compliment.”

“And yet I accept it.”

“……”

On Wiz’s side, the two Chicago shows got locked quickly too. He even offered, on the phone, that if Universal signed off, ‘See You Again’ could have its live premiere in Chicago. Scooter heard that and was dialing Universal Pictures before the sentence finished — tone extremely polite, content extremely hard-line. The gist: you can either enjoy the biggest free global pre-marketing campaign of the year, or you can keep a song that is going to detonate anyway locked in a vault until it grows mold.

The other end of the line went quiet for a long time.

Then they agreed.

❧ ❧ ❧

Sale day was set for January sixth. Three presale waves — fan club first, public on-sale last.

Scooter had worried the cheap standard tickets might trigger a mass scalper buy-up. The facts proved his worry fully justified. The scalpers didn’t just come. They came like a locust front moving across farmland. Within half an hour, the first batch of marked-up tickets was live on resale sites — first-row VIP flipped at two thousand dollars a seat.

First Light Tour · the resale watch, sale day +30 minutes

First-row VIP: $2,000 and climbing.

Standard tiers: flipped before the fan-club wave finished.

Manager’s mood: uncertain whether to be furious or delighted. Artist’s ruling, one line: “Add shows.” Every city that can take one, takes one. Effective immediately, the tour is thirty-six dates. The locusts are kindly invited to hold their inventory.

Scooter sat in his office staring at the prices on the screen, his face cycling through weathers.

“I honestly don’t know whether to be furious or thrilled.”

Baek Eun-ah, from beside him:

“Isn’t it a good thing?”

“For the artist’s heat, it’s a great thing.”

Scooter pointed at the screen.

“For the human moral order, I want to boil these scalpers alive in an army stew pot.”

Baek Si-on glanced over.

“Add shows, then.”

Scooter turned his head.

“Which cities?”

“Every city that can take one.”

Scooter suddenly laughed.

“I love this gentle cruelty of yours.”

What are scalpers actually afraid of? Not criticism.

Official add-ons.

You hoard? The venue grows. You inflate? Supply answers. You want to bleed the fans? The market’s supply gets slammed directly into your face. Nothing on earth moralizes quite as effectively as inventory.

And so, before the tour had even begun, Human Live 2015: First Light Tour went from thirty-two shows to thirty-six. The SB Projects team, reading the updated itinerary, grew briefly nostalgic for the Baek Si-on of his early American days — the man who, back then, could still be asked to do something without the whole staff being kept up until dawn by one casual sentence.

❧ ❧ ❧

January eighth. Los Angeles. Opening night.

From late morning, the line outside the arena wrapped half a block. Korean exchange students with hand-lettered support banners. American fans in Under Armour collab hoodies. Fans who’d flown in from other states dragging suitcases. And a handful of boys very visibly dragged along by their girlfriends.

The merch line at the doors was, if anything, more extreme than the entry line. Collab hoodies. Tour-limited caps. Posters. Glow wristbands. And two pop-up stands taped together at the last minute — one BodyArmor, one Bibigo. Baek Eun-ah watched American fans buy a sports drink with one hand and study how to heat frozen dumplings with the other, and felt a feeling too complicated to file. So this is what cultural export is. First you open their ears with music. Then you take their stomachs with dumplings.

At four in the afternoon, inside: lighting was on its final pass, the sound engineer lay across the console checking every band of frequency twice, dancers stretched in the warm-up zone, Adam’s band gear had landed. The corridors were a running machine of headsets, walkie-talkies, garment bags, and instrument cases.

Around six, the sky outside had not fully darkened, and the entrance was already a field of light sticks. The crowd filed in.

VIP block. Lee Mi-kyung sat near the front, and looked down at the stub in her hand. Two thousand dollars. Scalper premium. In an ordinary fan’s hands, that would qualify as the year’s most exemplary act of self-harm; in hers, it counted as not wanting to waste time fighting a countdown clock. At her net worth, camping a ticketing page like a teenager would have been the truly absurd performance art.

Lee Mi-kyung

Vice chairwoman · CJ Group

Exile in Los Angeles · the wrong-nephew bet · “You are a very interesting person.”

She helped build an entertainment empire, backed art the wrong minister disliked, and was handed an American “rest period” for it. She sat in the VIP row to price an endorsement and left holding a beverage joint venture, a screenplay aimed at the very top, and a toast poured from a three-dollar sports drink.

What interested her was not the ticket anyway. It was Baek Si-on — or more precisely, why her nephew had staked so much on this man for the brand’s most critical global campaign. Having now seen the room itself, the answer had become extremely concrete. This was not fandom as she understood it. This was closer to a religious gathering. Every time the big screen flashed the tour film, the arena detonated with screaming. Before a single note, the air was already hot.

Seven o’clock. The lights died. The arena held one instant of pure silence.

The next second, a low drum line rolled out from somewhere deep under the stage. The intro of ‘Legend’. The center lift began to rise, and Baek Si-on stood in the light, his black stage clothes cut into clean lines by cold white beams.

‘Legend’ · opening night · the first two lines

Here we go, here we go—

It’s my turn to make history—

The first line dropped, the dancers cut in from both wings, and light, rhythm, and voice snapped into alignment in the same instant. This was what the AMAs crew was for. When the chorus arrived, twenty thousand people shouted it back:

“Won’t stop till we’re legends!”

The sound banged around under the dome and came back multiplied. The formation spread to the sides; Baek Si-on walked the beat down toward the front of the stage. And midway through the second verse, his gaze swept across the first row of the VIP block — and stopped for a beat.

The camera followed his eyes; the big screen produced, immediately, a chubby, cheerful face.

James Corden. Baek Si-on had done his show. The moment the man realized he was now on a screen twenty thousand people could see, he stood up, fully cooperative, and raised his BodyArmor higher than any light stick in the building. Somewhere, Kobe was probably feeling very proud.

Baek Si-on looked at him for two seconds, walked over, crouched down at the stage edge, and held out the microphone.

Corden blinked — then lit up. He took the mic, cleared his throat. The arena went instantly quiet. Everyone waited for the king of late night to speak. He breathed in, opened his mouth:

“Won’t stop till we are—”

A shrill, spectacular note-crack tore through the air of the building.

The audience froze for one beat — and then laughter avalanched in from every direction. Corden himself was laughing, face scarlet, completely unburdened. A man who sings duets with the biggest voices alive on late night television does not have bad vocal cords. The crack was the joke. He’d come up in variety, and self-sacrificing comedy was his mother tongue — the same school as a certain Lee Kwang-soo.

Baek Si-on’s undisguised disgust at the crack was, by then, magnified a thousandfold on the big screen. He plucked the microphone cleanly out of Corden’s hand, stood, and said:

“Refund him.”

The house detonated all over again.

❧ ❧ ❧

For the next half hour he ran through several more songs, the room pinned at a boil the entire time. During his change-and-water break, the stage passed, as arranged, to the guests. Adam brought Maroon 5 up through the lift into the spotlight — ‘Sugar’ turned the Los Angeles arena into the world’s largest wedding reception — and then came ‘Animals’, last year’s chart-dominating calling card, with Adam selling every high note at full power, earning Baek Si-on a fresh vault of North American screaming and grabbing himself a very good night’s work in the process.

The show came down in the long tail of the last song. When the lights came up, the applause ran on and on. Baek Si-on bowed at center stage.

In the backstage corridor, the whole machine was waiting — crew, dancers, band. He walked in to a wall of applause. The dance captain came over and hugged him.

“Perfect, Baek.”

The guitarist thumbed a peace sign. The sound engineer leaned out of the control room and OK-ed him from across the hall. Baek Si-on bowed to all of them.

“Thank you for the work.”

It was a short sentence. Everyone back there knew he meant it.

As the crowd and the small talk thinned, he’d just sat down with a bottle of water when Baek Eun-ah came over and said, quietly:

“Cousin — Vice Chair Lee is here.”

He looked up. Lee Mi-kyung stood in the doorway of the lounge. She was dressed down today — a dark coat, hair pinned up, nothing that would let anyone clock the vice chairwoman of CJ at a glance.

Baek Si-on stood. He had, in fact, spotted her from the stage. There had been no interaction, not one extra second of camera time in her direction. This one was, in the most literal sense, a princess in flight. If a fan-sliced clip of the two of them interacting hit the internet, and a certain someone currently occupying an office next to the Blue House misread it as a signal, the consequences would out-trouble any entertainment scandal on earth.

“Vice Chair Lee. Good evening.”

He waved her in. Baek Eun-ah, reading the room flawlessly, withdrew and pulled the door shut behind her.

Lee Mi-kyung looked him over once, and opened with a smile:

“Congratulations on the show. Your pull in America is even more absurd than our press sells it.”

“The fans are generous.”

He pulled out a chair for her. She sat, visibly uninterested in spending time on pleasantries.

“The broadcast rights to this tour — have they all been sold?”

The woman was technically in America recovering, but her attention had never left the entertainment empire she built. Thirty-six exclusive broadcasts landing back on Korean television would make the finance department wake up laughing on ad revenue alone.

“Broadcast rights are small money.”

Baek Si-on twisted the cap off a bottle of BodyArmor and passed it across.

“There’s another partnership I’d rather discuss.”

She took the bottle but didn’t drink.

“Go on.”

He laid BodyArmor out for her — the brand background, Kobe’s stake, the local-bottling license, the Asia joint-venture authority. And then the equity structure he was proposing.

The Asia joint venture · the structure on the table, backstage, Los Angeles

CJ — 60%. Production lines, distribution, retail placement.

US technical partner — 20%. Formula and quality control.

Baek Si-on — 20%. Brand face, marketing, promotion.

The pitch under the numbers: local bottling strips out tariffs and transpacific freight entirely. Run it through CJ’s existing food lines and convenience-store network and the margin is, in the founder’s own words, terrifying. Competing bidders exist — Lotte, Nongshim, Orion, even Shinsegae’s convenience empire. He brought it here first.

Lee Mi-kyung knew the brand. Small today, but riding Kobe and Baek Si-on, with a ceiling that wasn’t visible from where she sat. And a man who brings you a project like this instead of shopping it to your rivals has, unavoidably, a reason.

“I never trust kindness without an invoice. What does this cost me?”

He didn’t dodge.

“I don’t like carrying debts.”

“What debt?”

Her brow creased slightly.

“Last year, at year’s end — MAMA. Some of the award allocation wasn’t clean.”

“I called the head of tvN and asked him to go repair it. In the end, the singer in question walked out with Song of the Year and Best Female Singer.”

Lee Mi-kyung went quiet.

She had, of course, never known about the petty back-room plumbing of an awards ceremony. MAMA’s allocations every year were a stew of sponsors, agencies, platform traffic, and horse-trading — details that never climbed high enough to reach her desk. But the shape of what he meant came through perfectly. For one female singer’s trophy, he had spent the leverage of the CJ system. And now he was delivering a cross-border joint venture as the repayment.

She had surfed the business seas for decades, and she could not find one clean word for this behavior. Sentimental? Too soft. Shrewd? A shrewd man does not repay favors at this exchange rate; the cost and the benefit don’t even pretend to match.

In the end she said only one thing.

“You are a very interesting person.”

“Too kind.”

Baek Si-on nodded at the fruit-and-coconut-water bottle on the table.

“Try it.”

She looked at the bottle, and did take a sip. Juice folded into coconut water, going down smooth, sweetness calibrated to the exact line. If local lines brought the cost down, this thing could carve out a truly enormous slice of Asia.

“There’s one more thing I want to discuss.”

She set the bottle down and waited.

“You know my uncle, Baek Jeong-hoon.”

Of course she knew him. When Baek Jeong-hoon took the Lion of the Future at Venice, she had sent people to sign the freshly awarded new director into the CJ system — investing in a genius at the starting line always beats investing in a finished master. But the stubborn old thing had somehow ended up at a table with Park Chan-wook, Bong Joon-ho, and Song Kang-ho. Worse, he carried that particular left-leaning filmmaker’s allergy to chaebol olive branches, meeting every one of them with a three-tenths sneer, as though capital’s money smelled of coin and only festival wine counted as holy water. She had very nearly gone to educate him personally. Before she could teach that arrogant director his lesson, though, the Blue House had gone ahead and taught her one — she was shoved to America to “rest,” and the re-education of Baek Jeong-hoon quietly died on the vine.

“I know him.”

Her voice was mild.

“Your uncle is the hot new director of Korean cinema these days.”

Baek Si-on declined to hear the sting.

“A while ago, I talked through a screenplay with him.”

“The frame: a man who has done every kind of evil wakes up one day and finds himself ranked only third on the wanted list.”

“He decides that a life of evil which never even reached No. 1 is an insult. So he sets out to kill the two men above him.”

Lee Mi-kyung had spent her life around movies; the outline got rated in her head before he finished. Fifteen-certified, probably nineteen — a limited-release crime picture, blood flying, a protagonist with the morals of a shark. Narrow audience, a box-office ceiling you could see from the door.

“Sounds ordinary.”

“The outline is ordinary. But the No. 1 he wants to kill — that setup is a little special.”

“How so?”

“The No. 1 is a teacher.”

No reaction yet.

“One who gets into mansions.”

He looked at her.

“One who rewrites fates.”

“Who carries messages for chaebol wives and politicians.”

“Who can make a family believe that one sentence from her — a child’s future, a husband’s career, a clan’s destiny — can all be rearranged.”

Lee Mi-kyung turned her head toward him, slowly.

She knew exactly who this “teacher” was. She and her brother were both direct casualties of that teacher’s abuse of power. The real reason she was parked in Los Angeles was, on paper, the left-leaning films she’d invested in — and underneath the paper, that she hadn’t paid enough protection money into a certain foundation bearing the teacher’s name.

The lounge was quiet for a few seconds.

“Do you know who you’re talking about?”

Her voice did not rise. Its temperature dropped.

“A fictional character. In a film.”

“Does your uncle see it that way too?”

“He’ll only shoot it more real.”

Lee Mi-kyung suddenly found the whole evening funny. She had come to watch a concert. Since the final bow, she had been handed a sports-drink joint venture, then handed a crime film engineered to keep certain people up at night. This Baek Si-on was a genuinely strange creature. Talk money, and he was a merchant. Talk favors, and he was a gambler. Talk movies, and he was an arsonist.

“A chaebol can’t march up to the Blue House gate with a placard. But a chaebol can finance an excellent Korean crime film.”

Baek Si-on picked up his own BodyArmor, twisted the cap off, and raised the plastic bottle toward her.

“You’re right. Excellent crime films deserve the full support of local capital.”

“Then — to Korean cinema.”

She looked at the orange bottle in his hand, then at the identical bottle in her own. At her level, deals of this size were usually toasted in custom crystal, with a vintage older than her nephew. Now, in a noisy backstage lounge, the seal was to be set on a blood-soaked picture aimed at the heart of the country’s power, using a sports drink with a retail price under three dollars.

She didn’t dwell on the shabbiness of it. She drank.

Chapter Five

When the Scoundrel Meets an Angel

A dozen or so minutes later, Si-on had the picture more or less straight.

Se-jeong’s letter had reached LOEN. Somewhere in the pile of fan mail it had been pulled out, and it had ended up in Lee Ji-eun’s hands. After reading it, she’d been ready to wire money to the address on the spot, until her manager stopped her and said they couldn’t do it that way — they had to send someone to confirm the situation first. And so Han-teuk had come.

And then been choked.

Si-on leaned back on the sofa, looking at the IU poster on the bedroom wall. Yesterday he’d sat right here and said that chasing a star was a luxury for the slow suicide of the poor. Today the star’s people had shown up at the door.

He was quiet for two seconds.

Then he stood up.

“Come on. Take me to your office.”

Han-teuk’s mouth opened.

“I have to report to the siljang first—”

“Call him on the way. Let’s go.”

❧ ❧ ❧
Cheongdam-dong, Gangnam Loen Entertainment The corridors of the machine

Loen Entertainment’s building was in Cheongdam-dong, Gangnam. The two of them signed in at reception and Han-teuk led Si-on inward. The corridor was short, with a few album posters on the walls.

They passed a recording room with its door ajar, and a guitar intro drifted out.

Clean. Clear. Carrying a lazy late-spring-afternoon warmth.

Si-on’s steps slowed for just a moment.

Then he kept walking.

At the end of the hall was an office. Han-teuk knocked and put his head in.

“Siljang-nim — the Anyang matter, I’ve confirmed it.”

A steady, middle-aged voice answered.

“Come in.”

The office was small. A desk, two chairs, a wall plastered with a dense schedule. Bae Jong-han sat behind the desk, mid-thirties, hair combed to order, shirt buttoned to the throat. When he saw the crew-cut man in the floral shirt behind Han-teuk, his brow tightened.

Bae Jong-han

Team leader · LOEN Entertainment

Manager · the quiet negotiator behind the scenes

The one who asks the sensible questions and nods slowly. He knows exactly what is at stake the moment a star decides to help a stranger, and he keeps the ledger straight when everyone else is busy feeling things.

“And this is?”

Han-teuk’s reporting was interesting. He didn’t begin with “he choked me”, but with “the situation described in the letter is confirmed.” As for the part where he’d been pinned to a sofa, that was compressed into one line: “after discussing the matter with this Mr. Baek, the situation is clear.”

Bae Jong-han listened, then let his gaze rest on Si-on.

“So you’re with that collection agency?”

“Temporary.”

Si-on corrected the wording. At his side, Han-teuk quietly added:

“Siljang-nim, Mr. Baek says he can help resolve the matter of the Se-jeong family’s debt—”

The sentence died unfinished.

The office door swung open from the outside.

“Jong-han Oppa, is Han-teuk Oppa back? How did it go out there—”

The words stopped at the threshold.

Si-on turned.

In the doorway stood a girl. Maybe a metre and a half plus a few centimetres, a loose bun with stray strands falling by her ears, a hint of baby fat still in her cheeks. She wore a cream, oversized sweatshirt, sleeves a little long, a bit of her fingers peeking out. In her left hand she clutched a sheet of A4 paper covered in writing.

No makeup. Bare-faced.

But her eyes were very bright — bright enough that you forgot everything else about her face.

Lee Ji-eun (IU)

Singer-songwriter · Actor · LOEN Entertainment

The Nation’s Little Sister · real-name counterpart

Somebody’s favourite singer — the one the whole country calls its little sister. When a fan letter reaches her hands about a half-basement and a debt collector, she does the one thing her manager warns her against: she decides to help.

Lee Ji-eun looked at Si-on for two seconds.

Floral shirt. Crew cut. Sitting loose, legs stretched far out. Something about him tugged at a faint recognition. Like he was … someone from the same debut era? But she couldn’t place the face. That 2008 crop, boy groups and girl groups stacked one on another, backstage at music shows a market-day scramble, dozens of faces brushed past and most of them forgotten.

She couldn’t remember.

It was Si-on who spoke first.

“You’re the angel who wanted to wire Kim Se-jeong the money?”

Lee Ji-eun blinked.

She answered quickly.

“And you’re the scoundrel who won’t leave people’s houses?”

The two of them looked at each other for about two seconds. Ji-eun didn’t smile. Si-on did, a little.

He reached into his pocket, drew out a folded, much-handled photocopy of the loan contract, spread it open, and laid it on Bae Jong-han’s desk.

“Fifty million principal, plus three months of interest — sixty-two million. The contract’s here. The debtor is Kim Se-jeong’s father. He ran, and the debt falls on the mother and daughter. You pay the money to me, I go back and close the account, and it’s done.”

Bae Jong-han reached out and took it, scanning line by line. Interest rate, signature, guarantee terms, stamps — all present. He gave Lee Ji-eun a small nod.

Ji-eun thought for a moment, then looked at Bae Jong-han.

“Oppa, wouldn’t it be safer to send the money straight to the girl?”

She asked Bae Jong-han, but it was Si-on who answered.

“If you don’t want every letter you get every week to be a plea for money, then no.”

Ji-eun’s eyes shifted to him.

“Human nature doesn’t survive testing. A litre of rice is a kindness; a bushel of rice is a grudge. You open that door today, and tomorrow there’s no closing it.”

The office fell quiet for a few seconds.

“So you’re saying I shouldn’t help her?”

“I’m saying you can be an angel. Just don’t let everyone know where the gates of heaven are.”

Ji-eun didn’t answer. She pressed her lips together and looked at Bae Jong-han.

Bae Jong-han nodded slowly.

Si-on turned his head to Han-teuk, standing in the corner, and lifted his chin.

“You. Come with me.”

Han-teuk pointed at himself, bewildered.

“All the way. Bring your phone — photograph what’s to be photographed, record what’s to be recorded. How the money is handed over, how the contract is cancelled. Line by line, note it all down, and report back.”

Bae Jong-han looked at Si-on. Nodded once more. Then he turned to Han-teuk.

“Go.”

Han-teuk opened his mouth, as if to say something — something like I was already choked once today, can you send someone else — but under Bae Jong-han’s gaze the faint protest died before it left his throat.

“…Yes.”

❧ ❧ ❧
Guro District The loan-shark office Closing the account

Guro District. The loan-shark office.

Si-on pushed the door open and found the boss with his legs crossed, watching horse racing.

“Oh, you’re back?”

The boss glanced at Han-teuk trailing behind Si-on but didn’t ask. Si-on slapped the transfer receipt down on the table.

“The Se-jeong account. Cleared. Sixty-two million, not a won short.”

The boss picked up the receipt and looked at it, then looked again, and raised an eyebrow.

“Nice one, Si-on. That’s a clean job.”

He set the receipt down, fished a calculator from a drawer, and tapped out a few numbers.

“Sixty-two million. One percent commission — six hundred and twenty thousand.”

He paused, then counted a few more notes out of a tin box and pushed the pile over.

“And an extra one hundred and eighty as a bonus. New hire closes a dead-loss account on his first try — that’s worth it. Count it.”

Si-on didn’t count it. He pocketed the whole thing.

“The contract.”

“What?”

“The original.”

The boss chewed his cigarette, squinting at him.

“The account’s closed. No use keeping it.”

Si-on pointed at the dust-covered shredder in the corner of the room.

“Shred it.”

The boss didn’t move.

Si-on looked at him.

“I said, shred it.”

The boss stared at him for two seconds. Then he stubbed out the cigarette, reached into a cabinet, pulled out a file folder, and tossed the contract onto the desk. Si-on picked it up and glanced at it. He walked to the dust-covered shredder in the corner and pressed the switch.

With a low zzzz, the mountain that had been pressing down on top of the mother and daughter for so long turned into countless strips of confetti in a heartbeat.

Han-teuk stood to one side, phone up, faithfully recording the moment.

Si-on brushed the paper dust off his hands and turned to leave. As he passed the boss’s desk, he stopped one step.

“I quit.”

“Huh?”

“Resigning.”

“You—”

Si-on didn’t give him the chance to finish. He pushed the door open and went out. Han-teuk hurried to follow. The glass door swayed a couple of times behind them, and the sweet-spicy scent of the tteokbokki stall below drifted up.

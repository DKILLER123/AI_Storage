Chapter One Hundred Fifty Nine

You Don’t Even Own a Cat

December 4 · past 9:47 p.m. — nearly 11 p.m. From the coat’s jurisdiction to the elevator threshold · then one phone call before dawn, Seoul time Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium → Seongbuk-dong → (next morning) Hwaseong Haenggung Two exits from one coat, one cat that does not exist, and a cushion order that came back around the houses.

Out at the main stage, the work went on.

A crane lifted the steel trusses up one notch at a time; up on the high steel, a worker tapped at the connectors with a wrench.

the night shift · voices across the bowl

The sound traveled the empty stadium, taken up by the stands tier after tier and let go into the winter night.

“Don’t crush the cables yet!”

“Main screen, left side, up a touch more!”

“Lighting rig goes in there first thing tomorrow!”

The backup alarms of the engineering trucks went off in broken intervals, and the cold-white work lights lit the half-built stage like a city that hadn’t woken up yet.

Clang.

Clang.

Clang.

But the small territory the down coat had fenced off was, absurdly, too quiet.

Outside: steel, cold wind, construction lights, and the skeleton of a sixty-thousand-person stage.

Inside: Choi Jin-ri’s breathing, buried against Baek Si-on’s chest.

“Want to go see your seat?”

The person in the coat moved a little.

“Where is it?”

“Floor.”

Choi Jin-ri lifted her face partway out of the down coat and looked up at him. The work lights landed in her eyes, noticeably bright.

“Then I want to see it.”

That was what she said. Her body, however, did not come out of the coat.

Baek Si-on looked down at their current geometry.

“Do you plan to walk over like this?”

Choi Jin-ri blinked.

“It’s not… not possible.”

Baek Si-on: “……”

He let go.

Before she could react, the entire down coat had been arranged around her instead — sleeves hanging empty down both sides, the hem nearly at her knees. She grabbed the lapels on reflex and looked up.

He was down to one Under Armour sweatshirt. The stadium wind came pouring through the gaps in the stands and pressed the fabric once against his shoulder line.

Choi Jin-ri asked:

“Aren’t you cold?”

Baek Si-on said:

“No.”

“You said this afternoon — the breath-fog when you sing was because it was cold.”

“I’m not singing now.”

Choi Jin-ri: “……”

She stared at him for two seconds. Wonderful. This man could dismantle romance into physics at literally any hour of the day.

But the coat was warm.

Warm enough that she decided to issue a provisional pardon.

the borrowed coat, formally transferred · floor section, under construction Jamsil · 9:58 p.m. · ownership: complicated

The coat, on her The long black Under Armour down coat, rehomed. Sleeves run a full hand past her fingertips and hang empty when she lets go; the hem loses its argument with her knees. Worn the proper way, it reads as a person drowning in expensive down. Read correctly, it is a border crossing: his warmth, her customs.
The sweatshirt, on him What remains: one black Under Armour sweatshirt, stadium wind directly applied. Its insulation rating was, until tonight, marketed for jogging. Its current assignment is holding a man upright against December on the strength of brand loyalty alone.
The physics One coat, two wearers, zero additional heat sources. The transfer is not thermodynamically sensible. Nobody in this transaction has ever once optimized for sensible.

Sleeves empty, hem at her knees — warm enough to issue a provisional pardon.

The yellow caution tape and the unfastened seat samples filed no objection. The coat, newly reassigned, kept both of them in its jurisdiction for the rest of the night.

The two of them walked the temporary protection boards toward the front of the floor section. Parts of the ground were still roped off with yellow caution line; beside it stood a few rows of unfixed temporary seat samples, waiting for approval.

The main stage faced the floor straight on. From this angle you could see how the thrust stage would run deep into the crowd.

Baek Si-on, following the position Baek Eun-ah had marked on her tablet, pointed at a stretch of ground where the seat numbering hadn’t been laid down yet.

“Roughly here.”

Choi Jin-ri walked over.

She picked the edge of a protection board and sat down, the coat’s hem spreading out around her like an oversized black blanket. She looked up at the main stage.

The stage was still ugly right now.

But Choi Jin-ri could already picture what this place would look like on December 18. Baek Si-on would belong to sixty thousand people then, to the cameras, to the viewers at home. Tonight, she was the only person sitting in this seat, looking at a stage that hadn’t woken up yet.

In a certain sense, that was more extravagant than the concert itself.

Baek Si-on stood beside her.

“Is this position alright?”

Choi Jin-ri nodded.

“This position is very good.”

At that point the venue manager came hurrying over from across the floor. He saw the down coat of Baek Si-on draped around Choi Jin-ri, stumbled visibly in his stride, and moved his eyes away almost instantly.

“Mr. Baek — we’re closing up the night work zone.”

“We’re re-rigging the caution lines, and the lifting continues after.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Got it.”

The manager worked very hard to keep his gaze fixed on Baek Si-on’s face and not let it drift even one degree toward the coat.

Rule one of the live-event industry’s field manual: what you shouldn’t have seen, you didn’t see.

Baek Si-on looked back at Choi Jin-ri.

“Let’s go.”

Choi Jin-ri stood up. The hem swung, the empty sleeves waved — a kid who had stolen an adult’s clothes and gotten away with it. Baek Si-on offered no review. Choi Jin-ri laughed first.

“It’s so big.”

“You’re too small.”

“I’m one point seven meters.”

“Mm.”

“……”

They walked toward the exit. Baek Si-on asked:

“How did you get here?”

“Taxi.”

She added:

“For the way here, I mean.”

Finishing the sentence, she herself understood how bad it sounded. If the way here was a taxi, the way back could obviously also be a taxi — and yet she had gone and tacked on half a sentence more.

Baek Si-on took it completely in stride.

“Then I’ll drive you home.”

Choi Jin-ri, holding the coat’s lapels:

“Is it out of your way?”

“No.”

“Isn’t your hotel the other direction?”

“You first.”

She pressed her lips together.

“Oh.”

Then, very quietly, she added:

“Thank you, then.”

Baek Si-on didn’t puncture the happiness in her voice. He just opened the car door and let her get in first.

…

The drive back to Seongbuk-dong was quiet.

Lee Ji-eun was not in the car, but her song was not playing either; nothing was. Master Zhao drove with his usual steadiness. The late-evening traffic had thinned; the shops along the street were lit in scattered patches, and someone outside a convenience store hunched their shoulders against the cold while waiting for a hot drink.

Choi Jin-ri sat in the back seat, still wrapped in the black down coat. It was far too big; she had sunk entirely inside it, only half a face and a pair of eyes showing.

Baek Si-on sat beside her, looking down at his phone. No conversation. No manufactured small talk. The silence, for the two of them, had never once been awkward.

Master Zhao glanced at the rearview mirror. The girl drowning in the down coat; Baek Si-on in only a sweatshirt.

He said nothing at all.

He simply reached out and silently raised the cabin temperature two degrees.

The most important thing about driving people for a living is not driving skill.

It is reading the room.

By the time the car stopped below the Seongbuk-dong building, it was nearly eleven. The street was quiet; the guard office light was still on at the gate. Master Zhao brought the car to a smooth stop.

Choi Jin-ri unbuckled, pushed the door open, and got out. Then she turned around — and saw Baek Si-on getting out too.

She blanked for a second.

“You… are you walking me up?”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“As far as the elevator.”

“Oh.”

Holding the coat’s lapels, she walked slowly toward the entrance. Two steps in, she looked back.

“Really just as far as the elevator?”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

“Where would you like me to walk you?”

Choi Jin-ri snapped her head forward again.

“The elevator is perfect.”

Master Zhao watched the two of them walk into the lobby one ahead of the other. He looked down at the cabin readout.

25 degrees.

Not cold.

As for whether it was cold upstairs —

that was no longer a driver’s business.

…

The elevator stopped at Choi Jin-ri’s floor.

With a ding, the metal doors slid slowly open to both sides.

Baek Si-on glanced at the corridor outside.

“Here.”

Choi Jin-ri made a small sound of acknowledgment and stepped out of the car with glacial reluctance. Two steps toward her door — then she turned around again.

His finger was already on the door-close button.

“Sleep well.”

The doors began to close.

And in that instant, Choi Jin-ri suddenly thought of the hug on MAMA’s big screen last night. That hug had screaming, a director, an all-Asia live feed. Everything that belonged to her tonight had been too quiet — so quiet that if the elevator doors simply shut, she felt the building’s night might swallow the whole thing whole.

“Wait.”

Baek Si-on stuck out one foot and caught the sensor. The doors sprang back open.

“What is it?”

Choi Jin-ri looked down at the coat she was wearing.

“I should return your coat.”

“Give it to me tomorrow.”

He pressed the button again. The doors began their second approach.

This time, Choi Jin-ri took one big step forward and jammed the toe of her shoe into the gap. The doors, sensing resistance, retreated once more.

“No.”

She said:

“I’m returning it right now.”

The corridor’s motion-sensor light came alive at the movement. The warm yellow lamps of the hallway and the cold white glare of the elevator cabin met exactly at the threshold, cutting the air into a bright half and a dark half. The two lights fell across her upturned face — stillness with something restless hiding inside it.

the pink knit, at the border of the light · return of the coat, with intent Seongbuk-dong · 11:04 p.m. · wind: unchanged, air: not

The knit Under the coat: a light-pink fine-gauge knit, soft color, soft yarn — and a threshold’s worth of cold-white elevator light falling too straight, so that the soft lines of it read considerably less soft than intended. She didn’t choose the lighting. She did choose to unzip all the way to the bottom.
The coat, in transit The enormous down coat, sliding off her shoulders into her arms — official reason: returned as promised. Actual function: the world’s warmest conversational hand grenade, held at chest height, pin attached.
The lighting Warm corridor lamp on one side of the threshold, cold cabin glare on the other, the air sliced one bright half, one dark half — professional cinematographers get paid poorly to achieve what this building’s electrician did by accident.

Soft color, soft yarn, and lighting nobody can be held responsible for.

The motion-sensor lamp, having seen everything and understood none of its job, stayed on at full brightness — the only professional in the building.

She didn’t hand it over right away.

Baek Si-on didn’t reach for it either.

Because the act of shedding the coat had loosened the front of the outer layer beneath it — and under that was the pale pink knit. The color was soft. The yarn was soft. But the threshold’s cold-white light was too direct, and it laid the lines of the knit out with unrequested clarity.

She was, objectively, just standing there.

And yet the air in that small strip of threshold abruptly became unsuitable for further silence.

Baek Si-on’s gaze stopped for a moment.

A very short moment.

So short that if Choi Jin-ri hadn’t been watching him the entire time, she would never have caught it.

Her ears went slowly red. Impossible to say whether it was the heat — or whether she also knew that the move had been a bit much. Her words, however, were not vague at all.

“Are you sure you have to go back now?”

“Not sure.”

Choi Jin-ri’s fingers clamped hard around the coat in her arms.

The truth was she hadn’t planned her next line either, if he actually said he wasn’t going back.

The next second, Baek Si-on spoke again.

“But I need to go home and feed the cat.”

“But… you don’t even own a cat.”

“So first I need to go buy a cat.”

Baek Si-on, expressionless, reached out and pressed the door-close button.

He paused half a second.

Then pressed it again.

The second press was noticeably heavier than the first.

“And then feed it until it’s full.”

The elevator doors slid closed.

This time, Choi Jin-ri didn’t stop them.

She stood in the corridor holding the coat, watching the two panels come together point by point, until only a slit remained. In the slit, Baek Si-on looked at her. He wasn’t smiling. He wasn’t doing expression at all. He just stood there, finger still on the button — like a man saying: take one more step, and I really will hold this entire dangerous evening upright with nothing but a fictional cat.

The doors sealed. The floor numbers started jumping downward.

Choi Jin-ri looked down at the coat in her arms. A few seconds later she laughed — once, very lightly, with a little anger folded in.

“What cat…”

Hugging the coat tight, she turned toward her own door. Two steps — then she stopped.

She lowered her head and smelled the collar.

Faint laundry detergent. And underneath it, some mixture of her own air and Baek Si-on’s that had been marinating in there all night.

Choi Jin-ri’s ears went hot again.

She buried her face in the coat and muttered, very small:

“Coward.”

Lee Ji-eun was fished out of sleep by a phone.

The thing had buzzed by her pillow for almost ten seconds before she got one arm out of the blanket, patted twice, found nothing, dug under the pillow instead. Found the phone. Squinted one eye at the screen.

Mom.

Chapter Twenty-Four

Goo Hara's Kindness and Krystal's Rage

June The unit block The call to a senior from eight hundred years ago

Si-on stood at the mouth of the unit block. Light slanted between the buildings, laying a shadow clean across the ground.

He pulled out his phone and dialled the number. Long tone. Long tone. Long tone. Long tone. Four rings. Most people answer within three; four or more either means they're busy, or they're weighing whether to pick up. On the fifth, it connected.

Baek Si-on → Goo Hara · afternoon · DSP building

“Hello?”

A guarded voice. Unknown number — anyone would be.

“Hara, it's Baek Si-on. A'ST1. Jae-kyung gave me your number.”

“……”

The line went quiet. Quiet for a long while. Long enough for Si-on to recalibrate his own fame once more.

“Ah.”

Found it. That “ah” was shorter than Park's, but the searching took longer. To even be found was something.

“Sunbae-nim! I'm sorry, I didn't catch it at first.”

Goo Hara's tone switched immediately — from wary to that standard polite register a junior uses with a senior.

“Why are you calling all of a sudden?”

“I've got something I'd like to talk about. Could we meet somewhere?”

“Sunbae-nim, it's not that I don't want to… today's a bit of a special case.”

She didn't go into detail. But Si-on knew what she meant. Twenty-odd fans down there, banners and placards wrapping half the street. Anyone coming or going from the DSP building right now got their phone out and filmed first, whether they knew you or not. Goo Hara was a KARA member; standing in that doorway made her a bullseye.

“Then let's talk over the phone.”

Si-on leaned back against the stairwell wall.

“It's simple. One thing.”

“Go ahead.”

“Recommend me an independent music producer. Someone who writes their own stuff, not tied to a company.”

Two seconds of quiet.

“Sunbae-nim, are you… planning a comeback?”

“Something like that.”

“Have you signed with anyone? I'm telling you, whatever you do, don't go back to DSP—”

Si-on looked at a scrap of wallpaper lifting off the stairwell wall. Kind of her, really. Her own group was being blockaded at the front door by fans, the company was running one mess after another, and her own situation was barely better. Yet the first thing a senior from eight hundred years ago, calling out of the blue, got from her wasn't “what do you want” but a warning — don't step back into that fire pit.

“Thank you.”

Si-on waited until she'd finished before speaking.

“If I'd signed with a company, I wouldn't be troubling you with this call.”

“……”

Hara made an “oh” sound.

“Right, right…”

She muttered to herself.

“My head. If he'd signed he'd just go straight to the company's production division; he wouldn't need an independent producer.”

“Hold on, sunbae-nim, let me—”

Ten-odd seconds later.

“Got one! Sunbae-nim, write this down.”

She read out a name and a number. Si-on typed the digits into his notes with one hand.

“He's called Jung Jae-joon. He did a couple of tracks with Jay Park before, and he's made beats for a few underground rappers. Reliable — composition, arrangement, recording, all of it. He's got a small studio in Hapjeong-dong.”

“Mention my name when you call.”

Si-on looked at the numbers in his memo app and nodded — though she couldn't see it.

“Thanks, Hara. Dinner sometime when you're free.”

“No, no, no.”

Hara's voice softened.

“Good luck, sunbae-nim. The road back isn't an easy one.”

“Mm.”

“I'll let you go then.”

“Goodbye.”

The line went dead. Si-on lowered the phone and looked at the name in his memo. Jung Jae-joon.

He pocketed the phone and walked out of the building.

June 11:10 p.m. SM, seventh floor, practice room The Red Light practice

Eleven-ten at night.

SM, seventh floor, practice room.

The choreography track for “Red Light” had looped forty-eight times. The choreographer clapped twice, said “good work,” packed up, and left.

The five scattered. Song Qian went to the corner for her water bottle. Park Sun-young, sitting straight down on the floor, back to the mirror, eyes shut, breathing hard. Amber Liu crouched by the speaker switching songs, probably planning to add some extra practice on her own.

Song Qian (Victoria Song)

Singer · Dancer · Leader of f(x)

Born Song Qian in Qingdao · on stage, Victoria

The first Chinese leader of an SM girl group. f(x)'s anchor — the one the practice room quietly leans on, and the one pressing the record button when the others have stopped.

Choi Jin-ri walked to the wall and bent to pick up her towel. Her hand had just touched it.

“Jin-ri.”

The voice came from behind her. Jin-ri's hand paused, she straightened, and turned. Jung Soo-jung stood two metres off. Sweat-plastered bangs on her forehead, breath not yet settled, eyes fixed on Jin-ri.

“Everyone's killing themselves.”

Every word forced through her teeth.

“Why did you stop trying?”

The air in the room changed. Song Qian's hand on the water bottle hovered, a glance flicked over, then away. Park Sun-young's eyes opened, looked over, then stayed still and silent.

Soo-jung ignored it all. She couldn't afford to mind anyone else's stare now, because she was barely holding herself together.

This fire had been building a while. After she'd shot The Heirs last year and that role as Lee Bo-na made her, overnight, more than “f(x)'s youngest” — made her Jung Soo-jung — the name no longer needed the group's prefix, or her sister's. That feeling had been so good.

So good she couldn't let it go once she'd had a taste.

So this year she'd come back to the group with a sour streak in her, all resolve. On the Red Light stage, she was going to make everyone see f(x) wasn't just a “concept group,” wasn't some “SM experiment.” She was going to make that stage explode.

For that, she'd practised until her knees went weak, taped up a bad back and kept dancing. When you know what you want, pain is just pain — not an obstacle, not an excuse.

But the moment she turned and looked at Jin-ri, there was nothing in those eyes at all.

That cut deeper than any slacking off.

Slacking means the person's still there, just not moving. But this — this sent Soo-jung from irritation to anger, from anger to a nameless fear, and finally she squashed the fear back down into rage. Rage she could handle. Fear, she couldn't.

“Our comeback's next month.”

Soo-jung stepped forward.

“Do you know how much time we have left? The stage blocking isn't aligned yet. Do you know how many beats you were late on the chorus today?”

Jin-ri looked at her, lips parted, but no sound came.

What could she say? That she knew how hard Soo-jung was working, how hard Song Qian was working, how everyone was sprinting? But that she couldn't run anymore, and didn't know when she'd stopped being able to. Maybe it was at lunch today, watching that person carry his tray away. Or maybe earlier, so much earlier that she couldn't even remember where it began.

But say that to Soo-jung?

Soo-jung was a blade. You don't explain what dullness is to a blade.

So Jin-ri said nothing. She dropped her eyes, picked the towel up off the floor, and slowly wiped her face.

“I understand.”

Soo-jung stood where she was, watching her profile, and waited two seconds — for the rest of it. “I understand” should be followed by something. “I'll be careful tomorrow,” or “I'm sorry,” or “Thanks for the reminder.” Anything that let the conversation keep going.

Nothing came.

Jin-ri slung the towel over her shoulder, bent for the bottle on the floor, unscrewed the cap, and drank. Then she sealed it, picked up her jacket, and walked to the door.

The practice-room door swung shut. Footsteps faded down the corridor, rounded a corner, gone.

Soo-jung stood where she was.

Looked down, and saw her own hands shaking.

Not from fatigue.

She walked to the speaker and dragged the progress bar back to zero. Amber Liu picked up her towel and bottle. As she passed Soo-jung she paused, and gave her shoulder a pat.

No words.

The door opened, and closed. One person was left in the practice room.

Forty-nine.

Chapter Two Hundred Four

The Kiss Mark Is the Answer

The same week · Pangyo Techno Valley → Gangnam → the KBS annex, past seven A bank balance bad enough to ration the vending machine · a grandmaster busking on the street · twenty million dollars for a quarter of the company, in cash, no strings · a manager two centimeters from the earpiece · two boxes of raw chocolate, entered as deposit · one eye-roll, aimed with precision · and a question that was the year’s dumbest sentence, answered in Cindy’s shade of red Bluehole → an unsigned tea room → a van outside Studio B

Pangyo Techno Valley. Bluehole Studio.

Bluehole, as found

Bank balance — ugly enough to consider rationing the lobby vending machine from weekly to biweekly.

The peak, ‘TERA’’s launch year: a valuation that turned competitors green. Beautiful visuals, a strong engine, combat feel top-three among Korean MMORPGs.

The present: LOL has eaten PVP; mobile has eaten the casual players; the traditional MMORPG user base falls like autumn leaves.

Tech team: still first-class in Korea. First-class tech does not buy groceries. To make payroll: outsourcing — signed, each time, with the feeling of a supreme grandmaster busking on a street corner.

Right then — the phone rang.

S.W Studio legal ↔ Chang Byung-gyu · voice · the first approach

“Hello — is this Representative Chang Byung-gyu of Bluehole Studio?”

“Speaking.”

“This is the legal department of S.W Studio. We have a film project that needs dynamic previsualization of several interior scenes built in a game engine. Director Yeon Sang-ho recommended your company.”

Chang Byung-gyu sat up a little straighter. S.W Studio. There was possibly nobody in Korea who didn’t know the name.

“What kind of scenes?”

“Large-scale action inside a closed space. A hundred-plus characters on screen at once, with full blocking simulation, camera planning, and lighting previsualization.”

Chang Byung-gyu ran the assessment in his head. They could do this work. It was, in fact, precisely what they were best at.

“We can do it. When would be convenient to meet and go through details?”

“This afternoon, two o’clock?”

Chang Byung-gyu looked at the desk calendar. The afternoon was empty. Accurately speaking, the entire week was empty.

“Two o’clock works.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Gangnam. A private tea room with no sign on the door.

The tea room, as entered — no sign on the door

Arrival one: a woman, around thirty, dark business suit, brisk face, briefcase. Lawyer — judged instantly. Both men stand.

Arrival two: baseball cap. Hoodie. Sneakers.

Game-industry reports and server-ops dashboards did not prevent recognition: Korea’s income chart, first by a chasm; a Billboard champion; the Venice Best Actor; that face — finance channels, entertainment headlines, taxi seat-back ads, and the barrage of his wife’s variety replay last night. Recognition was the path of least resistance. Three seconds later, at the table: the “mega-star” tag peeled off and replaced with “Client.”

“Baek… Baek Si-on-ssi?”

The young man pulled out a chair and sat. The woman sat too, and passed a business card across.

“Representative Chang, good afternoon. Seo Eun-ju, legal counsel for S.W Studio.”

Chang Byung-gyu took the card, sat back down, and spent about three seconds peeling the “mega-star” tag out of his head and replacing it with “Client.” Then he opened his laptop.

“Mr. Baek — regarding your film’s previsualization needs, I’ve prepared an initial technical proposal—”

“No rush.”

A waiter poured Baek Si-on’s tea. He lifted it, took one sip, set it down.

“Representative Chang — have you done previz outsourcing like this before?”

Chang Byung-gyu nodded.

“We have.”

“On which engine?”

“Unreal. During ‘TERA’’s development we did heavy secondary development on it — indoor lighting, collision, character-density ceilings all optimized. A hundred characters on one screen is not a problem.”

“Then here are the requirements.”

Baek Si-on recalled the script’s description and spoke slowly.

The requirements, as tabled over tea

Scene one — the nightclub. Seven functional zones: dance floor, bar, booth section, VIP rooms, corridors, toilets, kitchen. One hundred-plus extras distributed across all zones; the male lead enters from the ground-floor entrance and fights his way up to the second-floor VIP, gunfire the entire way.

To be simulated: every extra’s escape route and reaction timing; the lead’s complete path and reload nodes; at least three camera tracks; sync with the club’s strobe rhythm; and the physics of recoil and shell ejection.

Chang Byung-gyu’s pen couldn’t keep pace with his brain. Assessment, delivered with a full chest: “Not a small job. But we can do it.” Interior spaces, high character density, real-time collision — the ‘TERA’ dungeons ran on exactly this, pointed in a different direction. Total outsourcing volume: three to four times everything Bluehole had taken in the past year, combined.

“It isn’t just this one scene — the rest needs my director to work out the details with your team directly.”

“On scheduling—” Chang Byung-gyu began.

Baek Si-on raised a hand.

“Schedule and pricing — my team will interface with yours directly.”

He looked at Chang Byung-gyu.

“But the outsourcing isn’t the only thing I came for today. Representative Chang — I’d like to talk about investment.”

Chang Byung-gyu looked at him without processing. Investment?

“Mr. Baek… you’re interested in the games industry?”

“I’m interested in your future projects.”

Before the man could think it through, Baek Si-on moved to the numbers.

“Twenty million dollars. For twenty-five percent preferred equity, and one observer seat on the board. No control. No direction of development. No hand in daily operations.”

Chang Byung-gyu converted rapidly in his head. Twenty million dollars, roughly 220 billion won. Backed out at twenty-five percent, the pre-money valuation came to about sixty million — 66 billion won. That number, honestly, was not high. If anything, given Bluehole’s current state, it was higher than every venture fund of the past six months had been willing to go. But twenty-five percent was not a number a founding team nodded at easily. One quarter. It meant that from the day Baek Si-on entered, of every four won Bluehole earned, one went to a singer. The trouble was, Bluehole was currently failing to earn even the one. And of all those VCs frowning over financial statements in cafés, murmuring “let’s observe a little longer,” not one had been willing to put this number on the table. Baek Si-on was. In cash. Arriving in one payment. No installments, no ratchet clauses, no performance commitments.

“Mr. Baek.”

“Mm.”

Chang Byung-gyu looked up, voice unsteady:

“…You’re certain?”

Baek Si-on sipped his tea.

If you think twenty-five percent is too little, I can ask for thirty-five.

— the negotiation, entire; the counteroffer that ended negotiating

“…No. Twenty-five percent is very reasonable.”

The letter-of-intent process went faster than Chang Byung-gyu had imagined. Seo Eun-ju walked the supplemental legal clauses one by one; he nodded along more earnestly than he had ever nodded in any board meeting. Walking out of the tea room clutching those two pages, his steps were barely touching the ground. Arriving, the number in the company account had kept him up at night. Leaving, he suspected tonight would be sleepless too — for a completely different reason.

Inside the tea room, Seo Eun-ju looked at Baek Si-on.

The risk memo, as delivered over the tea

Twenty million dollars. Twenty-five percent preferred. No ratchet clauses. No performance commitments.

By any normal investment logic: extreme risk. If this company produces no new core product within two years, the twenty million becomes a pile of code and a few servers gathering dust.

She was quiet for two seconds.

“You’re still investing?”

“Mm.”

“On what grounds?”

Baek Si-on turned to look at her.

“Counselor Seo, do you play games?”

“No.”

“Then there’s no way to explain it to you.”

Seo Eun-ju pushed her glasses up and elected not to press. Her not pressing did not mean nobody would press. They had barely stepped out of the tea room when Baek Eun-ah’s call came through.

Baek Eun-ah → Baek Si-on · voice · the meltdown · held two centimeters from his ear

“Cousin!!!”

Baek Si-on moved the phone two centimeters away from his ear.

“What did you buy? A nuclear bomb?!”

He waited out the shouting.

“Shares in a game company.”

“A game? You spent over twenty billion won… on shares of a company that makes games?”

“Mm.”

“Is that game made of gold?”

“No.”

“Then what makes it worth twenty million dollars?”

“You’ll find out later.”

Baek Eun-ah was visibly unsatisfied with this answer.

“How late is later? Tomorrow? Next week? Or when my hair goes white?”

“Stop asking.”

“I’m your manager AND your bookkeeper! How can I not ask!”

“Then ask, and don’t grief about it after.”

“I can’t do that!”

Listening to her come apart on the other end, the corner of his mouth moved.

“Right — I have somewhere to be.”

“Where?! Somewhere else that costs money?!”

“Mm.”

He hung up.

Seo Eun-ju was smiling.

“Eun-ah reacted harder than Representative Chang did.”

“She just hurts for the money. In two years she’ll be fine.”

“Where to next?”

“Seongsu-dong.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Seven-thirty in the evening. Baek Si-on’s car stopped in the underground parking of the KBS annex. Jeong Han-teuk was already waiting, leaning against a pillar — and the moment Baek Si-on stepped out of the car, Han-teuk scanned both his hands. Left hand: phone. Right hand: empty.

Han-teuk’s expression became, in that instant, the expression of an ER nurse watching a patient pull out his own IV and head for the exit.

“You came like THIS?”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

“What’s wrong?”

“Empty-handed.”

Han-teuk pointed at the two hands, voice heavy with grief:

“You came to a set visit empty-handed.”

“I came to check shooting progress.”

“You came to die.”

Han-teuk allowed no explanation, took a fistful of his sleeve, and hauled him toward the parking exit.

“Han-teuk—”

“Shopping first.”

Hauled out of the garage, across the road, into a convenience store — where Han-teuk swept the snack aisle with a professional’s economy, extracted the two most expensive boxes of raw chocolate on the shelf, and pushed them into Baek Si-on’s hands. Then went to the counter and paid himself.

❧ ❧ ❧

The studio lights were blazing. Baek Si-on and Han-teuk came in through the side door into a major scene already running: Cindy’s music-show stage — Cindy’s comeback performance on Music Bank, inside the ‘Producers’ storyline.

The music was already up. Crisp drums, a lazy bass. It took Baek Si-on two seconds to recognize it — the version he and Lee Ji-eun had reworked together in a Milan hotel room. ‘Twenty-Three’. Cindy edition.

His gaze landed on the stage. The person standing there now was no longer quite the Lee Ji-eun he knew: a black lace crop top, the fabric catching the light in glimpses of sheer; high-waisted shorts drawing the waistline; a pair of very thin heels. He remembered Milan — she’d sat on the sofa in a house dress, its hem not entirely safe, announcing “this is character analysis,” and then kissed him. A different person from this Cindy. And not a different person at all: Cindy’s base color was Lee Ji-eun’s own — merely the part she kept hidden, brought out.

The music entered the chorus.

‘Twenty-Three’ · Cindy comeback stage · Music Bank · the chorus, live

“I’ll wear what I’ve never worn.”

“I’ll show an expression I’ve never shown.”

“Look at me.”

“I’m twenty three.”

At that moment, Lee Ji-eun’s gaze swept past the rear cameras — and between every staff member, the director, the camera operators, she found Baek Si-on with total precision. The music hit the beat of silence just after the chorus. The lights flickered once. Lee Ji-eun rolled her eyes in Baek Si-on’s direction, and then sneered.

Behind the monitor, the director sat bolt upright.

“Cut!”

Everyone stopped. The director watched the replay twice, eyes getting brighter.

“Ji-eun-ya! That sneer just now — brilliant!”

He stood and slapped his thigh.

“I always felt Cindy should carry the fight with PD Tak up onto the stage with her, and the previous takes all missed by one breath. This take — perfect! That whole ‘I could not care less how you look at me’ force, all of it, out!”

The assistant director nodded alongside.

“There’s something living in that look, for sure.”

The freeze-frame, as the monitor held it

Cindy’s sneer, suspended — lip-corner tilted; a thin film of disdain across the eyes.

The director’s three-word review: beautiful, dangerous, unreasonable. Applause, withheld by exactly one man — the manager in the corner, face quietly turned to the wall. He knew precisely for whom that sneer was not performed. Not for his actress. For the man behind the cameras. One hundred percent. Certified genuine.

Break called, Lee Ji-eun’s face switched on like a toggle. She turned to the staff and the smile was back in place.

“Everyone, thank you for your hard work.”

“Director-nim, thank you for your hard work.”

She even handed a bottle of water to a backing dancer, and traded two lines with the lighting engineer about how comfortable that strobe rhythm had felt. Then she turned, saw Baek Si-on — and the smile vanished. At a speed that suggested his face had been detected and every soft-light filter had been switched off automatically.

Baek Si-on walked over.

“Time to talk?”

“Ten minutes.”

She turned and walked. He followed. Han-teuk watched the two of them head toward the parking structure, and concluded that the money he’d spent on chocolate was, in all probability, never coming back.

❧ ❧ ❧

The van door shut. Lee Ji-eun sat with her arms crossed, a heel tapping the floor of the van, looking at him. Waiting for an explanation. Or an apology. Or some species of nonsense — “about last night, I’m sorry,” “I got back to Korea in such a rush I couldn’t reach you.”

Instead, Baek Si-on took out his phone, plugged in the earphones, and held one bud out to her.

“Listen.”

She stared at the earbud. She had assumed he came to coax her. Instead he wanted her to serve as the listening booth?

“You’re serious?”

“Mm.”

“You come back to Korea without a word, and the first thing you do at my set is make me listen to a song?”

“Listen first.”

She rolled her eyes at him — and then took the earbud and put it in.

♪

OOPS_demo_CP_MT_v1

Charlie Puth × Meghan Trainor · demo v1 · doo-wop finger-snaps · unmastered

He pressed play. The intro streamed in: retro finger-snaps, a light clap rhythm, the sweetness of sixties doo-wop. Then the voices — Charlie’s and Meghan’s woven together, like two people who had done something wrong, shifting the blame back and forth, and laughing harder with every pass. Lee Ji-eun listened, and the tightness in her expression loosened without permission. The song had a genuine talent for improving moods: melody like sugar, rhythm like a spring, every “Oops” delivered with a wink. Her toe had started keeping time before she noticed it — and the instant she noticed it, she stopped.

The song ended. She pulled the earbud out. Her face was softer than before, and her mouth was never going to admit it.

“And?”

She looked at Baek Si-on.

“Nice melody. You want me to sing it with you?”

He shook his head.

“No. I want you to help me fix the lyrics.”

Her expression ran a very fast sequence: a blank first. Then disbelief. Then the laugh of pure exasperation.

“Yah, Baek Si-on!”

“Mm.”

“You made me listen to that song so I’d work as your unpaid lyricist?”

“Not unpaid.”

Baek Si-on set the two boxes of raw chocolate on her lap.

“A deposit.”

She looked down at the chocolate. Then up at him.

“Two boxes of raw chocolate for a full set of lyrics?”

“Three boxes, if you want.”

“Your respect for lyricists and your knowledge of chocolate come to the same total.”

He let that pass, and said, seriously:

“The lyrics as they stand are too explicit.”

“So?”

“So they get rewritten. Keep the melody and that whole ‘oops — we may have crossed a line’ ambiguity, and wash the words clean.”

“Oops — we may have crossed a line.”

Lee Ji-eun repeated it. And she suddenly understood: he wasn’t talking about the song. He was talking about a hotel room in Milan. But she was not going to let herself go soft on schedule.

“You always do this.”

“Do what?”

“Use work as a shield.”

He looked at her and didn’t deny it. She bit her lip and moved her eyes off his face, to the window.

“Who is this song for?”

“Undecided.”

“Then why ask me to fix the words?”

“Because you know exactly how to say the things that shouldn’t be said so they sound like lyrics.”

She turned back to him. Last night she had taken “Milan,” “night,” “hotel room,” “only the two of us” — all the unsayables — wrapped them neatly in the coat of work, and delivered them to Jin-ri. And Baek Si-on had just deployed the identical logic: you’re good at this, so fix my lyrics. Her expression completed a two-second weather transition. Cloudy, turning to localized rainstorm warning.

❝

You know how to say the things that shouldn’t be said so they sound like lyrics.

— the compliment, and its load-bearing accusation

“What is that supposed to mean?”

Her voice dropped. Baek Si-on looked at her.

“What you said on the phone last night — it was deliberate.”

She didn’t flinch. She didn’t even blink.

“Correct. It was deliberate.”

“So you knew it would hurt her, and you said it anyway.”

Lee Ji-eun gave a cold little laugh.

“So what?”

She leaned forward slightly, arms still crossed.

“Did you come to settle scores on her behalf today?”

“To inform me I’m badly raised?”

“To tell me I wrecked your sweet little evening?”

Baek Si-on didn’t follow her down the argument. He said, very quietly:

“I’m not accusing you of anything.”

“I only wanted to ask — have you thought it through?”

The van went abruptly quiet — quiet enough that Lee Ji-eun could hear her own heartbeat.

Thought what through?

Whether to keep walking toward a man guaranteed to hurt her?

Whether to compete with Jin-ri — and with all the women whose names she didn’t know yet?

Whether one day she’d become a villain who felt guilty even while writing songs?

Whether losing, in the end, would look too ugly to survive?

Last night Yoo In-na had asked her the same question. She had said, at the time: I’m not crazy. Now Baek Si-on asked it to her face, and she found that language was too pale for it. Say “I’ve thought it through” — it sounds like a rehearsed line. Say “I’m not retreating” — it sounds like a declaration of war. Anything she said would be a performance of resolve, not its proof.

Lee Ji-eun looked at Baek Si-on. He sat less than thirty centimeters away in that Under Armour hoodie, a small stretch of skin showing above his collar. Her gaze stopped there. Then her hand shot out, grabbed him by the collar — and before he could react, she pulled, bringing his whole body to her, and kissed him.

Her lips pressed down hard, teeth catching his lower lip, a ferocity that answered to no one. His brow knotted at the bite. She didn’t release. Mid-kiss she deliberately tilted her head, lips sliding from the corner of his mouth to the side of his neck — and above the collarbone, exactly where the collar failed to cover, she bit down again. The whole thing lasted a dozen seconds or so. Baek Si-on didn’t push her away. He didn’t respond either. He simply sat there, like a man who had not yet finished deciding whether to fight back or to call the police.

Lee Ji-eun let go. Withdrew. Surveyed the results. On the side of Baek Si-on’s neck, at the collar’s edge, beside a faint kiss-mark, sat one extremely legible red lip-print. Her mouth-corner climbed, slowly. Mischievous. And with zero intention of answering for any of the consequences. She extended her thumb and slowly wiped the lipstick smudged at the corner of her own mouth — slowly, the way one admires a piece just completed.

“That is my answer.”

Baek Si-on sat where he was, looking at her; his lips moved and produced nothing. Lee Ji-eun took in the state of him, and her satisfaction climbed a straight line. She turned, pulled the van door open — and, one foot on the ground, looked back at the two boxes of raw chocolate on the seat.

“Deposit accepted.”

Baek Si-on: “……”

“Enjoy your evening~”

With that, she blew a kiss at the man still fossilized in his seat, and left on her heels.

❝

“Have you thought it through” — probably the single dumbest sentence he had uttered all year.

— the question, and its answer, in Cindy’s shade of red

Baek Si-on sat alone in the van. After about five seconds, he raised a hand and touched the side of his neck. The fingertip found a trace of moisture. He brought his hand up to his eyes. On the fingertip: a smear of true red.

Cindy’s shade.

Chapter One Hundred Sixty

A Supplier Audit Is Not Meeting the Parents

December 5 · noon — until night Lunchbox interruptus · then a tasting that was never only a tasting Hwaseong Haenggung → Chungjeong-ro · a back-lot restaurant called Good Days A negotiation in three currencies: won, kindness, and how much curry heat one aunt can be asked to deploy.

Noon. In the temporary rest room at Hwaseong Haenggung, Baek Si-on had just been handed his lunch box.

The rice was still sealed when the phone went off.

The caller display said: Scooter Braun.

It was lunchtime in Korea; in Los Angeles, night had only just fallen. For Scooter, clearly, this did not count as off hours.

Baek Si-on looked at the screen for two seconds and connected.

“Hello.”

Scooter’s opening line was a picture.

Scooter Braun → Baek Si-on · December 5 · 12:10 p.m. KST · from a lunch box to a chart siege

Baek — you’re surrounded on all sides.

Surrounded by whom?

A pause with a hand rubbing a face inside it.

…I mean Bruno Mars just dropped. ‘Uptown Funk’ landed at number three on the Billboard Hot 100. You’re about to get rear-ended.

And?

‘Legend’ is still sitting at number two. But ‘Love Yourself’ is down to twenty-two this week.

Twenty-two?

A laugh came down the line.

By your current standard, yes — twenty-two counts as “down to.”

Baek Si-on split his chopsticks.

So your point is: the moment my concert ends, I fly to the States.

Exactly. Streaming placements alone won’t carry it. You have to show your face — radio, TV, the whole circuit. American listeners need to remember you as more than a song the algorithm pushed at them.

Baek Si-on thought it over.

Then I fly on the twentieth, Seoul time.

Nineteenth.

Twentieth.

One second of silence.

The nineteenth, Baek. One extra day is two more radio hits.

Twentieth.

He was performing for sixty thousand people on the night of the eighteenth. On the nineteenth, a man needed, at minimum, a few hours of being an ordinary person. The twentieth was already the structural limit.

“……”

Scooter sighed.

Fine. The twentieth.

Oh, and — the ‘Love Yourself’ MV teaser numbers are excellent. That kiss is doing a shocking amount of the talking for you.

Mm.

Your Korean national little sister — IU, right?

I’m going to eat now.

HAHAHA, fine, fine, hanging up.

…

Evening. Chungjeong-ro.

A restaurant named Good Days hid behind a stretch of trees and a small courtyard. Outside, patio umbrellas stood under strings of warm yellow lights. Inside: a wooden counter, white brick walls, round pendant lamps, green tablecloths, tableware and glasses already set out. It looked less like a place that hadn’t opened yet and more like a place that had been waiting, fully dressed, for one particular guest.

In the corner stood a small Christmas tree.

On the black wall by the stairs, one line in white paint:

the black wall · by the stairs

A Delicious Day

Beside it stood Lee Ji-eun’s own soju, in one-to-one-scale cardboard-cutout form, smiling her most retail smile. The real one sat behind the front desk, smiling considerably less.

The shop was playing ‘Way Back Home’ — the original, turned down low, just loud enough to cover the occasional clatter of pots from the kitchen without ever getting in the way.

Lee Ji-eun sat at the front desk with her phone in her hands, screen brightening and dimming. She wasn’t reading anything on it. Her eyes kept sliding to the door — and snapping back down immediately, in the manner of someone who was, officially, only concerned about whether the wind chime had rung.

In the kitchen, the aunt was adjusting the soup — pot lid lifted a crack, steam escaping, the smell of the base slowly expanding through the rooms. her mother stood at the counter plating dishes.

Lee Ji-eun heard the kitchen sounds, failed to stop herself, and looked at the door again.

A van had just pulled up outside. Headlights swept across the glass.

Lee Ji-eun shot upright off the high stool. The moment she was up she decided the reaction had been too fast, and started to sit back down — but before her weight arrived she decided a seated hostess was a rude hostess, clenched her teeth, and stood straight back up.

“Mom. Auntie.”

She called toward the kitchen.

“The guest is here.”

Her mother’s voice came back:

“Coming.”

The two elders shed their aprons and came out. her mother wiped her hands clean. The aunt fixed her hair, unprompted, with visible intent.

The door opened. Outside cold pushed in first.

Then Baek Si-on walked in with Baek Eun-ah behind him, the string lights landing on his shoulder.

“Ma’am. Good evening.”

Baek Si-on bowed slightly.

Her mother hurried over, a smile she had no intention of restraining anywhere on her face.

“Ai-goo, the actor himself — taller than television.”

The aunt came out after her and looked Baek Si-on up and down with the frank thoroughness of a customs inspection.

Baek Eun-ah stepped forward at exactly the right moment and presented the gift bag.

“Ma’am, a small something from our side. First visit — we couldn’t come empty-handed.”

Her mother declined it twice.

“Aiyo, you came, that’s enough — why bring things!”

And then, smiling, took it.

Carrying the bag, she looked at Baek Si-on once more, and turned to her daughter.

“Ji-eun-ah, look at this child. Tall, well-mannered, and he even brings gifts.”

Lee Ji-eun, off to the side, privately rolled her lips. The gift-bringer was Baek Eun-ah. This man needed half a business day to produce the word congratulations — gifts? Not in this lifetime.

Her mother caught her daughter’s face and cut her eyes at her.

“What are you standing there for? Water.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

Wonderful. MAMA’s Song of the Year, back home, remained in charge of pouring water.

She picked up the pitcher and filled their glasses. Baek Si-on accepted his.

“Thank you.”

Lee Ji-eun looked at him.

“You’re welcome.”

The pitcher, however, came down onto the table with slightly more conviction than the water strictly required.

When the greetings had settled, her mother gestured at the fully laid table.

“Try the food first, then.”

Baek Si-on said:

“The cushions first, actually.”

Her mother blinked.

Baek Si-on explained:

“That one’s the urgent item.”

Her mother laughed at once.

“Fair enough.”

She turned to her daughter.

“Ji-eun, bring the sample.”

Lee Ji-eun acknowledged, turned to the high stool at the counter — and brought over the very thickened cushion she herself had been sitting on, handing it to Baek Si-on.

He took it. First he pressed it — thickness, rebound. Then he lowered his head and held the thing up to his nose, and smelled it.

Lee Ji-eun’s entire soul left her body.

“You’re SMELLING it?!”

Baek Si-on looked up.

“Sixty thousand of them get unwrapped the same day. If there’s a smell, people complain.”

“Who complains about a cushion?”

“The person sitting on it.”

Lee Ji-eun opened her mouth. She had been about to inform him that this particular cushion had been under her own body five minutes ago — and then decided that saying so would somehow make everything stranger.

She shut the mouth.

Baek Si-on set the cushion on a chair and sat down on it properly. Thickness: correct. Rebound: sufficient. Two hours on a December stand in an open stadium — it would hold off the cold.

He stood, and indicated Baek Eun-ah.

“You too. Sit.”

Baek Eun-ah proved to be an even more serious inspector than he was: once seated, she shuffled forward and back on purpose, testing the friction of the seat face and whether the corners slid.

Sample: passed.

“Ma’am — at this thickness, can sixty thousand units make the deadline?” Baek Eun-ah asked.

“With no special requests: first batch in five days, all sixty thousand inside ten.”

Her mother paused.

“On price — two thousand won each.”

Baek Eun-ah blinked at the number.

“This morning your quote was three thousand.”

“This morning I didn’t know what it was for.”

Her mother smiled.

“I only just heard — the actor’s concert doesn’t sell a single ticket. It’s a thank-you.”

“It’s so children don’t freeze on cold benches in December. That’s not money adults should be taking.”

The shop went quiet for a moment. In the kitchen, the soup pot went on bubbling.

Baek Si-on looked at her mother.

“Ma’am.”

“Mm?”

“Charge three thousand.”

Her mother froze. Lee Ji-eun’s head came up too.

Baek Si-on continued:

“Kindness can be priced in as a discount. It can’t be priced in as a loss.”

The shop went quiet a second time.

Baek Eun-ah looked down and opened a fresh contract note on the tablet.

“This concert is mine to run. The fans don’t freeze, and the supplier doesn’t eat the loss either.”

Her mother looked at Baek Si-on for several seconds.

She had been about to refuse again. But she had done business for decades, and she could hear it clearly: this young man was not performing courtesy.

He genuinely did not want the discount.

Finally, her mother smiled.

“Alright. Three thousand, as you say.”

Baek Eun-ah wrote it down immediately.

the cushion memo · 60,000 units · Namdaemun wholesale, joint venture with the aunt × S.W Studio

01 — Price: three thousand won each, as insisted — kindness priced as a discount, never as a loss. ₩3,000

02 — Zoned packaging, by stand section, delivery sequenced to the installation plan. ZONED

03 — Expedited schedule: first batch in five days, full sixty thousand inside ten. DAY 10

04 — On-site delivery team priced separately, per the quote. TBD

Samples had been sat on, shuffled on, and smelled. The smelled cushion had spent the evening under MAMA’s Song of the Year, a fact its auditor would never be informed of.

Standing to the side, Lee Ji-eun suddenly thought of Kim Se-jeong’s letter, half a year ago.

Back then her own first instinct had been to help, too — send money, transfer it, anything so the girl in that semi-basement would cry a little less.

And then Baek Si-on had turned up. Flowered shirt, buzz cut, vocabulary like gravel. At the time she had thought the man was cold past the point of decency.

But now, watching him stand in front of her mother and refuse, in complete seriousness, to accept an underpriced quote, she suddenly understood.

He wasn’t stopping anyone from being kind.

He only knew that kindness without a border wears down the kindest person in the room first.

“Ji-eun-ah.”

Her mother’s voice pulled her back.

“Mm?”

“What are you standing there daydreaming for? Bring the dishes out.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

She had just concluded a deep, nuanced internal seminar on the boundaries of human decency, and her mother had dismissed the session with one word: dishes. From philosophy back to waitstaff, no appeals accepted.

The tasting began in earnest.

The table filled fast. The restaurant’s concept was a deliberate hybrid — Italian mainline, Korean soul folded through it. The starter: a blackcurrant salad. The snack: garlic-butter roasted bread. Two mains arrived together, one basil pesto pasta and one thick-cut ribeye. As for the so-called Korean house special — it was, in fact, a plate of boneless fried chicken in a secret sweet-hot glaze. A small square of tiramisu closed the circuit.

Because this was a tasting, every plate came in smaller than menu size.

Baek Si-on ate quietly.

And completely.

Every course that landed, he finished — properly finished, edge to edge.

Her mother and the aunt stood nearby, visibly brightening by the plate. For a cook, the highest form of praise had never been adjectives.

It was a clean plate.

And Baek Si-on went past the clean plate.

He put down his fork, looked up, and asked, with total seriousness:

“Could I get another round?”

Her mother startled.

“Of what?”

“All of it.”

A clean plate was already the highest praise. A clean plate followed by another round had left the territory of praise entirely and entered some new genre of performance art.

Her mother and the aunt froze for two seconds, and then both burst out laughing.

The aunt clapped once.

“I TOLD you the food was good!”

Her mother headed back toward the kitchen, beaming, though she could not leave her profession at the pass:

“For the real opening, portions get recalculated for male customers, apparently.”

Lee Ji-eun watched both elders disappear in visible high spirits, leaned toward Baek Si-on, and lowered her voice.

“It’s actually good?”

“Mm.”

Lee Ji-eun didn’t doubt the food.

She was probing the man. She wanted to know whether someone who had to be waterboarded into saying congratulations would, for her mother’s sake, produce a polite lie.

He had said it was good. He had ordered seconds.

And now she couldn’t tell whether that was face-giving — or true.

Beside them, Baek Eun-ah sucked thoughtfully on a milkshake, working very hard to keep her face neutral.

Her cousin was a man who could put away three bowls of rice alongside a side dish of plain kimchi.

The moisture content of that “good,” therefore, was genuinely impossible to assay.

…

The second round came out faster than the first.

The aunt, visibly inspired by all of it, had upped every portion by half. her mother said don’t force yourself, eat what you can with her mouth and pushed the sweet-hot chicken closer to Baek Si-on with her hand.

Lee Ji-eun watched it all with perfect clarity.

Her mother was no longer running a tasting.

Her mother was running a feeding operation.

Lee Ji-eun had planned to keep monitoring whether Baek Si-on was white-knuckling it. Five minutes in, she concluded something worse: he wasn’t performing for her family at all.

He was simply doing this the way he did everything — conscientiously, thoroughly, without being asked.

And that was precisely what made it unbearable.

…

By the time they finished, the hour had grown late.

Her mother and the aunt insisted on packing food for the road; the negotiations over that lasted longer than the pricing negotiation, and ended with Baek Eun-ah holding two insulated bags — the elders’ affection, formally accepted.

Lee Ji-eun, as the same-generation representative, was dispatched by her mother to see the guests off — all the way to the van.

“My mom’s happy today.”

“Mm.”

“You were very courteous to her.”

“She runs her business with a straight spine. That deserves courtesy.”

Lee Ji-eun had expected some version of because she’s your mother.

Baek Si-on hadn’t said it.

He had praised her mother the person — not “IU’s mother.”

That distinction, specifically, was the thing that made the last of her prickles go soft. She was just deciding to say something about it —

“If you ever get tired of the singer thing, you could come back and inherit the fried chicken.”

“……”

The soft thing got shoved right back upright.

She glared at him.

“…YOU go sell fried chicken.”

Baek Si-on declined to extend the debate; he bent down and got into the van.

Baek Eun-ah rolled down the passenger window and waved.

“Ji-eun eonni — see you at rehearsal!”

The van pulled away into the night, taillights gone at the corner for good.

Lee Ji-eun was still standing at the restaurant door. The cold wind moved through her hair and she appeared not to notice it, staring down the empty street.

“Ji-eun-ah.”

She jolted — both shoulders jumping.

“They’re gone. What are you looking at?”

Her mother stood on the step, watching her daughter with a smile that wasn’t quite committing to anything.

“I was checking the door was locked.”

“The door has been locked for a while.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

“I was just looking.”

“Mm.”

Her mother didn’t take her apart. She simply looked, once, in the same direction her daughter had been looking. The courtyard wind came through, and the string of warm yellow bulbs at the door swayed a little.

Inside the shop, ‘Way Back Home’ had somehow looped all the way back to its intro.

Lee Ji-eun stood in the doorway and listened for a few seconds, and suddenly felt like laughing.

The words to that song had been written for somebody else’s road home.

Tonight, apparently, it had walked its own writer to her family’s front door instead.

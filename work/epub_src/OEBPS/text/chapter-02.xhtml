Chapter Two

The Script, First Meeting

Next day · afternoon The Baek apartment A knock at the door, six years late

The doorbell rang the next afternoon. Hye-ja went to answer it.

On the threshold stood a man. Late thirties, thin, with overgrown hair and a pencil tucked behind one ear. His jeans were worn white at the knees; his canvas shoes had a lace hanging loose on the left foot. Under one arm, a brown paper envelope.

“Jeong-hun.”

“Sister-in-law.”

“Come in.”

Hye-ja stepped aside. Baek Jeong-hun changed his shoes and walked in, and as he passed the black-and-white frame in the hallway his eyes caught on it, held a beat, then slid away.

Baek Jeong-hun

Uncle & indie film director

37 · Green Bottle Fly

Too thin, too tired, a pencil behind his ear and a screenplay six years in the making. With a nephew he barely knows, he finally climbs the stairs of his brother's house.

Si-on came out of his room and greeted him.

“Samchon.”

Jeong-hun looked up at him. The last time they’d met, the boy was getting his enlistment papers. The time before that, the funeral. In between, almost nothing. Not from lack of wanting — from fear. After his brother died, the sister-in-law had raised a child alone, and he’d thought he should help, but he’d never known how to begin. So he’d let it slip, year by year, until they were strangers.

Walking through this door today wasn’t about bringing a script. It was six years of swallowed courage, finally stepped over the threshold.

“Sit.”

He pulled a sheaf of paper out of the envelope and set it on the coffee table. Four characters were printed on the cover: Green Bottle Fly. Beneath it, in smaller type: Written & Directed by Baek Jeong-hun.

Hye-ja brought out two cups of tea, sat across from him, and picked up the script, flipping open the first page.

Green Bottle Fly · Scene 1 · opening

EXT. STREET — DAY. A man yanks a woman by the hair and slams her head against a wall. HANG-JIN rushes in and beats the attacker senseless. Then he turns around — and punches the woman too, the one who is crying and not fighting back.

One hundred and twelve scenes. Over three hundred instances of profanity. A third of the runtime given over to physical violence. A protagonist who runs loan collections, speaks in ssi-bal, and pins his own father to the floor.

Her brow creased. She turned the page.

Green Bottle Fly · Page 5 · the memory

INT. HOUSE — NIGHT. He is seven. FATHER raises a bottle. SISTER lunges to shield him and collapses in a pool of blood. MOTHER runs out. Brakes. Silence.

The origin of every blow that follows. Shown to the reader before the violence can land.

She turned another page. Then — THWACK. She shut the script and tossed it back onto the table.

“Si-on is not doing this.”

Jeong-hun said nothing. He knew what his sister-in-law had just read. The whole film was one hundred and twelve scenes, with over three hundred instances of profanity and a third of its runtime given over to physical violence. The protagonist was a gangster who ran loan collections: ssi-bal every other line, no hesitation in hitting women, and at home he pinned his own father to the floor. Any mother would have exploded.

“Mother.”

Si-on spoke up.

“Can I take a look?”

Hye-ja studied him for two seconds. She wanted to say what’s there to look at, but she swallowed it, and used the back of her hand to push the script across the table.

Si-on took it and opened it. One page. Two. Three.

Jeong-hun watched his nephew’s face, trying to read something off it.

There was nothing to read. Si-on’s face carried none of Hye-ja’s furrowing frown, and no excitement, and no disgust either. He was just reading.

Seven or eight minutes. He closed the script and pressed his fingers to the cover for a moment, then looked up.

“Samchon, I think we can change the first scene.”

Jeong-hun blinked. He’d expected something polite — that’s good or worth a shot. The first word out of his nephew’s mouth was change.

“Go on.”

Si-on flipped back to page one and pointed at the scene description.

“Right now the opening sees the lead stumble onto a domestic beating, beat the attacker senseless, then turn around and beat the woman who got hit.”

Jeong-hun nodded. He’d rewritten this one scene a dozen times, all to establish the character in a single stroke.

“I get what you’re going for. You want the audience to know: this man is not a vigilante. He is violence. When he sees someone hit, he doesn’t intervene — he drowns it out with a bigger hit.”

Jeong-hun nodded again, something complicated in his face. Having an idol put words to the design he’d laboured over for half a year was not a comfortable feeling.

“But the scene has a problem. It floats. The audience doesn’t know why he is this way; they only see a brute hitting people. You have to let them know where the violence came from first — then everything after has roots.”

“So what are you saying?”

“Make it a dream.”

Jeong-hun blinked again.

“Open on the lead lying in bed, everything black. The dream plays: as a child he hides behind the crack in a door and watches his father beat his mother. His sister rushes out to shield her and is caught in the blow. He carries his sister out, runs to the road — his mother chasing after him, and she’s hit by a car.”

Si-on paused.

“Then — he wakes. Sweat, gasping. Three seconds to register this is reality. He gets up and pushes open the next door: his father is asleep right there.”

“In the dream, he is hit. Awake, he hits.”

Si-on looked into his uncle’s eyes.

“Cause and effect in one frame.”

Jeong-hun was quiet. His mind was running the images. Fast, dense, like footage spooling in reverse on a cutting bench. Dream. The crack in the door. A fist. Blood. A scream. Cut to black. Waking. Breath. Getting up. Pushing the door. The father.

One line. From top to bottom without a single break.

The living room went silent for a dozen seconds. Jeong-hun took the script back, turned to page one, and read through the passage his nephew had just described. His mouth was still, but his brow worked, and Si-on could see it: his uncle was thinking, but not yet convinced.

Talk was cheap. A director believed in pictures, not arguments. You tell him this is better, shoot it this way, he nods, but he hasn’t seen it. And if he hasn’t seen it, it doesn’t count.

“Let me show you, Samchon.”

Si-on stood up and walked to the front of the sofa.

Their eyes followed him, both of them. He lay down. Closed his eyes.

The room went so still you could hear the second hand of the wall clock.

Five seconds. Ten.

On-screen · Green Bottle Fly · Scene 1 (proposed)

The Dream — performed live in the Baek living room

Beat. The body on the sofa is perfectly still.

“HUH—!” The man jolts upright. Eyes wide, pupils unfocused, breathing torn like he’s just been pulled out of deep water.

Three seconds. The focus returns. He sweeps the room — wall, window, coffee table — and only when he has confirmed all of it does the breath begin to settle.

Then his head drops, and the corner of his mouth twitches.

Not a smile. The after-image of hatred spasming once across his face.

LINE “Ssi-bal.”

A word, low. It is ambiguous whether it is aimed at himself or at someone else.

The next second he turns to look at Jeong-hun — and the older man’s back presses into the sofa cushion. Not a deliberate retreat. Instinct. Whatever is in those eyes is not being acted. It is something real.

Si-on rises abruptly and strides toward the director on bare feet, halting half a metre away and looking down at the man on the sofa. His right hand closes into a fist. The tendons along his forearm stand out.

Two seconds pass. The fist relaxes.

He steps back, and everything on his face drains away like water, and he is once again the crew-cut, freshly-discharged young man scratching the back of his head.

LINE “I’ll skip the part where he hits someone. Didn’t want to accidentally clock you for real, Samchon.”

A beat of silence. The tea has gone cold. Jeong-hun’s hand is empty; the cup he no longer remembers holding, with a little of its contents spilled onto his trouser leg, and he doesn’t notice.

Hye-ja is on her chair, her own cup perfectly level.

Nobody in the living room spoke.

Jeong-hun opened his mouth twice. The first time nothing came out. The second time what came out wasn’t what he’d intended. He’d meant to ask where did you learn that, but what he actually said was:

“How did you… do that?”

Six years ago this boy had lemon-dyed hair, winking at the camera on stage. Now he sat here, taking a script apart to the bone and performing a scene that made the back of a grown man’s neck prickle. The change was too big.

Too big to be normal.

Si-on felt the danger in it, at once. A nobody ex-idol, doing this two days after discharge. That was not normal. He had to pull it back, sand it down.

“Saw a lot of films in the army, with nothing else to do.” He scratched his crew cut. “Fiddled my way through it.”

Jeong-hun looked at him for a long three seconds. He believed maybe half of it.

The other half he decided to hold onto, for now. Because wherever this change had come from, the performance was real. And he could see the sister-in-law’s reaction too. Hye-ja’s mind was never on the acting; what she cared about was what her son had gone through out there, to be able to look at a person like that.

But she didn’t ask. And Jeong-hun didn’t ask again.

The silence stretched.

Then Jeong-hun looked at Si-on.

“Do you want the lead?”

Si-on turned to his mother.

“Mother. Can I take this?”

Hye-ja didn’t answer at once. She lifted her cup, drank, set it down. When she spoke her eyes were calm already.

“Your life is yours to make.”

Si-on smiled. He turned back to Jeong-hun and nodded.

Jeong-hun let out a breath and swung straight to Hye-ja, using the most earnest tone he had in him.

“Sister-in-law, don’t worry — I’ll keep the language in check, I promise I—”

“How much?”

Hye-ja cut him off.

Jeong-hun blinked, registering that she was asking for money, then ground his teeth and held up one finger.

“One hundred million.”

He deflated a little at his own number.

“I’ll transfer two hundred.”

His finger was still raised, frozen mid-air.

“Use better equipment.”

Hye-ja stood and started for the inner room.

“Don’t make it look like it was shot by beggars.”

She took two steps, stopped, and added, over her shoulder:

“And don’t let my son eat a boxed lunch with no meat in it on your set.”

Chapter Fourteen

The Han River Gives No Answer

April Late night The Han River The crying scene, and the light

Late night, by the Han River. The lighting gaffer had set up a huge condenser lamp behind the breakwater, throwing a cold white backlight to fake a bleak moonlight. The cinematographer rigged the camera low.

“Jin-ri, are you okay with the crying scene?”

Baek Jeong-hun held a walkie-talkie and made a final check. Jin-ri looked at him and nodded. He glanced at Si-on a few metres off, head down, staring at the river and working himself into the mood, and left him alone.

Another two minutes and the set was ready. Baek Jeong-hun took the headphones off and hung them around his neck.

“All departments ready.”

The script supervisor raised the slate.

April Night The Han River steps Yeon-hui, barefoot, at the water's edge

The water was black. The neon on the far bank fractured into wobbling patches of light on the surface.

Yeon-hui, barefoot, crouched on the steps at the river’s edge, arms wrapped around her knees, face buried, thin shoulders trembling faintly in the river wind. After a few seconds, footsteps came up behind her. Soft, but in the empty dark of a night river they carried clearly. She didn’t look back.

The footsteps stopped two or three metres behind her. Then the rasp of a lighter’s flint, a small flame flickering once and going out. The smell of cheap tobacco drifted over on the breeze.

Yeon-hui lifted her head and turned. Sang-hun stood behind her, cigarette in his mouth, hands in his pockets, shoulders slumped, back slightly bowed. He wasn’t looking at her. His eyes were fixed on the black water, like he was staring at something far away, something he could never reach.

The two of them said nothing. The river wind carried the faint, weedy smell of the water. After a long while, Sang-hun took the cigarette from his mouth and blew out a puff of white smoke.

“Want a drink?”

Yeon-hui set her chin back on her knees.

“I’m underage. Can’t drink.”

“Like you haven’t.”

Yeon-hui didn’t answer. Because he was right. Over the time she’d spent with Sang-hun she’d learned a lot of things she never used to know. She’d learned to curse, to drink, to fight back — even if fighting back got her chased by her father with a knife, at least she dared. These were Sang-hun’s lessons. Not taught outright — she’d just watched him, and learned.

Grow near vermilion, and you turn red. Grow near ink, and you turn black.

Sang-hun took a drag, flicked the cigarette to the ground and ground it out, then turned and walked back. A moment later he returned with a white plastic bag and sat down beside Yeon-hui, pulling out a can of ice-cold beer. He popped the tab one-handed and handed it over.

The two of them drank in silence. No sound but the river slapping against the levee, and the occasional soft clink of the cans.

Until the third can ran dry. Sang-hun crushed it in his hand, tossed it at his feet — then tilted sideways, and laid his head down in Yeon-hui’s lap.

“Ya!”

Yeon-hui flinched back on instinct, reaching to push him off.

“Just let me lie here a while.”

Sang-hun’s voice was muffled, thick with a stuffy nose. Yeon-hui’s hand froze in mid-air. Within two seconds she heard it — a sound so tightly suppressed, like choking. She looked down.

It was the man who cursed and collected debts with his fists, lying here with the back of his hand pressed over his eyes, crying in a low voice. Two seconds later, Yeon-hui’s own tears fell without warning. One drop, two, landing on his shoulder. She didn’t push him away. Instead she bent over and wrapped both arms tight around Sang-hun’s head, burying her face in the hollow of his neck.

He smelled of smoke, of liquor, of a whole summer’s worth of sweat that had never fully washed off. She didn’t find it dirty.

That was it — two people the world had thrown away, adrift at the dead of a night no one would care about, by the river, holding each other and weeping.

The wind came through, scattering the sound of their crying a little. The lights on the far bank still wobbled on the water. Breaking into a blur.

“…”

“Cut! Excellent! Very good!”

Baek Jeong-hun, into the walkie-talkie, his voice cracking with excitement. That lamp went out and the river fell back into darkness. The crew began to pack up; the sounds of boxes moving and voices broke the suffocating quiet. But the two on the steps hadn’t moved.

Si-on still lay in Jin-ri’s lap. Jin-ri still bent over, arms around his head. Only when a set-hand called out that the night-lunch had arrived did Jin-ri surface, like someone dragged back up from under the water, and take a sudden lungful of air. She lifted her tear-streaked face, let go, and shuffled back a little, embarrassed.

“I’m sorry…”

“It’s fine.”

Si-on sat up and wiped a rough hand across his face, scrubbing off the tears and snot, real and false. The wind came through, blowing the last warmth of the characters off both of them.

A few quiet seconds.

Si-on turned to look at her.

“You cried well.”

Jin-ri turned to him too, eyes red-rimmed, nose red-rimmed.

“…So did you.”

The mood was turning strange when Baek Jeong-hun came over with two plastic bags and handed them one convenience-store meal each.

“Good work. Get something in you.”

Jin-ri held the warm box and bowed.

“Thank you, Director.”

She straightened — and the box was gone. Si-on had taken it.

“That took a lot out of me. One isn’t enough.”

He stacked the two boxes together.

“Call it treating me. We’re square now.”

Jin-ri stood frozen. Square? When had they ever had a debt between them? By the time her head caught up, Si-on had carried both boxes to a wind-shielded corner behind the breakwater and was crouching, tearing the lids open.

She considered a moment, then walked over and stood beside him, using her body to block the wind off the river.

“Why are you still here, hovering?”

Si-on bit into a piece of fried pork cutlet and gave her a half-mumbled look.

“I can’t sleep anyway.”

It was a lie. Jin-ri’s own eyes betrayed it.

“Oh.”

He didn’t push. Head down, he dug back into the food. The convenience-store meals weren’t anything special — the rice a little hard, the food cold — but that did nothing to slow him down.

She stood there, watching him eat. She’d been going to ask why he loved food so much, but the question felt wrong at the last second. Too personal. It might poke at something she had no business touching. So she changed tack at the last moment.

“Why do you drop out of a scene so fast?”

She didn’t understand it. He’d cried his heart out one moment, and the next he was squatting here, cheerfully demolishing pork cutlets. Si-on, mouth full of rice, answered vaguely.

“First — in real life I don’t actually have any unhappiness I can’t get past.”

He gestured at the box in his hand.

“Second — I saw a meal.”

Jin-ri was silent. What kind of answer was that? But on reflection, it kind of made sense. A person who cheers up at the sight of food isn’t the type to get stuck in a feeling.

He ate fast. In under ten minutes, both boxed meals were clean. He stood, tossed the empties in the bin, dusted off his hands.

“You leaving or not?”

Jin-ri snapped back into it.

“Huh? Yeah… leaving.”

They walked toward the parking lot. Her van sat under a streetlight, white body speckled with moths.

“See you tomorrow.”

She pulled the door open.

“See you tomorrow.”

Just as the door was closing, Si-on called out.

“Hey.”

Jin-ri paused, pulled the door back open, and leaned out to look at him. Si-on had his hands in his pockets, standing under the streetlight, looking at those still-swollen eyes.

“Let me tell you a secret.”

She leaned forward slightly, waiting.

“Being alive is pretty good.”

Jin-ri froze. She stood there a few seconds, waiting for the next part that never came.

“…That’s it?”

Si-on nodded.

“That’s it.”

She looked at him. The streetlight fell across his face, and his expression was serious.

“Weirdo.”

She muttered it, almost to herself, and pulled the door shut. Si-on stood beneath the lamp, watching the van start, reverse, swing out of the lot and vanish into the night. Then he turned and headed the other way. After a few steps, he thought of something, and patted his stomach.

He could probably manage one more meal.

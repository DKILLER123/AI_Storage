Chapter Forty

The Elevator, the Stove, and the Past Nobody Mentioned

July Evening An apartment building, the f(x) floor Two guests, one kettle, and a bottle of soju

The elevator doors had barely started to close again when the buttons lit.

Si-on put a hand out to hold them open. The doors slid back, and a woman stepped in — small, sharp, wrapped in a hoodie that had been tied at the waist, carrying a paper cup of convenience-store coffee and a look that said she'd been expecting someone else and was now deciding what to do about it.

Eun-ah went still.

“Goo Hara sunbaenim.”

Goo Hara looked at her, then at Si-on, then at the boxes of meat. Something human moved behind her eyes — not recognition, exactly, more the quick arithmetic of a person who lives in a world where a stranger at the right floor is never a coincidence.

“You're the one who's been sending him music,” she said. It wasn't a question.

“He's my cousin, Hara-unnie.”

“Mm. And he's the one with the debt-collector face.”

The doors stayed open a beat longer than they needed to. Then Goo Hara pressed the button for the floor above and leaned back against the rail, out of the way.

“She's been alone three days,” she said, to the carpet, to the air. “Company said no visitors. But you've got meat and a stove, so tell me you're not here for a social call.”

“We're here to feed her.”

Goo Hara gave one short, exhausted smile — the kind that came from a woman who had spent her own year learning to read rooms.

“Then you should know,” she said, stepping out onto her own floor, “she's not cooked for herself in three days. And she hasn't put on a bra since Monday.”

The doors closed on her.

July Evening Inside, the apartment The clean-up, and the table that got set

The woman who opened the door was not the woman Si-on had met in the little studio in Yeonnam-dong.

It was still Choi Jin-ri underneath. That much was unarguable. But the light that had been in her when she'd walked in front of his uncle's camera — the sharp, awake, almost combative thing that had made Jeong-hun stop the take to stare — had gone somewhere behind a wall. She stood in the doorway in a loose shirt and shorts, hair unwashed and pulled back, and she looked at Si-on for a long moment as if she were trying to remember whether she was supposed to be happy to see him.

“Baek Si-on.”

“We brought meat.”

“You brought meat.” She looked at the boxes, and then at Eun-ah, and something in her face cracked — not a smile, not tears, just the sound of something loosening. “You're both insane. There's no oven here. There's a burn mark on the air fryer.”

“We brought a stove.”

Jin-ri stared at the portable butane stove in its box, and then she did something that would take Si-on a long time to forget: she turned away, walked back into the apartment, and sat down on the couch with her knees drawn up, one hand over her mouth, shoulders shaking. Not crying. Laughing — or something closer to it, the kind of sound a person makes when the world has been a closed fist for so long that an open palm arrives and they don't know which is stranger.

Eun-ah moved first. She set the boxes down, pushed the curtains back, opened the window to the evening, and then — with a gentleness that surprised even Si-on — cleared the pile of empty convenience-store cups from the table and carried the plate of half-eaten toast to the sink.

Jin-ri watched her, chin on her knees.

“You don't have to do that.”

“Someone has to,” Eun-ah said, turning the tap on. “And you're not going to be the one who feeds yourself tonight.”

Si-on went into the tiny kitchen and began opening the meat. He worked methodically, the way he did everything: sorting the slabs onto the one clean plate, checking the little stove for leaks, setting the flame to a low, even glow. He didn't talk. He'd learned, over a summer of half-basements and borrowed studios, that the most useful thing a man could bring into a room like this was the quiet competence of someone who knew what to do with his hands.

Jin-ri watched him for a long time. Then, from the couch, in a voice that was trying hard to sound bored and not quite making it:

“You don't have to pretend to be useful.”

“I'm not pretending.”

“Everyone's useful when they're finally asked to be.”

“No. Everyone's overrun when they forget how to cook for themselves.”

He said it without looking up, and the silence that followed was one of those silences that has a shape. Jin-ri let her chin drop off her knees.

“Baek Si-on.”

“Mm.”

“Is that why you came? Because you thought I'd forgotten how?”

He set down the tongs. He looked at her properly, and the room, for a second, was very quiet.

“I came because you were the only person in that studio who ever asked me what I actually wanted out of the film, instead of what I could do for it.” He picked the tongs back up. “That's not nothing. So I brought dinner.”

Jin-ri didn't answer. She turned her face away, toward the open window, and the evening came in, warm and loud with the far-off hum of a city that didn't know she existed.

★ flashback · the night of the World Cup · a year earlier

Si-on had been standing in a rented room, and a girl he barely knew had knocked, crying.

“Here. Take it. Good luck.”

He'd handed over a lottery slip, and she'd taken it like it was a stone and he was a stranger giving her a stone.

“Hyoyeon unnie isn't actually a bad person.”

The words had come out before she could stop them — the apology for someone who'd never known she needed one.

“She said something to me once,” Jin-ri said suddenly. “When everything was falling apart. She said Hyoyeon unnie wasn't actually a bad person.”

Si-on set the table edge to edge, two plates, two cups.

“Was she right?”

“I don't know. I think she was trying to be.”

“That's not nothing either.”

Jin-ri looked at him, and the evening light found the line of her jaw, the stubborn set of her mouth.

“You always have to make everything into a lesson,” she said. But she stood up. She walked over to the couch, picked up the bottle of soju Goo Hara had left on the armrest, and set it on the table between the plates.

“Okay,” she said. “Cook.”

July Later The apartment, at the small table Three people, a stove, and the past left outside

They ate. The stove did the only thing it knew how to do, and the meat went from pink to browned to the point where Eun-ah was telling Jin-ri she could stop and Jin-ri was telling her to shut up because it was the first time her apartment had smelled like food in a month.

Nobody mentioned the charts. Nobody mentioned the statement. Nobody mentioned the word exhaustion, or the comment wall, or the four other faces of the group that had just finished a comeback stage without her.

They talked about the film. About the scene on the slope, the one they'd shot in the early heat, about how Jeong-hun had made them do it again and again until the light was right. They talked about how the crane had almost tipped once. They talked about eating doenjang-jjigae at two in the morning and about the fact that Baek Eun-ah could still stand on one leg longer than anyone she'd ever met.

And Choi Jin-ri, for one evening, was just a girl at a table across from two people who had brought her meat and a stove and no questions at all.

At the door, with the boxes empty and the plates stacked, Jin-ri stood with her hand on the frame.

“Baek Si-on.”

“Mm.”

“You keep these visits to like, once a month, okay? I can't have you two showing up every time I go quiet.”

“Once a month,” Si-on said, and meant it.

Eun-ah hugged her — a real, hard hug that Jin-ri received standing stiff for a moment before her arms came up, and the two of them held on in the doorway a second longer than was strictly comfortable.

“We'll see you,” Eun-ah said.

Jin-ri nodded. And then, as they turned to go, her voice came after them, quiet and a little raw:

“Hey. The song. I heard it.”

Si-on stopped.

“You'd have done the same for me,” she said. It wasn't a question.

And the door closed on the warm, lit room, and the evening, and the two of them stood in the corridor in the kind of silence that means no one wants to be the first to speak.

Eun-ah finally did.

“She's going to be okay, isn't she.”

Si-on looked at the closed door, and at the light spilling out under it, the way it had been when they'd first arrived.

“Yeah,” he said. “She just needed someone to bring her a stove.”

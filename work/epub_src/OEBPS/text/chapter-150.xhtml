Chapter One Hundred Fifty

The Red-Hair Diplomat, Recast as Strategic Buffer

November 25 · 9:30 p.m. Night shoot · artificial rain, four trucks’ worth Jeonju · Sado backlot · the palace courtyard set The wind was obedient all day. After dark it stopped being polite and started coming in under the collars.

The work lights on the Jeonju backlot came back on at nine-thirty at night.

Lee Joon-ik stood behind the monitors, staring at the track laid across the courtyard, then at the water trucks parked along the wall.

Tonight they were shooting the Crown Prince drawing a blade and walking on his father’s palace.

In the entire emotional architecture of the role, this was the one true counterattack — the moment a man pushed to the far edge by his father, by ritual, by the palace, and by himself, finally turned the point of a sword toward King Yeongjo.

What Lee Joon-ik wanted was not heat.

He wanted desolation.

Which was why the production had requisitioned four water trucks. When the artificial rain came on, it pressed the whole courtyard flat under a curtain of wet air; the flagstones went black and glassy, and a layer of white mist drifted along the base of the palace walls like breath on cold glass.

The assistant director rubbed his hands together and couldn’t help muttering:

“Director, that much water — the actor’s going to suffer.”

Lee Joon-ik kept his eyes on the monitor.

“Which is why we get it in one.”

Assistant director: “……”

The sentence sounded almost tender. Translated out of the original director, it meant: you will suffer, but the suffering will be load-bearing.

Meanwhile Park Ji-hun crouched beside Baek Si-on with a pack of heat patches and the gravity of a field surgeon. He applied them to the key positions one by one, pressing each down with two hands.

the warmth frontline · applied under the sleeping robe, in order of importance

01 — Lower back. Non-negotiable. PATCHED

02 — Abdomen. PATCHED

03 — Below the shoulder blades. PATCHED

04 — Around the knees. PATCHED

05 — Operational directive, issued while smoothing the final edge: do not come off. ORDERED

Fallback plan, in full: “Then His Highness the Crown Prince will resist the cold through willpower alone.”

Baek Si-on looked down at him.

“And if they come off?”

Park Ji-hun looked up.

“Then His Highness the Crown Prince will resist the cold through willpower alone.”

Baek Si-on: “……”

Human civilization had reached the twenty-first century, and the Crown Prince of Joseon had finally been issued modern heating equipment. Historians would call it progress.

At the center of the set, every light came up to standby, and the four water trucks began to hum in low register. Lee Joon-ik rose from behind the monitor and swept the whole lot with one look.

“Ready.”

The assistant director raised the walkie-talkie.

“Actors to marks.”

“Water trucks ready.”

“Guards, first and second row, confirm positions.”

The slate went up.

“Sado. Night rain, palace incursion. Take one.”

Whap.

“Action.”

…

Baek Si-on came in out of the dark beyond the set, wearing a moon-white sleeping robe and soft-soled cloth shoes, water running straight down his face from overhead. The robe’s shoulders darkened from moon-white to slate as it drank.

the incursion wardrobe · one robe, one blade night rain, take seven

The robe Moon-white hanbok sleeping robe over loose trousers, both destined to drink: the shoulders go from moon-white to slate within the first minute of artificial rain, and the hem finishes the take hanging heavy as a wet sail. Four heat patches are hidden underneath — lower back, abdomen, below the shoulder blades, around the knees — a modern thermal power grid invisible to every camera and known to exactly two people on set.
The feet Soft-soled cloth shoes, dark with water, silent on wet stone — the footwear of a man who has stopped announcing himself. The sword in his right hand points down and slightly forward, rain running off the blade in one thin bright line: not a pose, a sentence whose period hasn’t landed yet.

Moon-white going slate; the blade saying what the robe no longer can.

The costume department had built the robe to be ruined. It had not been built to look this awake. Somewhere under the wet silk, four heat patches held their line — the dynasty’s first central heating.

He kicked the gate open. The red-lacquered doors boomed on their hinges. A guard at the door pressed a sword into reach, and Baek Si-on closed a hand around the hilt and walked into the courtyard.

In the courtyard, twenty-some actors in black guard uniforms knelt in two neat rows in the rain. Rain hammered their armor with the sound of a sack of beans poured out on sheet iron.

He stopped in front of them. No speech. No order. Not one line of dialogue. He simply swept them once with his eyes and walked on — and the rows came up behind him in sequence, and the sound of twenty-odd blades leaving twenty-odd scabbards ran down the line in one low metallic shiver.

The camera pulled slowly from his face out to a full medium shot: the prince in front, robe hem heavy with water, sword-point almost grazing the flagstones, a river running off the blade; behind him, ranked rows of black-clad guards with drawn steel, footsteps pressed down under the rain. Even. Heavy.

Lee Joon-ik, behind the monitor, nodded once, satisfied. The frame was better than the one in his head.

Then: noise at the side gate. The Crown Princess, played by Moon Geun-young, burst out — three running steps through the rain — and dropped to her knees in front of him, both arms locking around his leg, face tipped up.

“Your Highness!”

She was crying, shouting it.

“Your Highness, you must not go!”

Rain rolled off her forehead; the makeup on her face ran thinner and thinner; the whole picture was wrecked and desperate in exactly the way the scene description asked for.

Moon Geun-young had been the nation’s actual, certified little sister — the first to hold the title with the whole country’s signature on it, and the first to be welded into it. She was twenty-seven years old now, and people still said the little girl from that autumn drama, and she had decided a long time ago that the way out of the weld was through work like this film. She cared about Sado — cared enough that in this scene she pushed the Princess’s terror and despair out to the very front of the performance.

Which was exactly the problem. She had pushed it all the way to the ceiling in the first three seconds, and left the rest of the scene nowhere to go.

“Cut!”

Lee Joon-ik called it and waved the slate down.

“Again.”

The assistant director was already on the walkie.

“Reset positions! Water pause! Actors back to marks!”

Moon Geun-young climbed up off the stones, bowed toward the monitor, and walked slowly back to her mark behind the side gate. She knew precisely what had gone wrong and had no idea how to fix it — the worst of the four possible states.

The second take, she added a beat — arms out to bar the way first, then the kneel, the leg-grab. Lee Joon-ik’s verdict floated over the courtyard: this was not an idol drama. Again.

The third take, the emotion was reined in, but the leg-grab landed half a beat late.

The fifth take, she came out of the gate, slipped, and cracked a knee on the flagstones — a thick, muffled knock. She refused to stop, crawled up and locked onto his leg anyway. That take had the emotion right. It had the body right. It had one wrong thing: the grab was too heavy, and the impact hauled Baek Si-on half a step backward. The Crown Prince of this scene could not retreat. One backward step and the whole scene lost its edge.

Take seven.

The slate went up again.

“Sado. Night rain, palace incursion. Take seven.”

Whap.

“Begin.”

The rain pressed everything down again. Baek Si-on came out of the dark. Kick the gate. Take the blade. Walk. The guards knelt. Rose. Steel left scabbards in one shiver. Every beat identical to the six before it — except the man was colder now. Noticeably, measurably colder, as if takes one through six had been spent draining the last of the person out of the prince.

Moon Geun-young burst from the side gate on the rehearsed rhythm, timed to stop two meters in front of him — kneel, grab, speak.

She never got the chance.

The instant she closed into range, Baek Si-on’s next forward step turned, without one frame of preparation, into a straight kick to the chest that put her flat on the soaked flagstones.

The entire set froze.

Moon Geun-young, sprawled in the water, was still professional enough to be reaching for her line. Then she tilted her face up — and what she saw was not her screen husband. It was a blade, point-on, aimed at her, rain running down the steel and off the tip onto the bridge of her nose.

She was, genuinely, frightened. Frightened so completely that the line jammed in her throat and would not come out.

And the jam was right.

What could the Crown Princess say at this point? Plead? Beg? Weep? All of it had expired. The man standing over her was no longer a husband who could be stopped by a wife — he was a madman who had carried a sword into his father’s palace and meant to use it.

Baek Si-on took his eyes off her. The blade-tip sank. He turned and walked on without looking back, and the guards came up behind him in step, their footfalls pressing through the rain.

The camera did not follow him. It stayed on her: kneeling in the flood of water, hair pasted to her cheek, watching the backs of the procession go. The footsteps got smaller. The rain kept falling. She did not cry out. She simply sat there in the water like a woman who had finally understood that what she had married was not a pitiable husband but a catastrophe no one alive could still stop.

“Cut.”

Lee Joon-ik’s voice drifted over from the monitor bank.

The water trucks shut down. With the rain gone, the set went abruptly, unnaturally quiet. Moon Geun-young was still sitting on the stones. Staff sprinted in with blankets. When she took hers, her hands were trembling slightly — not entirely the cold; more the residue of a fright that had arrived without an appointment and had not fully checked out.

…

Lee Joon-ik ran the playback.

When the kick arrived on the monitor, the assistant director leaned in before he could decide to.

On screen: no wind-up at all. The walking step simply became the kick. Clean. Merciless. Nothing like the scripted beat — the wife bars the way, a pause, a standoff, he shoves her off. And yet nothing about the frame felt wrong.

Lee Joon-ik watched it twice. He could see exactly what it was: improvised. Not merely in character — there was a trace of genuine actor’s impatience in it too, the sourness of a man kicked back to his mark six times by a scene that would not close. None of that mattered. The shot worked. Cinema is not a documentary; it means showing the audience the most story through the shortest lens.

In the history and in the script both, there had never been any love-story illusion between Sado and his Princess to preserve. The kick didn’t wound the tragedy of the marriage — it kicked the marriage’s long compression, fear, and estrangement directly into the audience’s face.

And it was elegant, in the way of freed labor: it handed the Princess her later motive for turning against her husband and allying with Sado’s mother to accuse him. The script had needed several more scenes to build that — quarrels, a midnight terror. Now it needed nothing. The single kick had amputated three days of shooting from the schedule.

“That’s the take.”

He nodded to the assistant director.

“Next scene. Prepare.”

The assistant director stood frozen for half a second, then raised the walkie.

“Moving on! Next scene — prepare!”

Only then did Baek Si-on walk over and offer Moon Geun-young a hand.

“Sorry, senior. I should have warned you first.”

She took the hand and came up. The kicked spot didn’t hurt much. But in that half-second she had truly believed he was going to kill her.

“It’s fine.”

A beat, and then she laughed.

“That kick of yours — it felt real.”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

“I pulled it.”

Moon Geun-young: “……”

She had been about to be generous with compliments. The generosity packed up and left.

…

For the next scene Lee Joon-ik stopped micromanaging the tempo. The Prince’s guards swept the gate ahead of him, dropping the palace watch where they stood. The rain kept on. Blood loosened off the stones and crept along the seams, a slow red thread following every gradient downhill.

Sword in hand, the Prince fought his way to the entrance of the King’s residence — and there, the paper windows: candlelight, and two silhouettes. King Yeongjo sat inside. Across from him, small, straight-backed, sat his grandson — the boy who would be Jeongjo.

The King was talking to the boy. The voice came through the paper low and half-blurred, but enough words made it through the wet air: learning. A king. Patience. Father and son. And under the words, unmistakable even through paper — a patience the King had never once spent on his own son.

That was the part that cut.

Baek Si-on stood in the rain, sword sagging degree by degree, looking at the shapes on the paper. In that moment the Prince did not fail to kill because his arm failed. He failed because through the paper he saw another life. The same father. The same king. And the gentleness the son had begged for across an entire lifetime, handed freely, through a window, to the next boy.

The hesitation lasted no time at all.

It lasted exactly long enough.

The palace guard arrived from four directions and closed the courtyard. The Prince’s men and the King’s men met with a crash — but Lee Joon-ik refused to shoot it as action cinema. No leaps, no choreographed steel filling the corridor. A story about a father’s power is told in fragments: rain. Footsteps. A blade goes down. Someone falls. Cut.

Next scene: the Crown Prince alone in the middle of the courtyard, face undone, sword turning in his hand to cover all four directions — and the blade had nothing left of the incursion’s certainty, only the last bluff of a cornered animal. His guards were down in the water everywhere, and the rain went on trying to wash the red out of the seams and failing.

The great doors opened from within.

King Yeongjo came out — played by Song Kang-ho with no anger in him at all, and no fear either. He looked at the son who had brought a sword to his door with nothing in his face but a king’s cold appraisal of treason — no father anywhere in the frame to be found.

Baek Si-on stood in the rain under that look, and the sword-point went down, and down, and down.

“Cut.”

Lee Joon-ik called it, and then:

“That’s the take. That’s the night — wrap!”

The whole set exhaled at once. Rain, night, an incursion, a kicked senior — the production had managed to load every suffering it owned into a single shift.

Park Ji-hun arrived at a run with a towel the size of a carpet and cocooned Baek Si-on from head to shoulders.

“Hot shower. Now. Move.”

“Mm.”

New York Doesn’t Believe in Blue-and-White Porcelain

The coffee-house video leaked onto Twitter anyway.

The composition was nothing professional.

The person filming had hands that shook twice — which did nothing to stop the clip from making several rounds in short order.

The reason was simple.

The man playing the piano in the frame was handsome, and the song was good.

And the person watching him from beside the piano was Emma Stone.

❧ ❧ ❧

Late April

Afternoon

Four Seasons Hotel, New York · a suite conscripted as a styling room

Inside the Four Seasons suite.

By the time Baek Si-on returned to his room, the living area had already been converted into a temporary styling studio. Three racks stood in a row under neatly arranged black dust covers; accessories were laid out in an orderly line; the shoes sat on red velvet.

Park Ji-hoon had learned his lesson this time.

To keep the fashion PRs from brawling in front of Baek Si-on again —

Or, more precisely, to prevent a repeat of the last pageant: three couture houses at the suite door, warmly greeting each other’s ancestors —

Park Ji-hoon had reflected bitterly on his sins and chosen one house only: Tom Ford.

And Tom Ford did not let the exclusivity go to waste.

They arrived with three proposals, each with its theme essay conscientiously written.

Mark stood beside the racks, dressed — as always — more precisely than the client, his shoe polish bright enough to reflect human vanity back at it.

“This year’s brief is ‘Flowers in the Mirror, Moon on the Water.’ Everything orbits Eastern imagery.”

He gestured at the first rack as he spoke.

The assistant stepped up and lifted the dust cover.

Late April · Four Seasons, New York · the Met Gala fitting briefing

Dragon damask, single-breasted Near-black ground · turn in the light and the dragon surfaces, winding from the right shoulder down to the left hem · impact: immediate · the house’s favorite
Blue-and-white porcelain, double-breasted Blue and white interwoven across the whole canvas, the pattern running from collar to cuff · after the Yuan ware in the museum vaults, modernized · detail fine as a whole official-kiln tea service fired into cloth
Ink-wash bamboo, single-breasted Pale-grey ground, ink bleeding through it in uneven shades · a few freehand bamboo strokes from the left chest to the hem

The visual impact of the first was considerable.

It was, by a wide margin, Mark’s favorite.

Park Ji-hoon rejected it without touching the fabric.

“The dragon element — on an American, it’s exotic flavor. On a European, fashionable curiosity.”

“On him, it’s a political incident.”

Mark went quiet.

He genuinely had never considered the question.

The brand’s logic usually runs: if it looks good, ship it. The East Asian cultural and political load underneath is not their department.

He turned and signaled the assistant to uncover the second.

The blue-and-white porcelain double-breasted.

“Softer than the dragon. We took the Yuan blue-and-white patterns from the museum’s own collection and restructured them for now —”

“No.”

Park Ji-hoon cut him off:

“In that palette, a fishtail gown is beautiful. A qipao is beautiful. A fitted evening gown, beautiful. Blue against white flatters a woman’s skin and her lines by itself.”

“But put it on a man, and nine times out of ten, ‘blue-and-white porcelain’ proceeds directly to ‘a large ceramic achieving sentience, live on the carpet.’”

“That is a disaster for the artist and the brand in equal parts.”

Mark looked at the porcelain suit, then looked up at Baek Si-on’s proportions.

In the privacy of his own head, he admitted Park Ji-hoon had a point.

Out loud, he would never.

“Third one.”

The assistant uncovered the last rack.

The ink-wash bamboo, single-breasted.

Park Ji-hoon walked over, took it off the hanger, spread it open, and held it up under Baek Si-on’s chin for a comparison.

Then he stepped back a pace, squinted, and ran the assessment again from the start.

In the end, he still shook his head.

“No.”

Mark finally lost the fight with himself.

“What is wrong with THIS one?”

“My boss, in this, reads as the auctioneer specifically hired to remind everyone of lot two’s opening bid.”

Baek Si-on: “……”

The assessment was damagingly specific.

“Who’s the artistic director this year?”

Park Ji-hoon turned to Mark.

Mark cast his memory back a little, and said:

“A Chinese director. Wong Kar-wai.”

❝

Sunglasses King. The man who shot qipao, neon, rain-slicked alleys, the damp nights of old Shanghai, and every almost-said thing inside them, into the single most recognizable aesthetic the East owns.

— the examiner, identified

If the chief examiner was that man, he was not going to love anything surface-deep.

Because the grammar of Eastern aesthetics has never once been direct.

When they say regret, what they say is —

When they say love unattainable, what they say is —

The brief, as actually graded

When that tradition says regret, it does not say regret. It says: “If one day we stand in the same snow, this life already counts as growing white-haired together.”

When it says love unattainable, it says: “Luoyang lies in full bloom of brocade flowers — yet no spring came the day I did.”

Real Eastern aesthetics shows you lamplight, and never the heart itself. Pile on the dragons, phoenixes, red, gold thread, embroidery and auspicious clouds, and the grade that comes back reads “did the reading” — at best, a pass.

At that thought, Baek Si-on looked over at Park Ji-hoon.

Park Ji-hoon had, evidently, arrived at the same thought some moments earlier.

The thing worth honoring was not the elements.

It was the examiner.

“The problem with all three of these is that they’re too overtly ‘Chinese motif.’” Park Ji-hoon said.

“…That’s a bad thing?” Mark asked.

“For your Western talent, no. For my boss — yes.”

“Meaning what?”

“Change direction.”

“To what?”

“Old Shanghai.”

“…Wait. You mean don’t do the surface of Chinese style — do the aesthetics of Chinese film?”

“Yes. Can the new direction be re-filed with the organizers in time?”

Mark’s brain was re-organizing at extreme speed:

Overtime pay at the fabric workshops. The mental state of the design team. The organizers’ approval window. And his own sleep schedule, which had just ceased to exist.

“If I call them right now and tell them not to sleep —”

Mark smiled, slightly.

“We can.”

“Then please.”

Park Ji-hoon turned his head toward Baek Si-on, who had offered approximately zero constructive opinions on the wardrobe all day.

“Nothing you want to say?”

“No.”

“Really, nothing?”

Baek Si-on, legs crossed, delivered one piece of feedback with complete honesty:

“I thought the porcelain one was quite good.”

Park Ji-hoon did not hesitate for even a beat.

“Then you can stop talking.”

“On matters of taste, I am unilaterally revoking your participation rights.”

That was when there were two knocks at the door. Mark’s assistant went to open it.

Before he came in, Justin Bieber turned back and said “I’ll be out in a minute,” leaving both bodyguards in the corridor.

“Hey.”

“What brings you here?” Baek Si-on asked.

“Stage design. For the performance.”

And with that, Bieber’s eyes had already traveled to the racks.

Three Tom Fords, sentenced to death but not yet cleared away, hung there quietly.

“These your battle dress for the Met Gala?”

“Something like that.”

Bieber walked over and ran a hand across the porcelain fabric.

“You’d look unreal in this.”

“That’s what I said.”

Park Ji-hoon, off to the side, took his forehead in his hand and pressed.

These two men were, in music, genuine geniuses.

In aesthetics, they would probably spend this lifetime parked at the stage of “if it sparkles, it’s good.”

“If Mr. Bieber likes the element, he could try it.”

Bieber’s eyes lit up, and he turned:

“Really?”

“…Subject to the brand’s opinion.”

Having said it, Park Ji-hoon turned his gaze to Mark.

Mark received the look.

Under standard operating procedure, when a brand PR sees a man at Justin Bieber’s altitude standing in front of his rack and showing obvious interest —

Theoretically, he should have begun selling on the spot.

At minimum: get a jacket onto the man’s shoulder, then take two photographs that looked accidental and were anything but, for the PR department.

But Mark looked at Bieber’s outfit.

The Tom Ford verdict, as delivered in silence

Hoodie.

Sweatpants, waistband gone to half-mast, the logo on the underwear already open for retail business on its own authority.

The sneakers were, admittedly, very expensive. But expensive and right have never been the same thing.

Tom Ford dresses are particular about the man. On Baek Si-on it reads: a brute in a suit — a scoundrel in gentleman’s clothing. On Bieber: ——

Mark declined to render that image in full. A very civilized form of protection. Of the brand, and of Bieber both.

Bieber, entirely unaware that Tom Ford had just declined him on the spiritual plane, put down the porcelain and wandered to the third rack, squinting at the ink-wash bamboo.

“Your team really understands styling.”

“Thank you,” Baek Si-on said.

Park Ji-hoon checked the time and calculated that watching these two trade aesthetics would probably cost him several years of life expectancy. He turned to Mark.

“Direction’s set — we’ll go execute.”

Mark nodded and signaled the assistant to re-wrap the suits in their covers.

“I’ll have the new proposals’ samples over as fast as the ateliers can move.”

He bowed, slightly, to Baek Si-on.

“We look forward to watching you shine at the Met Gala.”

Then he and the assistant and the entire rack convoy evacuated the battlefield at speed.

Watching them go, Bieber drifted to the sofa area and dropped into it, boneless.

“So — business?”

“Weren’t you here for business?”

“I am. Your outfits were so good I nearly forgot.”

Baek Si-on poured him a glass of water.

Bieber took it, didn’t drink, and spun it twice on his knee first.

“Here’s my idea. A swap. I sing yours, you sing mine — and we do one together in the middle.”

“Fine. Which of mine do you want?”

“‘Love Yourself.’ Yours?”

“‘Children’ — off your new album. I like that one better.”

Bieber nodded:

“And the one we sing together?”

“Your call.”

“‘Where Are Ü Now’?”

“So I stand there and sing ‘you never understood me’ — at Selena?”

Bieber: “……”

The original plan for the Met Gala performance had been the full assembly of SB Projects’ big three.

But Ariana was mid-tour, and freshly split from Big Sean besides — in no state of mind for a gala.

The deliverable, however, does not shrink just because the lineup did.

The three hours after that, the two of them spent rehearsing.

❝

Say anything else you like about the man — the private life, the mood that changed like weather. One thing no one had ever managed to deny: the talent.

— the rehearsal, hour three

So the rehearsal went smoothly.

Lyrics run through. The swapped sections run through. Harmonies, blocking.

Occasionally they stopped to change two beats — or because somebody’s run had come too easily, too smoothly, and got its owner one contemptuous look from the other side of the room.

“That run just now was way too much showing off.” Bieber said.

“What?”

“The kind of run whose entire purpose is making the whole room scream.”

“…Making the whole room scream is the assignment.”

Bieber was defeated by the sheer righteousness of it, and laughed.

“You could stand to be subtler.”

“Learn subtlety first. Then come educate me.”

Chapter One Hundred Ninety Four

Safekeeping Business, Again

The Milan morning after · plus one Seoul office, twenty-seven minutes before a phone rings A girl-group plan nobody should like · a duet request that gets described as a queue-jump · the word Dream scribbled on a plan’s back cover · lips that remember temperature, which dreams don’t · a doorbell, a hard case, and a question about whether her account could cover two and a half million dollars · one string, plucked once Cheongdam-dong, JYP → a Milan hotel, the morning → the peephole → the living-room floor An entertainment company weighs a survival show and a survival show of a different kind; the Nation’s first love files an impossible request with perfect confidence; and the music history that Dubai bought in Doha gets re-shelved, overnight, into Lee Ji-eun’s living room.

Seoul. Cheongdam-dong. JYP Entertainment.

Park Jin-young had been staring at the girl-group proposal on his desk for twenty-seven minutes. Page one carried the provisional title — SIXTEEN.

the girl-group plan · page one · provisional title: SIXTEEN

FORMAT — sixteen trainees. Elimination. Survival war. Audience participation. The winner takes the next JYP girl group.

BUSINESS LOGIC — use the show to manufacture awareness in advance; use competition to manufacture memory; use trainee tears and growth arcs to save half the debut marketing budget.

HUMANITARIAN LOGIC — error. Page not found.

Reviewed for 27 minutes. The plan is clear, fundable, and — read as a document about people — not much resembling one.

He turned to page three and was studying one trainee’s file — age, nationality, years trained, vocal score, dance score, camera presence, personality keywords — when the phone on the desk rang. He hadn’t planned to answer. Then he saw the caller ID, and his hand got there ahead of the plan.

Bae Suzy.

To JYP, Bae Suzy was not an ordinary artist. She was the advertiser contract’s favorite child, the bargaining chip you could put on the table in any film or drama negotiation, one of the only line items in the financial statements that improved the mood of whoever read it. More plainly: she had earned the company more than one building. So the voice Park Jin-young answered with was at least two octaves gentler than the one he’d been using on trainee files.

Bae Suzy → Park Jin-young · voice · the Milan call · JYP HQ, Cheongdam-dong

PD-nim, are you busy right now?

Busy, sure. But when our Suzy calls, how could I possibly be busy.

A light laugh down the line.

See — PD-nim really is the best~

The corner of his mouth curled.

Go ahead. How’s Milan? Did the Fendi show go smoothly?

Smoothly. But I’m not calling about fashion week.

Then?

I want to sing.

“……” He froze. Then he sat up straighter.

You want to sing?

Mm.

So our Suzy finally remembers she’s a singer?

The voice on the other end even managed a note of innocence.

PD-nim, I never forgot.

He didn’t expose her. Of course he knew she remembered. But “remembering you’re a singer” and “actually wanting to sing” are separated by a minimum of three dramas, twenty commercials, and a hundred brand events.

Sing what? Solo? An OST?

He picked up a pen.

Mm… I want to do a duet with Baek Si-on sunbae.

Park Jin-young took the phone away from his ear, looked at the name on the screen, and put it back.

Who did you say?

Baek Si-on sunbae.

He laughed — the particular laugh of a parent hearing “PD-nim, I want the moon, please fetch it for me.”

Suzy-ah. More people want him on a feature than there are trainees trying to get into JYP.

So PD-nim means it’s hard?

I mean the queue is long.

Then cut the queue for me~

“……”

He had been preparing to continue with reality. Her next sentence blocked the road reality needed.

Korea has plenty of producers who can write songs. But the person who can write something Baek Si-on sunbae would actually open his mouth to sing — PD-nim, that’s probably just you.

Park Jin-young discovered that he was incapable of objective judgment while being flattered at this caliber.

Then… your thoughts on the song?

She knew perfectly well that the question meant he was already considering it. She did not get smug.

I’m thinking of a song about dreams.

Dreams?

The short kind you have in the afternoon. There’s still light outside. You close your eyes and doze off, and there’s someone in the dream. You can’t see the face clearly, but you know it’s smiling. Then you wake up — the sunlight is still there, the person is gone, and your mood is good.

…Are you pitching lyrics, or chatting with me?

Both. What does PD-nim think?

He was quiet. Honestly: the concept wasn’t bad. Sweet without being cloying. Light, with an aftertone. And more important — a Billboard champion, a Venice Best Actor, a man the press calls a suit tyrant, suddenly singing low about an afternoon nap. The contrast was itself the headline. And most important of all: nothing in Baek Si-on’s entire public catalog ran in that direction.

So your bet is that novelty gets him.

Not a bet. Faith in PD-nim’s ability.

He picked up the pen and wrote one word on the blank back cover of the trainee plan: Dream.

One last question. Suzy-ah. Do you want the duet, or do you want to get close to him?

PD-nim, are those two things in conflict?

…No.

Then I leave it to you, PD-nim.

Don’t leave it to me yet.

He capped the pen.

I’ll study what he’s been into lately. I’ll think through the song’s direction. On your side — you say nothing to anyone.

Why?

Because if Baek Si-on says no, I don’t want the entire industry finding out that Park Jin-young got returned.

The laugh came out of her at full volume.

Relax, PD-nim. He won’t say no.

You’re that confident in yourself?

I’m confident in PD-nim.

“……”

He hung up, set the phone on the desk, and looked at the single scrawled word on the plan’s back cover. Dream.

❝

A dream, filed in a business plan, is a very safe thing. The same dream, arriving in a Milan morning — considerably less so. Depends whose sleep it lands in.

— two cities, one word

❧ ❧ ❧

Because in a Milan morning, for Lee Ji-eun, dreams were not safe at all. She woke staring at the ceiling and spent three seconds determining whether the picture in her head was a dream or a memory. Then she remembered. Not a dream. Dreams don’t leave temperature on your lips. Worse — she had, for a moment, actually hoped it wasn’t a dream. She pulled the blanket up to her chin, closed her eyes. Opened them again.

“Insane.”

Unclear whether she was cursing the version of herself who had initiated last night, or the man who had said the teacher had better see it through — and then had. She turned her head toward the phone on the nightstand. Unread messages. She picked it up.

Yoo In-na. The earliest was last night’s three question marks. Then:

unread · Yoo In-na · overnight

“Lee Ji-eun.”

“Did you eat something in Milan that you shouldn’t have?”

03:27 — “Wake up and reply. Otherwise I’m assuming the Italian mafia has you.”

Studied for five seconds. Then, with tremendous dignity, the phone was placed face-down on the nightstand. Official position: not awake. Not fully.

She sat up, hair slightly wrecked, the pillow from last night still lying where she’d thrown it on the sofa. The MIDI keyboard sat alone on the coffee table, its cable coiled into a knot more complicated than Jung Han-teuk’s career prospects. She got up, slippers slapping, and walked to the table. When the laptop woke, her eyes locked automatically onto Cindy_23_Milan_final (not the final version). The parenthetical seemed to be summarizing last night on her behalf: this isn’t the final version either. But it has been saved.

The doorbell rang.

Her whole body locked. First thought: Baek Si-on. Second thought: shame at the first thought. She checked her pajamas, spun around, pulled on a robe, dragged her hands through her hair twice, and only then went to the peephole. Outside stood Jung Han-teuk. Not surprising. Surprising was the thing beside him: a black hard-shell case, about 1.2 meters long, metal guards on the corners, two numbered shipping labels on the surface. And beside the case, a suited middle-aged man holding a folder, and a hotel security guard.

the delivery manifest · 9:40-something a.m., Milan

ITEM — one (1) hard case, black, approx. 1.2 m, reinforced corners

LABELS — two, both numbered, both international

ESCORT — one courier (suit, folder), one hotel security

SENDER — a man who left the country at 7:15 without knocking

Recipient’s first reaction: him. Recipient’s second reaction: shame regarding first reaction.

“Han-teuk oppa.”

“Ji-eun-ah.”

Han-teuk glanced at the case, then back at her.

“Si-on asked me to hand this to you.”

She looked down at the case.

“What is it?”

“Background first — Si-on flew out this morning for the BRIT Awards in London. He left this with me on his way.”

Her eyes moved from the case to his face.

“He’s gone?”

“Mm.”

“What time was the flight?”

“Seven fifteen. Should be airborne by now.”

Her face showed nothing at all. But in that instant the door handle felt, briefly, cold. Kissed good night, gone by morning — it was exactly the kind of thing Baek Si-on would do. What was worse was that she minded, a little. Han-teuk, reading the flicker, hurried to add:

“He left too early to knock — he said your sleep is bad, and he wasn’t going to wake you.”

She looked up at him. He immediately raised one hand, swearing:

“Verbatim. I didn’t polish a word.”

“So — what is this?”

She let go of the handle and pointed at the flight case. Han-teuk opened the folder, glanced at the documents, then looked up at her.

“Ji-eun-ah. First, a question.”

“Ask.”

“Is your account currently good for two and a half million dollars?”

“……What?”

Her brow came down. He handed her the folder.

“A gift from the Qatari royal house. The 1962 Gibson J-160E acoustic guitar used by John Lennon. With the collector’s certificate, the full chain of provenance, the appraisal issued by the Qatar National Museum — and an international transport insurance policy written at two and a half million dollars.”

She absorbed the paragraph, and then asked the only reasonable question:

“Why was he carrying this thing on his body?”

“Because it’s expensive.”

“……”

The explanation held. A vintage guitar with a certificate and royal provenance is, paradoxically, safest in person. She nodded.

“Then what does he mean, you’re safekeeping it for him?”

“He says this thing can’t go to London.”

“Why?”

“His exact words were — if the British catch sight of this thing, odds are they’ll find a way to make it disappear for another half century.”

“……”

She went quiet. Logically, it was airtight. One press photograph and the entire British music industry detonates — and then comes the loan, the exhibition, the donation, the restitution request, each one a separate year of trouble. Not taking it was right. Leaving it with someone he trusted to carry back to Korea was right. The problem was — why her? Baek Si-on did not lack for competent people. Park Ji-hoon could have done it. Any employee at S.W Studio could have. And yet, her. She didn’t ask the question out loud. She knew what Han-teuk would say — he didn’t explain — and she already had her own answer anyway.

❝

If the British catch sight of this thing, odds are they’ll find a way to make it disappear for another half century.

— Baek Si-on, on the logistics of other people’s heritage

“Ji-eun-ah.”

Han-teuk broke into her thoughts.

“Mm?”

“Could we possibly speed up the signing?”

He pointed at the folder in the courier’s hands:

“This transfer needs your signature. And the insurers need the recipient registered.”

She took the folder. Page one: the collector’s certificate, the Qatar National Museum seal. Page two: the full provenance chain. Page three: the international transport policy — and her eyes landed on the insured-value column. Two and a half million dollars.

“This thing is worth two and a half million dollars?”

“That’s the insured amount. The actual market value has no ceiling. Because it will never come to market,” Han-teuk explained.

“And if I break it while it’s in my custody?”

“Which is why I asked whether your account covers two and a half million dollars.”

“……”

She glared at him, took the pen, and signed the receipt line. Han-teuk closed the folder with the relief of a man who has personally survived a customs inspection, handed it back to the courier, and the suited man — with a polite bow — carried the case inside together with the security guard, set it down beside the living-room coffee table, and withdrew without a sound.

❧ ❧ ❧

The living room held Lee Ji-eun and one black flight case. She crouched down. Her fingers found the latches. The metal was a little cool. First latch: open. Second latch: open. She lifted the lid. Dark-gray fitted foam, and lying in the molded hollow, quiet as furniture that had always lived there: a guitar. The wood grain sat somewhere between honey and amber; the tuning pegs carried the faint oxidation of years; the frets held small dents. The whole instrument was not beautiful. It was even, frankly, old. But lying there, it had a kind of weight that did not need to explain itself to anybody.

She looked for a long time. Then she picked up her phone, opened Baek Si-on’s thread, and typed:

“You put a museum in my room?”

Send. She waited. Nothing. She typed again:

“What happens if it gets damaged while it’s in my custody?”

Send. Still nothing. And then she remembered: he was on a plane. Milan to London was roughly two hours — before the airport procedures, he was almost certainly not looking at a phone. She set it on the carpet and looked back at the case. Reached in. Her finger found the thinnest string and plucked, lightly. One very short tremor ran from the string into the air and disappeared somewhere between the walls and the curtains. Sixty-some years ago, someone may have plucked this exact string in exactly this way. She didn’t know. What she knew was that from now on, this piece of music history’s weight was temporarily in her hands.

She lifted her fingers off the string. Picked up the phone once more. Still nothing. She typed one last line:

“You’d better come back with the award.”

Send. Then she laid the phone face-down on the carpet, got up, and walked to the window, pulling the curtains open. Milan’s morning light was excellent — a very blue sky, very old buildings, a church spire driving up into the distance. She said it quietly, to the window:

“Who told you to choose me?”

Milan went on not answering. The phone went on being silent. And she was already, somewhere in the back of her mind, seriously considering whether this guitar should be used to play ‘Three Bears’.

Chapter One Hundred Forty Nine

Senior Baek Si-on Gives Out Another Chance

November 25 · 4:04 a.m. Final descent · cabin lights dimmed to amber Somewhere over the Yellow Sea · inbound Incheon The last stretch of a long-haul flight, where a person can no longer tell whether he is awake, or merely forced to keep working from inside the cloud layer.

The cabin lights had been turned down low.

Baek Si-on was reclined in his seat with the thin airline blanket over him, asleep, though not deeply.

Beside him, Baek Eun-ah still had her tablet in her arms. She had meant to organize the post-landing schedule, and had made it exactly halfway through before listing sideways herself.

A flight attendant came over and spoke softly:

“Mr. Baek, we’ll be landing in about twenty minutes.”

Baek Si-on opened his eyes.

“Mm.”

He set the blanket aside and got up to change. Baek Eun-ah snapped awake too.

“We’re here?”

“Soon.”

From his carry-on pocket he took out a black Under Armour training set — jacket, tee, tapered pants, all black. His own signature line hadn’t gone into production yet, but the signing fee had already been collected, and a man in his position could at least manage the self-awareness of an endorser.

the carry-on · one outfit, worn off the rack endorser’s instinct

Under Armour All-black training set: zip jacket over a performance tee, tapered training pants, nothing branded louder than a small wordmark at the chest. Worn with the folded grey airline blanket over one forearm. It is not a look; it is a logistics decision — the airplane equivalent of wiping down a workstation. And yet: broad shoulders, narrow waist, and the cabin’s amber reading light do for black activewear what stage lighting once did for a court coat.

All black, pre-dawn cabin, blanket on the forearm — the endorser’s instinct, field-tested.

Somewhere in Baltimore, a signature fee slept soundly, unaware of the publicity it was earning at four in the morning over the Yellow Sea.

November 25 · 4:30 a.m. Wheels down · the phone comes back to life Incheon International Airport A soft jolt as the wheels kiss the runway — and then a nation that has been holding its messages all night lets go of them at once.

Around four-thirty, the plane touched down at Incheon. The wheels hit the runway with a soft jolt.

Baek Eun-ah rubbed her eyes and switched off airplane mode first thing.

The next second, her phone looked like someone had dropped it into a beehive.

KakaoTalk. Email. Missed calls. Interview requests. The studio group chat. Screenshots of headlines from every major outlet — jumping out one after another.

Baek Eun-ah had still been half-asleep. After thirty seconds of scrolling, her face went from drowsy to fully awake. After another minute, there was no trace of sleep left anywhere on her person.

“Cousin.”

“What.”

She looked up at him.

“While you were sleeping, Korea finished holding your album-launch press conference, your scandal clarification, your fan celebration, and your battlefield, in that order.”

“Give me the point.”

She looked back down and double-checked.

“‘Love Yourself’ is now the second Human single.”

Baek Si-on’s eyes came up.

“Who decided that?”

Her expression was complicated.

“The Apple Killer.”

Baek Si-on took out his own phone. Signal reconnected — and the KakaoTalk notifications from Lee Ji-eun and Choi Jin-ri were instantly buried under the avalanche pouring in behind them.

He read for nearly three minutes, and by then the shape of the night was clear.

As for the decision itself — Scooter promoting “Love Yourself” into the second Human single — he had no real objection.

He had never actually liked “King” all that much. Too theatrical. Yes, the stage effect was genuinely excellent. But a song working on stage and a person liking it were two separate matters.

“Love Yourself” was different. It had emotion, melody, a storyline running through the video — it was easier for a general public to catch.

More important: the song’s writing credit included Justin Bieber. Pushing it up now doubled, incidentally, as a warm-up for the eventual return. One arrow, many birds.

The only flaw in the archery was that nobody had informed the person tied to the arrowhead.

Baek Si-on backed out of the news page and opened his messages.

Scooter had already sent a long block:

KakaoTalk · Scooter Braun · received over the Pacific, unanswered until now

SCOOTER: Baek. By the time you land you’ll have seen the change. Read it all first. Then decide whether to yell at me.

SCOOTER: “King” has completed its mission. Tonight’s throne stage showed the entire world you can sit on it. Keeping it as the second single is strong, but it locks you into a box labeled KING, CONQUEROR, VICTOR.

SCOOTER: Human cannot only sell that.

SCOOTER: “Love Yourself” fits right now. Emotion, imagery, a hook built for spreading — and it walks into radio and into ordinary listeners’ daily lives far more easily. The video teaser uses the director’s cut. No dirty editing. Nothing staged beyond what was shot.

SCOOTER: I know this is a call I should have made to you first. But you were over an ocean, and the Spotify window doesn’t wait. After Taylor pulled out, the homepage banner and the Today’s Top Hits pin were open for a few hours only. Miss it, and the next equal slot arrives after Christmas.

SCOOTER: You didn’t hire me to hesitate. You hired me to place the bet when the bet is there.

SCOOTER: So I placed it.

SCOOTER: If you’re angry, aim it at me. But look at the data first.

Read 4:41 a.m. No reply typed. No reply deleted. The phone simply went dark, and went into a pocket.

Baek Si-on finished the block, locked the screen, and put the phone in his pocket.

He didn’t yell. He didn’t praise, either.

The plane finished taxiing. The door opened, and cold air came pouring up the jet bridge.

Incheon at four-thirty in the morning should have been the quietest hour of its day. Instead, the moment Baek Si-on stepped out of the VIP channel, flashbulbs lit up in his face.

Click.

Click-click-click-click.

Baek Eun-ah squinted against the flashes. She had assumed this hour might produce two or three well-informed reporters at most; she had not assumed an entire picket line. The professional devotion of Korean journalists sometimes genuinely moved a person — sometimes genuinely made one want to call the police.

the arrivals corridor · four-thirty in the morning, microphones first

“Baek Si-on-ssi!”

“How does it feel to win two awards at the American Music Awards?”

“‘Human’ is sweeping the Korean realtime charts — your response?”

The microphones arrived before the questions finished loading.

“Miss Taylor kissed you on the cheek at the ceremony. What is your relationship?”

“Will you collaborate with her?”

“The ‘Love Yourself’ video with IU-ssi — was the kiss actually filmed?”

“One more question!”

“The IU rumors—”

At the end of the corridor, a black van waited with its engine running, radiating the patience of a man who had seen every airport in Asia.

Baek Si-on looked at the cluster of microphones and spoke:

“Thank you to every member of the press for coming to the airport before dawn.”

“That ‘Human’ could receive this much love from Korean listeners is my greatest honor. I thank everyone who has supported the album.”

The microphones inched forward half a stage.

“The two awards are an encouragement to me personally, and a small footnote to Korean pop music being seen by the world. I’ll keep working.”

Someone immediately followed up:

“Miss Taylor kissed your cheek at the ceremony. What exactly is your relationship?”

Baek Si-on looked at that reporter.

“If it had been you standing there, she would have sent you off with the same cheek kiss.”

“……”

The reporter refused to surrender.

“Will you collaborate, then?”

Baek Si-on said:

“With the right work, any excellent artist is a possibility.”

The reporter pressed again:

“The kiss scene in the ‘Love Yourself’ video with Lee Ji-eun-ssi — was it really shot?”

Baek Si-on gave that reporter a single glance.

“When the video is officially out, everyone can watch the work.”

Reporter: “……”

Which was a no-comment wearing a coat.

Baek Eun-ah stepped in instantly:

“Thank you all for your hard work. Actor Baek Si-on is heading straight to set to film — please let us through.”

The two security fell in around the channel. Baek Si-on nodded his thanks down the line of cameras.

“Thank you for your work.”

The van’s door slid shut, the flashes were amputated mid-gesture, and the car pulled out of the airport ramp into the pre-dawn expressway.

By the time they reached the edge of Seoul proper, the sky was turning pale. Baek Eun-ah got out near Gangnam. Before closing the door, she leaned back in.

“Cousin. Eat something at the set before you shoot.”

“I know.”

Only then did she shut the door, and the van started again, south, toward Jeonju.

While the first morning light spilled across the fields past Suwon, the sky faded from ink-blue to ash-blue, and the sun climbed out from behind the eastern ridgeline of Gyeonggi Province like an employee who was late but extremely diligent.

November 25 · 9:07 a.m. Off the expressway · straight to work, no stop at home Jeonju · the Sado backlot, Studio Set B A double AMA winner walks back through the gate in a black training set, and the first thing the crew does is congratulate him for winning while wearing someone else’s clothes.

Just past nine, the van came off the expressway and stopped at the entrance of the studio backlot.

The crew spotted him, froze for a beat, and then the greetings rained down.

“Actor Baek is back?”

“You’ve worked hard!”

“Congratulations on the awards!”

Baek Si-on nodded to each in turn.

“Thank you.”

He walked over to Lee Joon-ik.

“Director.”

Lee Joon-ik looked up and studied him once, head to toe.

“No shooting this morning. You just got off a plane — go rest. We start again this afternoon.”

“Understood.”

Baek Si-on was on his way to change when a small, careful voice came up behind him.

“Senior.”

He turned.

A young actress in a court-lady’s hanbok stood a short distance away. She bowed.

“Hello. I’m Park So-dam.”

In this film, Park So-dam played Royal Noble Consort Moon. A low-ranking court lady, spotted late in King Yeongjo’s reign, elevated past every convention to the rank of royal consort, bearing him two daughters. And in the official history, this Lady Moon was one of Crown Prince Sado’s most lethal political enemies: the voice that spent years beside Yeongjo, amplifying every report of the prince’s instability, widening the crack between father and son. In a certain sense, she was one of the hands that pushed Sado, step by step, toward the rice chest.

Park So-dam, visibly afraid he might not remember her at all, of the hands that pushed Sado, step by step, toward the rice chest.

Park So-dam, visibly afraid he might not remember her at all, hurried to add:

“You probably won’t remember me, senior, but when you gave that lecture at the Korea National University of Arts — I was in the audience.”

“I remember you.”

Park So-dam’s eyes lit up like someone had plugged them in.

“You — you remember me?”

“Mm.”

For the record: that lecture had seated at least four hundred people. She had been third row from the front, right of center. He had been standing on a stage with several hundred heads between them. By any law of probability, committing her to memory was impossible.

While Park So-dam’s brain was still running the odds, Baek Si-on spoke again:

“You appeared in that ‘Little Apple’ video, didn’t you?”

Her brain stopped.

Completely stopped.

That had been more than a year ago. How could he know? How could a man standing at the very top of the industry have watched the music video of a Chinese viral novelty song?

“Senior… how did you…”

“A K-Arts-trained graduate, and to survive, taking fifteen-second extras in a foreign one-hit wonder’s video.”

Park So-dam’s ears went slowly hot.

“You’re short on opportunities, and you’re short on money. Am I wrong?”

She lowered her head and gripped the sleeve of the court-lady hanbok with both hands.

“…Yes, senior.”

She drew a breath and lifted her head again.

“As long as there’s a camera, I’ll act in anything.”

In this profession, dignity is a later development. What a person pays in before dignity arrives, only the person knows.

“Keep that.”

Park So-dam blinked.

“With that drive, you’ll succeed at whatever you do.”

Her eyes went hot. She bowed fast, before anything could spill.

“Thank you, senior.”

Baek Si-on nodded, said nothing further, and walked off toward the makeup trailer.

Park So-dam stood in place for a few seconds before straightening up, slowly.

A fellow extra in an identical court-lady hanbok sidled over and whispered:

“What did he say?”

Park So-dam blinked the heat back down.

“Nothing.”

A beat. Then:

“He said I’ll succeed.”

The extra stared.

“Senior Baek Si-on said that? Personally?”

“Mm.”

“Then you can skip the lottery today.”

Park So-dam failed to suppress the laugh. Fair, honestly. Compared with a lottery ticket, a solemn you will succeed from a man who had just flown back from the American Music Awards carried a far stronger guarantee of mystical backing.

…

Baek Si-on changed into rehearsal clothes, and with hair and makeup not yet due, went back to the van, lay down in the high-roof compartment, and was asleep within minutes.

To be honest, the vehicle’s greatest value no longer had anything to do with the manufacturer’s brochure phrase, premium mobile business lounge. Its true function was letting a human boundary-condition test case remain approximately human between two jobs.

In the afternoon, he shot two palace scenes. Nothing complicated — mostly re-finding the Crown Prince’s physical state and emotional position. When they wrapped, he had just settled into a chair beside the monitors when Park Ji-hun brought his phone over.

The screen said: Baek Eun-ah.

He answered.

Seoul Plaza?

Not an ordinary venue. It sat directly in front of City Hall, beside Gwanghwamun Square, with Gyeongbokgung to the north and, further north still, the Blue House — the most politically and symbolically loaded square meter in the country, the ground where every major gathering in modern Korean history had chosen to happen.

Decline it.

What? Cousin, that’s Seoul Plaza!

I’m holding this concert for the fans. Not to convert the center of Seoul into a personal triumphal arch. And not to draft every commuter in the city into a forced field study titled “The Baek Si-on Effect, Traffic Edition.”

He left it there, without going deeper. But a second layer was audible under it. Relations between the Blue House and the cultural world had been poor for a while — the left-leaning filmmakers, the Busan festival, the diving-bell controversy, and the blacklist more and more people could smell. One thing stacked on another had made the government’s cultural image genuinely ugly, and an official push to stage a beloved new global star in the nation’s symbolic heart had an answer key that was not exactly hidden. Not that Baek Eun-ah assembled the entire political diagram — she was not yet able to string the Blue House, the festival, the ministry, the chaebols and the opinion pages into one web. What she did hear was simpler: her cousin declined to spend celebrity privilege on a backdrop.

Public goodwill looks useless on ordinary days. Then a controversy arrives, or a coordinated pile-on, or a media lapdog doing laps — and the people who never promised to march for you become valuable for the purely negative service of not joining the boot.

But there’s a second problem, and it’s more practical.

Go on.

The sound of a tablet being woken up; the set list, scrolled.

A normal concert runs two hours minimum. This album has eight songs. With talk segments and costume changes, we top out — generously — at one.

So where does the other hour come from?

The question was plain. It was also fatal. A free concert does not get safer by being shorter — quite the opposite. At his current altitude, every detail gets magnified, and if the free show ran one hour the think-pieces would write themselves before the encore sweat dried: “Because It’s Free, So Why Try.” “For the PR, So It’s a Show.” “Using Fan Passion as Marketing.” One or two of those would be enough to sour the whole gesture.

Two blinks’ worth of silence. This had left the territory of doting-on-fans entirely. This was promoting the fan base to co-producers. The moment it went public, the fandom would lose its mind — and, on present form, so would every rival agency in the country.

Don’t you dare say “thanks for the work.” Every time you say it, I know actual work just got assigned.

No rebuttal was offered. The call ended, and the general went to war.

Baek Eun-ah started executing immediately. Call one went to the city government. Her tone was faultless. Her reasoning was impeccable: gratitude for the support, respect for the city’s commitment to culture — and, regrettably, Mr. Baek’s wish that a free concert keep its impact on citizens’ traffic and daily order to a minimum, so Seoul Plaza would, for now, be set aside. The official on the line was visibly disappointed and visibly motivated to persuade. Baek Eun-ah did not budge. She was long past the girl who once sat through her first board meeting with blistered shoes and a straight face. Courteous when courtesy was due. A wall when a wall was due.

After hanging up, Baek Eun-ah could see it without seeing it: Bang Si-hyuk already sprinting for the practice room, clapping his hands over his head, bellowing for the boys to line up.

The scene would look a great deal like a small military drill. The only difference was the chant — retired from one, two, one, and re-commissioned as:

“Senior Baek Si-on has given us another chance!”

By evening, the first message groups had already started moving. Baek Eun-ah, who understood by now that her cousin never did anything with one layer, laid out the day’s campaign like a general’s table:

the war room · one cousin, four fronts, zero budget

01 — Venue: decline Seoul Plaza, politely, permanently, with gratitude in every clause. DONE

02 — Runtime: eight songs cannot carry two hours — so the second hour gets built out of guests. NOTED

03 — First guest, locked by phone: Bang Si-hyuk’s boys, one song, no fee, his enthusiasm exceeding all measurable bounds. LOCKED

She was fertilizing the chive patch. The chive patch, as usual, had no idea it was about to be given a roof.

That night, in a Jeonju hotel, the man who had spent the day as the arrowhead read the vote numbers one more time, set the phone face-down on the nightstand, and went to sleep — having decided, without ever saying so out loud, that the bet had been placed correctly.

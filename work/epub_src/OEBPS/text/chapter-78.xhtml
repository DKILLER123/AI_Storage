Chapter Seventy-Eight

The Wave-and-Retreat Game and the Critics' Charge

August 28 10 am Venice Lido · the beach in front of the Excelsior The most idle morning of a man's second life

August twenty-eighth.

Lido Island.

Ten in the morning.

The sun was good.

So good that Baek Si-on felt like a piece of pork belly being pan-fried.

He lay in a beach chair, sunglasses pressing down on the bridge of his nose, a towel spread beneath him, and on the small table beside him half a glass of lemonade whose ice had already melted, and a bottle of sunscreen.

Fifteen metres ahead, the shallow water of the Adriatic.

Baek Eun-ah was crouching on the sand, digging a hole with a plastic trowel she'd found somewhere.

She'd dug for a long time — probably building a sandcastle.

It collapsed halfway through.

She went "ah" and started digging again.

Choi Jin-ri stood beside her, feet in the shallow water. The waves washed over her ankles every few seconds, and she'd take a small step back, and when the wave receded, follow it in again.

Like she was playing some game with the waves whose rules only she knew.

Baek Si-on lay in his beach chair, his eyes narrowed behind the sunglasses, watching the scene in front of him.

Didn't want to move.

Didn't want to do anything.

This was probably the most idle morning he'd had since being reborn.

✿ ✿ ✿

The screening of The Green Fly was scheduled for September second.

The screening schedule at the Venice Film Festival was not random, and it wasn't arranged in the order of application.

The day a film was assigned to screen was itself a signal of how the committee felt about it.

The best slot was the third to fifth day after the festival opened.

That window was where the committee's favourite contenders were placed.

Oscar-season hopefuls, new films by established directors with Golden Lion pedigree, and the top three in the selection committee's internal vote.

To be scheduled here was the committee saying to media and distributors around the world, "watch these."

The middle slot was the sixth to eighth day.

The films in this band were mostly Asian cinema, independent productions, documentaries, or artistic films the committee thought "good, but lacking commercial hit potential."

Quality was assured, but they weren't the inner circle.

The worst was the second day after the opening, and a day or two before closing.

The second day and everyone was still shaking off jet lag and processing the opening film, arguing over the red carpet spectacle.

The last few days, the journalists who were going to leave had already left, the reviews were already out, and a film scheduled here was roughly equivalent to "filler."

The Green Fly was placed in the first day of the middle band.

An independent film from Korea. The director had never won any A-festival award, and both leads were making their big-screen debuts.

Getting into the main competition had exceeded most people's expectations. Landing the first day of the middle band was the committee's position of "we acknowledge you, but you have to prove yourself."

Five days to go.

Five days for Baek Jeong-hun meant the final technical checks and the fermentation period of anxiety.

Subtitle proofing, the final confirmation of the audio mix, the sound-stage adaptation test in the screening hall — every one of those required him to be present personally.

For an actor.

Five days was pure vacation.

Baek Si-on sank another two centimetres into his beach chair.

The sky above the Lido.

So clean and blue there wasn't a single cloud.

He was about to close his eyes for a nap.

His phone on the small round table buzzed.

Baek Si-on picked it up off the table, lifted it in front of his sunglasses, and looked.

KakaoTalk.

Son Nam-won.

Son Nam-won — KakaoTalk

"Boss, have you seen the news at home?"

Three links followed.

Naver Entertainment headline.

Daum real-time trending.

Twitter Korea trends.

Baek Si-on tapped the first link.

The page loaded.

The headline was long, but the keywords jumped out at once:

「Venice」 「Baek Si-on」 「Choi Jin-ri」 「Red Carpet」 「Hand in Hand」.

The attached photo was a high-definition shot.

Lido Island pier.

The warm golden light of the setting sun slanted in from the left of the frame.

Baek Si-on stood on the pier, right hand extended, palm up.

Choi Jin-ri was stepping off the bow of a water taxi onto the pier, her left hand lifting the hem of her navy Dior gown, her right hand resting in Baek Si-on's palm.

The background was the water of the Venetian lagoon and the silhouette of the main island's bell tower in the distance, all of it washed in a pool of melted gold.

The photographer had chosen the angle perfectly, catching the two of them full-length and the whole of Venice behind them, the composition close to a golden-ratio crop.

Baek Si-on looked at it for three seconds.

Nodded.

Not bad.

Then scrolled down to the comments.

He didn't read it closely. He backed out, switched back to KakaoTalk, and replied.

He set the phone back on the table.

Leaned back into the beach chair.

Under the sunglasses, his gaze crossed the sand and landed on the shallow water fifteen metres out.

Baek Eun-ah's sandcastle was finally standing, crooked as it was.

Choi Jin-ri was crouching beside her, helping fix the wall. She fixed it twice; a wave rolled in and knocked a corner of the wall out.

Baek Eun-ah shrieked.

Choi Jin-ri laughed so hard she leaned backward.

August 29–30 Daytime Palazzo del Cinema · the screening halls Sizing up the jury, one film at a time

August twenty-ninth.

Baek Si-on did not go with Baek Eun-ah and Choi Jin-ri to see St Mark's Square on the main island.

Instead he took a boat alone to the film palace on the Lido.

During the festival, the screening halls at the Venice Film Festival ran from nine in the morning to eleven at night, with main-competition films slotted across the hours; anyone with a festival credential could queue and enter.

Baek Si-on had a main-crew credential.

Unrestricted access.

He went to see Joshua Oppenheimer's The Look of Silence, about the Indonesian mass killings.

He came out, sat on the steps of the film palace entrance, and thought for ten minutes.

At ten he went back in to see Xavier Beauvois' The Price of Glory, from a French director of the establishment.

When he came out again, his expression was a shade heavier than before.

August thirtieth.

He watched 99 Homes and Black Souls.

Walking out of the screening hall, Baek Si-on stood in the corridor for a moment.

He began to study the jury.

Not on a whim.

He'd been thinking about it since finishing the first film.

The competition section of a festival is not a gaokao. There is no standard answer and no unified grading rubric.

Twenty films sat before the jury, and who got the prize in the end depended on those several people's aesthetic preferences, professional judgment and on-site bargaining.

To know whether he had a chance, you first had to know the referees.

Chairman: Alexandre Desplat.

Alexandre Desplat

French composer · jury, Venice

Real-name counterpart · Alexandre Desplat

One of the great living film composers — the man whose name on a poster already promises a certain sound. On the jury he is the quiet one who listens for the score beneath the picture.

He was an extremely rare "non-image-creator" among the jury presidents of the three major festivals.

His trade was film scoring.

His credits included Girl with a Pearl Earring, The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, The King's Speech and The Grand Budapest Hotel.

A master composer sitting in the chairman's seat meant he would judge every film with a musician's ear.

Rhythm. Image-sound relationship. Sound design. The timing of silence.

And the entire sound layer of The Green Fly was made up chiefly of ambient sound, dialogue, silence, and a string of f-words.

If Desplat read that as an intentional sound-design strategy by the director — points.

If he read it as the helpless compromise of a two-hundred-million-won low-budget indie — deduction.

Baek Si-on couldn't think of any way Baek Jeong-hun could prove it was the former.

Sandy Powell.

Sandy Powell

British costume designer · jury, Venice

Real-name counterpart · Sandy Powell

A costume designer with an Oscar shelf, and a sharp eye for what a single seam can do. The woman who will notice the gown before anyone else notices the wearer — and the voice that settles whether a red carpet is talk or is art.

English, a costume designer, three-time Academy Award winner for Best Costume Design.

In Hollywood and the European art films, she was a "visual chief justice" level of existence.

When she watched a film she didn't only look at the acting and the plot; she'd look at whether the clothes each character wore served that character's internal logic, at how the fabric's texture reacted under different light, at whether a costume's distress marks were carefully designed or slapdash.

And the entire wardrobe of The Green Fly's leads, Sang-hun and Yeon-hui, came to less than fifty thousand won.

Putting that wardrobe in front of Sandy Powell was equivalent to having a three-Michelin-star chef taste a bowl of Korean instant ramyeon.

Of course, if the ramyeon was cooked well, that was also a kind of aesthetic claim.

But the premise was that the judge would be willing to admit ramyeon was cuisine.

Moving on.

Tim Roth.

Tim Roth

British actor · president of the main competition, Venice

Real-name counterpart · Tim Roth

The jury president — an English actor with a name known to every filmmaker in the room, and a gaze that weighs a film in a single pass. Where he sits, the whole conversation between the jury and the press pivots.

British actor.

Quentin Tarantino's Reservoir Dogs, Giuseppe Tornatore's The Legend of 1900.

No more introduction needed; the man was himself a textbook on "conveying the most complex emotion with the smallest movement of a facial muscle."

His standard for acting was probably the highest of all nine jurors.

This was the only place Baek Si-on could see a glimmer of light.

The rest of the jury.

German director Philip Gröning, Austrian director Jessica Hausner, Palestinian director Elia Suleiman — all old-school European cinephiles obsessive about a distinctive visual aesthetic in their own fields.

Philip Gröning

German director · jury, Venice

Real-name counterpart · Philip Gröning

A German documentarian of slow, patient films — the sort who can hold a shot long enough to watch a person change their mind. He brings the patience of the monastery to the prize table.

Jessica Hausner

Austrian director · jury, Venice

Real-name counterpart · Jessica Hausner

An Austrian director of precise, unsettling films. On the jury she is the one who looks at a picture the way a surgeon looks at a cut — calmly, and far too closely.

Elia Suleiman

Palestinian director · jury, Venice

Real-name counterpart · Elia Suleiman

A filmmaker of deadpan comic stillness, forever watching borders and the small absurdities in between. On the jury he is the one who laughs at the wrong moment — and is usually right to.

Not optimistic.

Very, very not optimistic.

But Baek Si-on wasn't going to burden himself with extra anxiety either.

A two-hundred-million-won low-budget film making it into the Venice main competition was itself already a great win.

A prize would be ancestral-smoke rising from the grave.

Not getting one was still coming home with a gold-plated halo of having been abroad.

Nothing to lose.

September 2 7:30 am Hotel suite · makeup chair Two looks for the day, and the critics already watching

September second.

Venice.

Seven thirty in the morning.

Baek Si-on sat in the makeup chair in his hotel suite, Park Ji-hun standing behind him, left hand steadying the crown of his head, right hand combing the short hair that had grown back into a pattern with a very fine steel comb.

Today's styling came in two sets.

For the daytime photo call and press conference, clothes from the sponsor brand — a Valentino dark navy suit, white shirt inside, no tie, collar open two buttons.

For the evening premiere red carpet, the suit Kim Jae-kyung had made custom.

Park Ji-hun sprayed styling product twice, stepped back, examined the whole effect, and nodded.

"Done."

Baek Si-on sat in front of the vanity, looking at his own face in the mirror.

Today, The Green Fly would have its world premiere at seven that evening.

But before that, the film had already been seen by others.

Nine in the morning.

The Sala Darsena.

Compression of the size of the media screening at the Venice Film Festival.

Half the capacity of the main Sala Grande hall, but the people inside had ten times the destructive power of the Sala Grande.

The chief critics of the world's major film publications.

The daily-ratings scorers.

International rights buyers.

These people would post the first comment on Twitter within fifteen minutes of the film ending.

And those comments would have travelled through the entire festival before the crew had even stepped onto the red carpet.

Baek Si-on knew this flow.

A few days ago, while studying the jury, he'd also gone through Venice's media-ops mechanics.

So now he sat in front of the vanity, running one question over in his head:

Those critics, in the darkness of Sala Darsena right now, were watching Sang-hun on the screen.

Watching Sang-hun wake from a nightmare.

Watching Sang-hun beat his father.

Watching Sang-hun slam his fist into one face after another on the debt-collection road.

Watching the thing in Sang-hun's eyes at the end, when he protected the schoolgirl.

What would they think?

He didn't know.

September 2 12:30 pm The Casino · rooftop terrace, the photo call A press release that changes the arithmetic

Noon, twelve thirty.

The Casino Palace.

The photo call was staged on the terrace.

A huge backdrop printed with the Venice Film Festival Golden Lion logo and sponsor marks stood at the centre of the terrace, with X-shaped tape marks on the ground in front of it marking camera positions, and across from the backdrop, a semicircular photography area where several hundred cameras were packed densely across three tiers of stepped platforms.

Baek Jeong-hun went up first.

Stood in front of the backdrop, hands folded in front of him, face-on posture easy and relaxed.

Finecut's Lee Seung-cheol stood on the far right of the photography area, his phone going off every now and then.

Baek Si-on and Choi Jin-ri came out of the side door of the Casino Palace.

Choi Jin-ri's daytime look today was a nude-pink Gucci knee-length dress.

A simple A-line cut, the waist line cinched cleanly, paired with same-tone pointed pumps. Her hair was a side-parted half-down style, with a pearl stud replacing last night's diamond clip on the left side.

Her makeup was a little lighter than on opening night.

The lighting at a press conference was different from the red carpet.

The red carpet was a burst of flashbulbs — you needed it heavier to hold up. The press conference was continuous softbox panels; makeup too heavy looked fake.

SM's styling team really did know their craft.

The two of them walked to the backdrop and stood firm.

Baek Jeong-hun in the centre, Baek Si-on on the left, Choi Jin-ri on the right.

The shutter sounds crashed in again.

About fifteen minutes of shooting.

The photography ended, and the three of them stepped down from the backdrop and walked along the Casino Palace corridor toward the press conference hall.

At the corner of the corridor.

Lee Seung-cheol came running up from behind, holding his phone.

"Director Baek!"

All three turned at once.

Lee Seung-cheol's face carried an expression Baek Si-on had never seen on him.

This project director, who'd been running international festivals for over a decade and seen everything, had the lines at the corners of his eyes pressed deep by a smile.

He walked up to the three of them and, without a word of preamble, held the phone out.

"Take a look."

Baek Jeong-hun took the phone.

Baek Si-on and Choi Jin-ri leaned in from either side.

The first.

The Hollywood Reporter website headline review.

Posted: thirty minutes ago.

"A Korean masterpiece that leaves Venice gasping. Director Baek Jeong-hun, with his opening montage, completes the most brutal excavation of domestic violence in nearly a decade. And lead Baek Si-on delivers the most shudder-inducing performance in this year's main competition. He is like a beast gnawing in despair in a pool of blood. Every punch he throws lands precisely on the rotting root of a patriarchal system."

— The Hollywood Reporter · online first review

Baek Jeong-hun swiped down.

The second.

Variety.

Chief critic Guy Lodge's personal Twitter.

"Forget those neat Hollywood uniforms. Koreans have once again shown the world what pain-cinema means. The debt-collection sequences in the first half are vivid and kinetic; the male lead's violence is not release but a despairing howl. In this year's Venice best-actor race, this Asian young man has kicked the door wide open."

— Guy Lodge · Variety · chief critic

Baek Jeong-hun kept swiping.

The third.

European independent critic, David Ehrlich, IndieWire columnist.

"Saw The Green Fly. I don't even want lunch. My stomach is full of physical shuddering. Baek Si-on looks like an incurable scum in the film, but the look in his eyes when he finally protects that schoolgirl had me, a forty-year-old man, in tears in the screening room. An absolute Golden Lion contender."

— David Ehrlich · IndieWire

The rest were short reviews of wildly effusive praise.

Baek Si-on's gaze lifted from the phone screen.

The media's reaction was far better than the pessimistic forecasts he'd made alone in his hotel room a few days earlier.

He'd thought those old-school European cinephiles, who were used to master-level mise-en-scène, would disdain this film's rough industrial quality.

But he'd underestimated one thing.

Those intellectuals who ate fine French every day and drank dry martinis.

Now and then being slapped in the face with a bowl of Korean lower-class violence ramyeon that was mixed with blood and mud, the pain and rawness that exploded from the nerve endings became, for them, the stimulation they craved most.

The roughness hadn't become a deduction.

Instead they'd filled it in themselves, reading it as the most extreme realism.

"Seems… better than I thought."

Baek Jeong-hun heard this and laughed out loud.

"Better?"

He reached over and slapped his nephew on the back of the head.

"Come on. Press conference."

September 2 1 pm The Casino · second floor, press-conference hall Questions, and a crew that has already won

The press-conference hall was on the second floor of the Casino Palace.

A long table was set on the platform, with three name cards, three microphones and three bottles of water on it.

About two hundred journalists sat below.

Long lenses and cameras packed the front three rows to the fifth, and behind them reporters held up recorders and phones; further back were film journalists and cultural correspondents from every country.

Baek Jeong-hun sat in the middle.

Baek Si-on on the left.

Choi Jin-ri on the right.

The moment the lights came up, the shutter sound below fired one round first.

Then went quiet.

The host introduced the three leads in English and Italian respectively.

The question-and-answer session began.

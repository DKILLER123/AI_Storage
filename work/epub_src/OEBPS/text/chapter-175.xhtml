Chapter One Hundred Seventy Five

Agents Have Version Gaps Too

December 26 · 1:00 a.m. until the meeting room folds One studio booked at the hour normal people reserve for emergencies · one chorus recorded before sunrise · three phone calls and one rough mix · a conference-table standoff settled by a wristwatch Los Angeles · Henson Recording Studios → Universal · one conference room with a long table and shorter patience A chorus borrows its grief from a helicopter crash that has not happened yet, a rapper learns what an Asian work ethic sounds like at three in the morning, and a manager demonstrates precisely which version of himself a client paid for.

After Charlie left, Baek Si-on did not go back up to his room to sleep.

He went straight down to the car and set off for Henson Recording Studios.

In America, if you wake most industries’ employees at one in the morning, the boss can expect an email from a law firm by breakfast.

The music industry runs entirely in reverse.

Los Angeles studios sit empty all day; the golden hours run from eight at night to four in the morning.

Producers keep vampire hours, and inspiration, apparently, evolved to match them.

So when Baek Si-on called at one in the morning to book time, the other end of the line asked exactly one question:

“Which room?”

Baek Eun-ah sat in the front passenger seat, her soul visibly queuing at a departure gate.

“Cousin. It’s one a.m. in Los Angeles. Which means it’s six p.m. in Korea.”

“Mm.”

“So technically, I’m living on Korean time right now.”

“That’s one reading.”

“Which means when I fly home, I won’t even have jet lag.”

“Theoretically.”

Baek Eun-ah nodded.

Good.

She had located a reason to keep living.

❧ ❧ ❧

Henson Recording Studios. Half past one in the morning.

Baek Si-on exported Charlie’s original demo from his phone and handed it to the engineer.

The engineer was a middle-aged white man with a full beard whose state of mind looked a great deal better than Baek Eun-ah’s.

Which was fair.

She was working overtime against her will.

He was a nocturnal creature in his natural habitat.

The room went quiet.

Piano.

♪

“See You Again” · demo

Charlie Puth · studio playback · the engineer’s first listen

It’s been a long day without you, my friend—

The demo finished. He took his headphones off and nodded.

“That’s a good demo.”

“Mm.”

Baek Si-on sketched the background quickly. Furious 7. The Paul Walker tribute. Wiz’s rap was already in the can — what the song lacked was a chorus strong enough to carry its entire emotional weight.

The engineer listened once more, gesturing at the mixing desk as it played.

“Got it.”

Baek Si-on started warming up.

He hummed the melody a few times to find the range and the breath points, then ran the chorus twice, adjusting diction and where the air sat.

This song was about losing your closest friend.

And the problem was: Baek Si-on did not have many friends.

Searching his present circle for an emotional sample of “lost the dearest friend I had” turned up surprisingly little.

So when he went hunting for the anchor, the first image that surfaced was Kobe.

Not the Kobe of Newport Coast — the one grilling steaks and racing him through lunch.

The Kobe of another timeline.

January 26, 2020.

Fog in the hills above Calabasas.

A Sikorsky S-76B helicopter.

Nine people aboard.

No survivors.

When the news first broke, the whole world assumed it was fake.

Kobe Bryant — dead? Impossible.

But the reports kept confirming. ESPN confirmed. The Lakers confirmed.

And in the end everyone had to accept one sentence: some people are so strong that they make you forget they can leave.

♪

“See You Again” · the chorus · take one

Baek Si-on · Henson Recording Studios · 1:47 a.m.

It’s been a long day without you, my friend—

And I’ll tell you all about it when I see you again—

When Baek Si-on sang it into the microphone, the engineer’s hand stopped on the mixing desk.

It was a little embarrassing to say out loud.

‘Legend’ had been written with Kobe in mind, and now ‘See You Again’ was being sung with Kobe in mind too.

The man had fed him five steaks at lunch a few days ago, and here was Baek Si-on, mining the grief of losing him for inspiration.

If Kobe ever found out, he would probably say:

“Baek. Could you find a different muse? I’m still alive.”

But precisely because he was still alive —

Baek Si-on understood, better than anyone, how this song needed to be sung.

❧ ❧ ❧

3:20 a.m. The recording was done.

Baek Si-on listened through the headphones once, took them off, and dialed Wiz directly.

Wiz was conspicuously awake — music in the background, voices, the clink of glasses.

Baek Si-on → Wiz · Henson Recording Studios · 3:21 a.m. · outgoing

Baek?

I recorded it.

Recorded what?

The chorus.

Two full seconds of silence, minus the party behind him.

You—the— you flew back from Chicago YESTERDAY.

Mm.

What time is it right now?

Around three.

So you landed, met the YouTube kid, walked into a studio, and cut the song. In one night.

Mm.

Is Asian work efficiency all this terrifying?

Baek Si-on considered the question.

Not really.

Then what was it?

I was in a hurry.

Two seconds of silence on the line.

Brother, that reason is even more terrifying.

Baek Si-on didn’t prolong the call. He sent the file.

A few minutes later, the phone rang again:

Baek Si-on ← Wiz · Henson Recording Studios · 3:27 a.m. · incoming

THIS IS IT! Baek — this is the sound!

I’m flying to Los Angeles right now!

No need to rush.

You cut it at three in the morning while I was still in a bed — what kind of brother would I be?

A normal one.

I don’t want to be normal.

The sound of a man already looking for his keys.

Universal. In the morning. Be there.

❧ ❧ ❧

3:45 a.m. Baek Eun-ah was finally released to go sleep at the hotel.

Baek Si-on grabbed a short refill of sleep in his own room.

A little after seven, the alarm sounded, and he was awake almost before it finished ringing. A quick wash, and he dialed Scooter.

The phone was picked up fast.

Baek Si-on → Scooter · the hotel, Los Angeles · 7:04 a.m. · outgoing

Baek. Tell me you have not prepared me a surprise.

I made a song.

…What does ‘made a song’ mean. Made HOW.

So Baek Si-on gave him the night, in order: Furious 7, Wiz’s rap, the YouTube kid Universal was crushing, the five hundred thousand, and a chorus recorded before sunrise that now needed packaging and delivery to Universal.

Stay there. I’m coming to get you.

Half an hour later, Scooter appeared at the hotel, his face wearing the full sentence: I knew you were incapable of a quiet Christmas.

Baek Si-on handed over the rough mix straight from his phone.

Scooter took it.

the rough mix · played on a phone in a hotel lobby

“See You Again” — rough mix · 3:12 remaining

He played it once from the top. Pulled the progress bar back to zero. Played it again.

When the second playthrough ended, he looked at the audio file, then at Baek Si-on:

“The song is great.”

“You sang it even better.”

“But half a million dollars — isn’t that a little high?”

Baek Si-on shook his head:

“Don’t underestimate human greed.”

“If I offer a hundred or two hundred, Charlie takes that number to Universal to negotiate and squeezes out a little more for himself.”

“At five hundred, the gamble dies at the source.”

Scooter nodded slowly.

The sentence was cold for a morning conversation.

And it was correct.

“Fine. I’ll handle the negotiation.”

“Wiz will be there too.”

“Even better.”

Scooter gave him one more look.

“I’m just curious about one thing.”

“What?”

“How did you sing a chorus with that much feeling in it?”

A teasing look surfaced.

“Don’t tell me you were sulking because I skipped Chicago yesterday.”

Baek Si-on’s expression went strange.

“This song is a memorial. For a friend who’s gone.”

“……”

“Forget I said that.”

“We’re going to Universal. Now.”

Baek Si-on didn’t twist the knife. He just picked up his coat.

“Let’s go.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Universal Pictures. The conference room had been frozen for ten minutes.

One side of the long table: the film’s music supervisor, a production representative, and the head of marketing.

The other side: Wiz, alone.

A player sat at the center of the table. The rough mix had just finished.

“So what you’re telling me is — you flew in from Chicago overnight to pitch us some Asian singer for the chorus of this song.”

Wiz corrected him:

“Not a pitch. A decision.”

“Wiz, we understand how you feel about this song. But you know it isn’t just yours anymore. It belongs to the film’s entire marketing plan—”

Before the sentence landed, someone knocked.

The door opened.

Baek Si-on walked in behind Scooter.

Wiz’s impatience loosened a notch the moment he saw him.

Scooter, by contrast, was entirely at ease. He’d crossed this table too many times — he shook the music supervisor’s hand first, traded two pleasantries with the production rep.

“Long time.”

“Sorry to interrupt your first day back after the holidays.”

Polite words. A tone that was not remotely sorry.

Pleasantries done, he stepped aside and introduced:

“Baek Si-on.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Hello.”

The room’s attention settled on him.

Young. Quiet.

Not the sharpened knife they had pictured.

Which made it harder, somehow, for the music supervisor to say what he had to say.

But it had to be said.

“Mr. Baek. First — we acknowledge your version is outstanding.”

“But there’s a practical problem here.”

“A song about saying goodbye to a beloved friend — sung by a man who doesn’t share a single thread of cultural context with that friend? The emotional chain is broken.”

Scooter patted Baek Si-on’s arm lightly.

Meaning: this one’s mine.

Baek Si-on nodded.

Scooter turned back to the music supervisor.

“Since we’re doing practical problems, let me ask one.”

“Is this film releasing in North America only?”

He said nothing more.

He didn’t have to. Everyone at the table heard the rest.

Furious had not eaten from North American box office alone for years. Its global pull — above all its growth across Asia — had reached a size no production representative could look at and claim not to see.

Hollywood’s idea of honoring Asia, most of the time, was stuffing a few Asian faces into the frame, or teaching the lead to say “thank you” in three languages at a premiere — a bow in Korean, a nod in Japanese, a smile in Mandarin.

Camera. Line. Photo.

And then every core resource got allocated exactly as North-America-centered as before.

Scooter’s meaning, though, was different.

This time it wasn’t surface flattery. It was putting someone genuinely from Asia — someone already charting inside the North American pop mainstream — into the film’s single most important emotional exit.

The music supervisor frowned.

“Scooter. This isn’t a skin-color thing.”

“Of course. In Hollywood, no skin-color thing is ever called a skin-color thing at first.”

Scooter didn’t give him room to answer.

“And I know the counter you’re loading — if we’re serious about Asia, a singer from the mainland moves more units; the market’s biggest.”

“Which runs into a much more practical problem.”

He raised his wrist and looked at his watch.

“Ten minutes ago, Charlie signed the exclusive license.”

“This song now moves through us or not at all.”

“So the conclusion is simple. Do you want a perfect song in your end credits, or do you want to keep defending your so-called practical judgment?”

“Your choice.”

The room went quiet.

Universal’s people understood: this had just become a problem.

Solicitation for the Furious 7 end-credits song had opened in July. Universal, Atlantic Records, and the film’s music department had filtered together — seventy, eighty songs piled into conference rooms, until everyone’s ears lost the ability to tell one heartbreak from another.

Then a demo had arrived from a YouTube kid named Charlie Puth, and the piano’s first bar and first line told a lot of people in that room the same thing: found it.

The problem was that Charlie Puth wanted to sing it himself.

A creator with no commercial record and no major-label muscle, negotiating terms in Universal’s own house.

So all December, Universal and Atlantic had run the same play. Squeeze him. Persuade him. Freeze him out. Let the lawyers send papers. Teach him that talent, in front of a contract, is worth exactly what the contract says.

Charlie had been wavering when Baek Si-on came out of nowhere.

Worse — the man hadn’t just bought the rights. He’d recorded a finished, fully formed version overnight.

Which put Universal in a genuinely painful spot.

The internal picture-lock date was closing in; the music was long past the “keep shopping” stage. Reselecting meant new auditions, new edits, new rights negotiations, a new assessment of every piece of global marketing material.

Every day of delay was money.

And more importantly — they had all heard that version just now. It would take real dishonesty to call it anything less than excellent.

The music supervisor was silent for a long moment.

“We’ll need to discuss internally.”

Scooter nodded.

“Of course.”

He checked the time.

“We have a next appointment. You’ve got ten minutes.”

He stood. Baek Si-on stood with him.

Wiz looked slowly around the table, and put his sunglasses back on.

“One more thing.”

Everyone looked at him.

“If you drop Baek’s version — don’t use my rap either.”

“Pick a new song.”

He said it flatly, and the flatness was what made it land. The whole track had been built outward from that rap verse. If Wiz pulled out, Universal wasn’t swapping a chorus — they were demolishing the song and starting over.

He followed Baek Si-on and Scooter out.

The corridor was far more comfortable than the conference room — for one thing, it didn’t smell of prejudice dressed up as “practical considerations.”

A few steps in, Wiz suddenly laughed.

“Baek. Your manager is a revelation.”

Scooter glanced back.

“Thank you.”

“I thought managers were for phone calls, plane tickets, telling artists not to be late — maybe yelling at the label on the side.”

Scooter smiled.

“That’s the standard version. I’m the paid version.”

Wiz thought it over.

“Honestly? You’re making me want to switch managers.”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

“Scooter only likes undervalued stock.”

“……”

Wiz looked down at himself. A Grammy nomination. A Billboard Hot 100 number-one single — ‘Black and Yellow’. A number-one album on the Billboard 200 — Blacc Hollywood.

“I’m not undervalued enough for you?”

“You’re a finished product.”

Baek Si-on said.

“Finished product. Low margins.”

Three seconds of silence.

“Was that a compliment or an insult?”

“Both.”

Wiz stared at him. Then he laughed.

“Damn it, Adam was right — you are a punchable man.”

“Still better than ‘low margins.’”

“……”

“I take back what I said.”

“You’re not punchable.”

“You’re extremely punchable.”

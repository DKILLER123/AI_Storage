Chapter One Hundred Fifty Four

She Learned Arrangement for This

December 2 · morning into afternoon One quiet scene, then a airport gate Hwaseong Haenggung, Suwon → Hong Kong After the rice chest, the book-room scene felt less like work and more like a transfer to a sanatorium. Ink, brush, silence. Recovery, filmed as punishment’s opposite.

The morning belonged to one scene: the Crown Prince in his book room, painting in ink.

Very quiet. Very short. Against the rice chest, it was like being moved from a cell to a sanatorium.

At midday wrap, Baek Si-on asked Lee Joon-ik for the rest of the day off, and went straight to the airport.

By that afternoon he was on the ground in Hong Kong.

The city’s density climbed a layer at a time along the drive from the airport to the AsiaWorld-Expo, and the skyline of Victoria Harbour kept flickering in and out of the far windows. His first time in the city. No time to look at it.

Out of the car and straight into rehearsal — the familiar chaos, every agency’s idols, dancers and assistants crushed into the backstage corridors, Korean and Cantonese braided through the air.

He stood center stage with “Human” in his in-ears, lights dropping straight down from above. He walked the line once: start mark, chorus mark, camera-cut nodes, and the angle of the final line’s camera. Fast. Not complicated. Compared with the King throne at the AMAs, this was a rehearsal for people who enjoy sleeping.

Afterward the party went to the hotel to drop bags. The plan for the evening, drafted by Baek Eun-ah, was modest: a short walk nearby. Not far. Not crowded. Air. Her cousin had spent recent memory being rained on at a set, locking himself into a wooden box until his knees shook on the way out, flying, walking carpets, singing stages — and however much a person resembles a machine that runs on duty, the machine still needs to see streetlights occasionally.

The moment they stepped out of the room, they ran into Lee Ji-eun and Jung Han-teul in the corridor.

the incognito uniform · off duty, technically Hong Kong · hotel corridor, 7:40 p.m.

The cap and shirt A plain black baseball cap pulled down to the brow line, over an oversized red-and-navy plaid flannel worn loose like a borrowed opinion — the official off-duty geometry of a top idol: three parts comfort, two parts camouflage, and the last five percent pure “I am not on any schedule and you cannot prove otherwise.” Nothing about it says stage. Everything about it says she chose it herself, in under ninety seconds, and would defend each item in court.
The details Dark straight-leg trousers, white sneakers, one white wired earbud already in, the cable running to the phone in her hand — because the whole point of tonight, whatever she claims, lives inside that phone. When she walks, the sneaker rhythm is unhurried. She is incognito the way lighthouses are: technically incognito, extremely findable.

Cap down, flannel loose, one earbud in — incognito, officially, to anyone who doesn’t look.

Jung Han-teul, holding her phone and her room key because he had been requisitioned as a mobile navigation unit, understood before anyone: an outfit this casual was not planned. Neither, therefore, was anything that happened next.

She had a black cap pulled low and an oversized plaid shirt, and looked prepared for no formal agenda whatsoever. Jung Han-teul carried her phone and her room card, visibly conscripted into the role of human navigation.

Four people stared at each other in the corridor like a frozen frame.

Jung Han-teul recovered first, waving with practiced brightness.

“Si-on. What a coincidence.”

Lee Ji-eun looked at Baek Si-on, then at Baek Eun-ah.

“You’re heading out too?”

Baek Eun-ah nodded.

“Mm. A walk nearby.”

Lee Ji-eun said immediately:

“Us too.”

Jung Han-teul: “……”

He distinctly remembered a different sentence. The original plan, verbatim, had been: buy a dessert, come right back.

And so the two parties merged, without any motion being filed, into a single four-person squad walking the Hong Kong streets.

December in Hong Kong is hardly winter — much kinder than Seoul. Cha chaan tengs, dessert shops, and old pharmacies lined the sidewalks. Baek Eun-ah and Jung Han-teul took point, swapping agency schedules and industry gossip with the fluency of two people who run the same bureaucracy from different desks. Baek Si-on and Lee Ji-eun fell in side by side behind them.

After a block, she pulled out her phone, untangled a pair of wired earphones, and handed him one.

“Listen to this.”

“What is it?”

“‘Way Back Home.’ I made a version that fits me.”

He took the earbud and put it in his right ear. She put the other in her own.

The track opened. In the original, Jung Jae-joon had built the chorus out of stacked, processed vocal slices — rhythm and air, deliberately electronic, deliberately untouchable. What was playing now was a clean wooden guitar and her voice, close and unprocessed. No industrial slicing. The repeated lines of the verses had been doubled by hand, take over take, real voice stacked on real voice, and it gave the song less haze and more warmth — the temperature of somebody telling you a story from very near.

Baek Si-on listened to the end. Then he took the earbud out.

She was watching him sideways with a face manufactured for indifference, the eyes under the cap brim betraying her completely.

“Well?”

Baek Si-on said:

“Unexpectedly good.”

Her eyes narrowed.

“Unexpectedly?”

“Better than I imagined.”

“Somehow that isn’t an upgrade.”

Baek Si-on asked:

“You arranged this yourself?”

“Mm.”

“When did you learn arrangement?”

“Just now. Recently.”

She answered it the way you’d answer a question about the weather.

Baek Si-on: “?”

His step actually stuttered. Recently? The software had a lower barrier than it used to, admittedly, but nobody reads a manual over breakfast and walks out with a finished demo by dinner.

She put the phone away and looked ahead at the neon flickering down the street.

“I want to be a singer who writes. Consider this the first swing.”

And that’s when it occurred to him — the thought arriving with the weight of a decision already made: should I learn this too?

His head was full of songs. Some sharp. Some blurred. Some reduced to one chorus with no way to rebuild the structure around it. So far, every one of them had been extracted through Jung Jae-joon — direction from him, arrangement and production from Jae-joon. The partnership worked.

But what about the day he needed fragments landed fast, alone? Some 3 a.m. when a melody walks in and Jae-joon is on the other side of the ocean? Hum it into a voice memo and pray the morning keeps it?

Too passive. Structurally, fatally passive.

“Who taught you?”

She turned, surprised.

“You want to learn?”

“Mm.”

“Don’t you have a producer?”

She knew exactly what Jung Jae-joon was worth — the production on Human had been praised to the ceiling of the industry.

“Which is why I’m asking you.”

She processed that.

“Meaning what?”

“He overthinks.”

If Jung Jae-joon ever heard that Baek Si-on was studying arrangement, his first reaction would not be wonderful, you’re growing. It would be so you’ve decided I’m no longer enough — followed, possibly, by a heartbreak ballad written in the studio that same night.

Lee Ji-eun needed two seconds, and then she laughed — slid both hands into her flannel pockets and drawled:

“So you want to learn in secret.”

“Roughly.”

He didn’t deny it.

“And secretly have me help?”

“Asking whether you have a recommendation.”

She tipped her head. The cap brim tipped with it.

“Is this you asking for my help?”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

“I got you the Park Ji-eun audition.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

The smugness froze mid-smile. Again. Just how many more withdrawals was this man planning to make against the Writer Park Ji-eun, second female lead account? She ground her teeth and gave him a stare with genuine violence in it.

“Don’t push it!”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Then that’s a yes.”

“When did I say yes?!”

“You didn’t refuse.”

“I am refusing right now.”

“Too late.”

In the end, Lee Ji-eun surrendered. Not because the Park Ji-eun card was overpowered — though it was — but because somewhere in the last hundred meters she had noticed something: the fact of him wanting to learn arrangement at all had made her, quietly, absurdly pleased. It felt like walking a road alone for years and hearing footsteps fall in behind you — someone asking to walk the same direction, at the same pace, because your road was worth walking.

“I learned from Lee Jong-won.”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

“Your producer?”

“Mm.”

She gave him a sidelong glance.

“I can introduce you. How far do you want to take it?”

He thought about it.

“Far enough to land a melody as a demo, fast.”

She didn’t find the goal intimidating.

“That’s not too complicated.”

“Requirement: patience.”

Baek Si-on said:

“I am extremely patient.”

She looked at him.

“With things. Yes.”

“With people? No.”

Baek Si-on: “……”

He offered no defense, because by any honest audit the charge was roughly fair.

She pulled the phone back out and opened a screen for him. An arrangement workspace: tracks stacked in rows, a metronome, a library of virtual instruments, an effects rack. She tapped open a piano track and placed the notes for him one by one, unhurried, the teacher’s cadence already natural in her mouth.

Baek Si-on leaned in toward the screen.

“What’s this software called?”

“Logic.”

“It runs on a phone?”

“A cut-down version does. Real demos want a computer.”

He asked one question at a time; she answered in paragraphs. Neither of them noticed when their pace synchronized — stride for stride, pause for pause, even the angle at which they both bent toward the same six-inch screen, like two ends of one string being drawn through the crowd.

Ahead, Jung Han-teul registered that the world behind him had gone quiet. He turned around.

Lee Ji-eun was holding her phone up, walking Baek Si-on through an arrangement interface. Baek Si-on was bent slightly toward the screen. The neon off the shopfronts swept over the pair of them in bands — red, blue, warm yellow — layer after layer catching on a cap brim and a flannel shoulder.

If you didn’t know it was arrangement software, you would swear you had walked into the final thirty seconds of a romance web drama.

Jung Han-teul’s mouth twitched.

He wanted — viscerally, professionally — to take the photo and send it to her fansite with a caption of exactly four words of commentary:

draft post · never sent · sender intends to remain employed

Your eonni learned arrangement software today — to flirt.

He did not send it. He would like to keep working in this industry, and the first rule of surviving a top idol’s manager is knowing which photographs are landmines. He put the phone away and kept walking, a man carrying both a room key and national-level classified material.

Baek Eun-ah, noticing Jung Han-teul turn, turned too — and looked at the same scene with rapidly complicating feelings. On one hand: Lee Ji-eun was a guest, singing “Way Back Home,” now apparently discussing arrangement with her cousin — wonderful working chemistry, the professional read, the correct read.

On the other hand, as a bystander, she had to admit the picture was infuriatingly, catastrophically harmonious. Harmonious enough that she would need two separate PR contingency plans by morning: one for the fan-screaming when the full “Love Yourself” video dropped, and one for the atmospheric pressure event known as Choi Jin-ri eonni.

“This is fine,” she said, to nobody, in the voice of a woman filing paperwork against a hurricane.

December 3 · MAMA day 5:00 p.m. departure · no four-car convoy this time Hong Kong · AsiaWorld-Expo The organizers ran shuttles. The red carpet, whatever people say about MAMA’s prestige, has never once lacked for heat.

The brands arrived on schedule, again — but this time there was no Four Seasons corridor skirmish in premium ready-to-wear. Possibly Hong Kong hotel corridors are narrower. Possibly enough publicists had finally done the math: photographs of publicists body-checking each other in tomford overcoat territory are bad for the concept of luxury. Park Ji-hun resolved three brand teams in five minutes, with the cold efficiency of a treaty negotiator: one outfit for the carpet, one for the stage, one for sitting in the audience. Three looks, three territories, no war. Civilization. It suits everyone.

At five, Baek Si-on left the hotel in an organizer shuttle, and AsiaWorld-Expo was already drowning — fans and media packed wall to wall outside the carpet line. Big screens. The press pit. The interviewer’s platform. Flashbulbs came down like a bright white rain on every artist who walked out of a car.

He stepped out; the flashes went first, the screaming followed.

the carpet line · four languages, one name

“Baek Si-on!”

“Legend!”

“Oppa!”

“Here! Look here!”

Korean, Cantonese, Japanese, English — braided over each other, none waiting its turn. Southeast Asian fan sections hoisted lightboards in every color the LED spectrum offered.

He walked the line. Every few steps a bank of photographers would chant his name in unison; he would stop, turn, hold the frame, let the flashes detonate, and move again. Stop. Turn. Hold. Walk. The far end of the carpet held the sponsor wall and the signature board. He signed it.

The host came up smiling.

“Baek Si-on-ssi, welcome to MAMA.”

He took the microphone.

“Thank you.”

“Your first time in Hong Kong, and your first MAMA — how does it feel?”

“It’s an honor to attend an international awards ceremony in such a beautiful city.”

The carpet screamed louder, on cue. Several fans holding the Human cover art had whipped their slogan towels into outright fans.

The host tried once more:

“Tonight’s opening stage is ‘Human’ — anything you can tease?”

“Quieter than ‘King.’”

“…Quieter?”

“Mm.”

“Then somehow I think they’ll look forward to it even more.”

Baek Si-on handed back the microphone, which was its own press statement.

Inside, the lighting was already set. The main LED wall ran the countdown; the floor seats ran unbroken into the second-tier stands, and in the dark, ten thousand lightsticks glowed in their separate colors — a temporary city, assembled for one night, zoned by fandom.

In the backstage corridor the agencies were already out in force. People bowed as he passed. Somebody murmured sunbaenim. He nodded his way down the gauntlet and into the private dressing room the organizers had set aside. Park Ji-hun handed him the stage outfit; he changed; came out; Park Ji-hun looked him over once, squared the collar, pressed a shoulder seam flat.

“Good.”

Just like that. Baek Eun-ah ran the final checklist — in-ears, mic number, floor marks, lighting cues — and then there was nothing left to do but wait.

…

Seven o’clock, exactly. The main lights died.

A ripple went through the audience first — then the lightsticks lit up section by section, floor to upper bowl, each fandom a different colored sea: red, silver, blue, white, a floating city grid laid out in the dark.

The big screen woke. MAMA’s opening film: rapid-cut city nights — Seoul, Tokyo, Peking, Bangkok, Singapore, Hong Kong — then years of MAMA highlights compressed into seconds. Singers on rising platforms. Fireworks. Screaming. Carpets. Trophies. A narrator, in the most solemn voice paid narration can buy, explaining how Asian music had crossed languages, borders and oceans to arrive at the world stage.

The edit was shameless. Every frame said: this is not an ordinary awards show; this is the entire Asian music industry pasting gold on its own face tonight. Excessive. Also — infuriatingly — effective. The crowd ate it wholesale; every legacy clip detonated its own section of screaming.

Then the film slowed.

A black-and-white wasteland. Low clouds. Wind through dead grass. The imagery of the Human cover, unpacked frame by frame like film — and a figure standing far off in it, coat moving, back to camera. The camera pushed in.

The screaming started early — the fans knew this footage.

“Human!”

“Baek Si-on!”

“AAAAAAAH!”

The figure on the screen turned around. The camera found his face and kept pushing — closer, closer — and then, frame by frame, the archival grain gave way to live feed, and the face on the screen became the face standing in the middle of the stage, in the flesh, under one column of cold white light.

The first line landed and the director cut wide: Baek Si-on at center, the monochrome wasteland fading slowly behind him. No dancers. No throne. None of the machinery that signals big stage to the back rows. One vertical light and one microphone held like it was the last object in the country.

“Thinking I can see through this, and see what’s behind —”

The camera pushed in from the front — medium, then close, then face. MAMA’s director knew exactly how much that face could carry on an LED wall. High definition magnifies every grain of fatigue, and fatigue was precisely the right color for this song; the technology ended up doing the job of the lighting design.

In the floor seats, his fans started singing along. Scattered at first — a voice here, a voice there — then stitched together into one long cloth. Off-key. Accented. Some of them only knew the melody in front of the chorus. But that is how a live room works: someone opens the door first, and the rest of the crowd walks through it without deciding to.

“Got no way to prove it, so maybe I’m blind —”

As the line fell, warmth began leaking into the wasteland behind him — the monochrome thawing at its edges, degree by degree.

Across the floor, the industry sections had risen to their feet. Big-company boy groups clapped with the discipline of people who knew cameras were cycling; the girl-group rows had entirely lost custody of their expressions — more than one idol forgot the cutaway shot existed, eyes lit up like the lightsticks she was supposed to be representing.

The song ended the way it had been built: without fireworks. He lowered the microphone, gave the room one small bow — barely a tilt — and turned for the wings. The follow-spot walked him out. Only when the darkness took his back did the house lights begin to come up, and the applause was still landing on the stage as the host walked out into it, waiting several seconds with the mic up, grinning, for permission to speak.

“Thank you, Baek Si-on, for that opening performance!”

“Next — another wonderful stage!”

The lights reconfigured. The next act came out fast, with more dancers, fuller lighting, busier cutting — the director working hard for close-ups. And it was all fine. It was all completely fine.

Only the specific thing was gone — the thing where one person holds the breath of sixty thousand strangers at once, like a hand closing gently around a room.

For one moment, somebody had pushed the front door open and let the wind and the view pour in.

Now the door had swung shut again. Inside was warm, and loud, and bright.

But everyone inside had seen, just for a second, exactly what was outside.

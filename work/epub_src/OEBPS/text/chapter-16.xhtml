Chapter Sixteen

The SM Earthquake Hits a Rookie Trainee

June The SM Entertainment lobby The trainee contract, read aloud

Si-on reached out and took the document from Eun-ah’s hands. He opened it and ran his eyes over it. Training fees. Room and board. Costumes. Cosmetics. Medical costs. A dense itemised list, every line marked with a figure and a date. He looked at it a few seconds, closed it, and raised his eyes to the middle-aged man.

“SM sheds a fair number of trainees every year.”

“The legal gentleman” — the man from the company — nodded.

“Yes, we do.”

“So do you go after every one of them for the money?”

“No.”

“So why my cousin?”

The man from the company looked at him, then at the red-eyed Eun-ah standing beside him.

“Because your cousin didn’t terminate through the proper procedure. Her actions here constitute a unilateral breach.”

Si-on understood. Normally, once a big company ships a new group, it can’t wait to have the older trainees who didn’t make the cut clear out of their own accord, freeing up the dorms and the resources for the newcomers. But the problem was that Eun-ah hadn’t gone through the process. She’d run off in a fury. In ordinary times, that wouldn’t have been a big deal.

But in this window, with Kris’s contract dispute, the stock wobbling, and panic running through the house — any small movement got magnified. What SM’s top brass feared most right now was a runaway. So the thirty-two million wasn’t the point. The point was a signal. Take Eun-ah, the bird that flew straight into the muzzle, and make an example of her to every trainee.

“Understood.”

Si-on closed the document and looked at the man.

“We’ll pay this off within a month.”

“Cousin—”

Eun-ah whipped her head around, tears holding in her eyes. Si-on held up a hand to cut her off, keeping his gaze on the man.

“Agreed?”

The man gave Si-on an uncertain look. He’d expected this household to collapse into wailing and begging, like every other low-income family. He hadn’t expected someone this easy.

“Agreed. Here’s my contact — reach out in advance to arrange the payment.”

Si-on took the card, glanced at it, and tucked it in his pocket. The man nodded and turned down the stairs. The sound of his leather shoes on the steps, crisp, step by step, growing fainter, and finally gone beyond the unit door.

Eun-ah stood in place, and the tears she’d been holding back for so long finally broke.

“Cousin, I’m sorry… I didn’t know it would be like this… I can’t come up with that much money…”

“Inside.”

Eun-ah pulled out her key, hands shaking so badly she missed the lock three times. Si-on took the key from her, slid it in, turned, and pushed the door open. The apartment was empty.

He carried her suitcase in and set it by the door, then walked to the kitchen, opened the fridge, and took out two cans of Coca-Cola. He turned. Eun-ah was still at the doorway, head down, lips pressed, tears falling one by one onto the floor.

Si-on walked over and pressed a cold can against her cheek. She flinched at the chill.

“Alright.”

“A girl not yet twenty — you trained four years and got cut at the last second. You’re upset. You want out of the place that hurt you. That’s normal.”

“You didn’t do anything wrong.”

“What’s wrong is the company that treated you like a tool.”

Eun-ah’s mouth bent again. The tears fell harder.

“The money — your cousin will figure it out.”

He put the can on the shoe rack and reached out, ruffling her hair.

“Stop crying, or your eyes’ll swell up like walnuts.”

She sniffled, scrubbing her face with the back of her hand, but the tears wouldn’t stop. Si-on pulled a tissue from the coffee table and handed it over. She took it and blew her nose hard, red-rimmed eyes on him.

“Cousin, where are you getting thirty million won?”

“Figure it out.”

Thirty-two million won — a bit under two hundred thousand US dollars. For the foundation Baek Jeong-hwan had left, or a professor’s salary, it was nothing special. But for two young people with no real jobs to scrape together in a month, it was a mountain. He knew the legal tactics of a big company. This money wasn’t escapable.

“Figure out what?”

Eun-ah muttered, sullen.

“Are you gonna go back to your old trade and shake down loan sharks?”

Si-on turned it over in earnest. Between his discharge allowance of a little over ten million won — if he went to Old Choi’s operation and worked up, squeezing twenty-odd billion won of bad debt back — that penalty fee might just get covered. Watching her cousin’s face actually run the arithmetic, Eun-ah panicked.

“Cousin, you’re not actually considering it, are you? Auntie would kill you!”

Si-on pictured Yoon Hye-ja’s disappointed look. That was worse than death — genuinely worse. That kind of gaze was deadlier than any violence.

A beat of silence. Then Eun-ah suddenly proposed.

“Cousin, why don’t we go sing at a bar?”

He looked up.

“Look — I trained at SM four-plus years. I can sing, I can dance. You had real idol training too, you’ve got the foundation. The two of us singing? No problem at all!”

The more she talked, the more animated she got, eyes lighting up.

“A lot of bars in Hongdae have live sets. If we get lucky with a rich tip, we could pull in hundreds of thousands in one night! Scraping together thirty million in a month is nothing!”

Si-on turned it over. It sounded… actually pretty sound. But following that thread down, a sharper idea surfaced. Since they’d resolved to make money by singing — why not just take advantage of being a reincarnator and copy down a few future hit songs and release them outright? That beat busking for drunk patrons any day.

But the idea, the instant it came, was doused by cold water. The problem looped right back to money.

How much did it cost to produce a single commercial-quality song? Buying a beat, renting a studio, hiring an arranger, mixing, mastering — and that didn’t even count the promo and chart battles afterward. Ten million won tossed into a pit with no bottom gave you not even a ripple.

In this business, without money, you didn’t even qualify to be a plagiarist.

“Hah…”

Si-on turned to his cousin, who was gazing at him with expectant hope for a decision. His gaze slid off her face and down to the can of Coke in her hand.

Red packaging. White logo. A line of printed characters on it.

“2014 FIFA World Cup Brazil.”

Si-on stared at that design, and suddenly a chain of images flashed through his head. Germany 7-1 over Brazil. The Netherlands 5-1 over Spain. Costa Rica’s shock run to the last eight. Argentina and Germany meeting in the final.

Those scores, those outcomes, he remembered all of them. Not because he’d been a fan in his last life. But because that year’s World Cup had been so wild, every result hitting the trending feeds — it was impossible not to remember.

Si-on stared at the can, and the corner of his mouth slowly lifted.

What in the world earned money faster than this?!

Eun-ah, unnerved by that expression, felt a chill.

“Cousin, what’s so funny?”

“Eun-ah.”

“Huh?”

“Do you believe in luck?”

“What… what luck?”

“The lottery.”

“……the lottery?”

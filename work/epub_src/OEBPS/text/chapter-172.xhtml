Chapter One Hundred Seventy Two

Five Million Dollars, Lost to Steak and Coconut Water

December 22 · noon, forty minutes of it One address that needs no nameplate · a steak race with no rules and no trophy · one bottle that changes the afternoon · a signature earned at the grill Newport Coast · a Spanish-style estate — half-court on the left, pool on the right, grill in the middle A five-million-dollar cash offer dies quietly beside the grill, equity is signed for dessert, a small girl holds her audition for the Lakers’ future, and a manager learns to stitch his own wounds — then haggle for extra points.

Noon.

Newport Coast.

Once the car cleared central Los Angeles, the air visibly cleaned itself up.

No exhaust. No cascading horns. Only the sea wind off the Pacific, carrying salt and the cool green smell of palm trees.

The houses along the road grew bigger, the gaps between them wider, the walls around them higher.

Nobody out here needed a nameplate at the gate. The people who lived here were the nameplates.

The car stopped in front of a Spanish-style estate.

Baek Eun-ah looked out the window.

“This is Kobe’s house?”

Scooter nodded.

“One of them.”

“……One of them.”

Baek Eun-ah elected not to follow up on NBA-star real-estate counts.

Knowing too much about that kind of thing was bad for an employee’s mental health.

The gate was already open.

They walked in together.

The front yard was enormous.

The lawn looked mowed to a measured line, white roses stood open in the beds, and the fountain ran with a very quiet water sound.

Around the side of the main house, along the covered walk — and the backyard opened up all at once.

Half a basketball court on the left.

Pool on the right.

Grill station in the middle.

Kobe stood at the grill in gray gym shorts and an ordinary white tank top.

Under the half-court hoop, a small girl was dribbling a basketball, working on her handles.

Natalia, the elder daughter, sat in the shade of the walk with a book open on her lap, looking up now and then to check on her sister.

Vanessa was setting the long outdoor table; hearing them, she turned and waved.

Kobe looked up, saw Baek Si-on, and split into a full row of very white teeth.

“Baek!”

He set the tongs aside, came over, and hugged him.

“So you finally remembered Los Angeles has friends.”

Baek Si-on said:

“You only called today.”

Kobe laughed even harder.

“So the problem is yours. You should’ve told me sooner.”

Just then the small girl from the court came running up, hugging the basketball.

“Baek!”

Her eyes were all shine.

“I listened to your ‘Legend’!”

Baek Si-on crouched down to her level.

“And? Did you like it?”

“Loved it! When I shoot, the drums from that song are all inside my head!”

She demonstrated the chorus rhythm, dribbling and humming at the same time.

‘Legend’ · the hook, as hummed courtside

“Bang bang, bang bang—”

“And did your shooting percentage go up?”

The small girl — Gi-Gi — thought about it and shook her head, entirely honest.

“Not yet.”

Kobe burst out laughing beside them.

“This kid plays your song on her morning runs. Every single morning.”

“Sometimes she makes me run with her.”

He glanced at Baek Si-on.

“You know what it’s like — a thirty-six-year-old man still training like his life depends on it, woken up by his daughter playing your song. It’s a strange feeling.”

Baek Si-on said:

“It means your household has good taste in music.”

Kobe pointed at him.

“I like that sentence.”

He pulled Baek Si-on toward the shaded walk.

“Come. Introductions.”

“My wife — Vanessa.”

Vanessa put out her hand, smiling.

“Welcome, Baek.”

“Thank you for having me.”

“And Natalia.”

Natalia, much the quieter of the two, stood up to greet him.

“Hello.”

“Hello.”

Gi-Gi, hugging her ball, could not help cutting in:

“Can I show you my shooting later?”

Kobe clapped a hand on her shoulder at once.

“Food first. Athletes eat.”

Gi-Gi muttered:

“You say that every day.”

Kobe looked down at her.

“Because you don’t listen every day.”

“……”

Introductions done, lunch landed on the table fast.

Steak.

Grilled vegetables.

Salad.

Bread.

And a small dish of Korean kimchi, prepared specially.

Baek Si-on looked at the kimchi dish.

Kobe said at once:

“I had someone get it. Figured you might not be used to everything here.”

“Thank you.”

Kobe sat down and picked up his knife and fork.

“You have forty minutes, right?”

“Right.”

“Then let’s not waste them.”

He cut a proper slab of steak and sent it into his mouth.

Baek Si-on started eating too.

He had always eaten fast.

In Korea, Baek Eun-ah had long since gotten used to watching him finish a meal inside the gap between two schedule items.

Kobe, clearly, was not used to it.

At first he ate at a perfectly normal pace.

Then, mid-bite, the corner of his eye caught the state of Baek Si-on’s plate: half gone already.

Kobe’s motion hitched.

And then his cutting speed abruptly increased.

Baek Si-on looked up at him.

Kobe’s face did not change.

Baek Si-on kept eating.

Kobe kept eating too.

One by one, the rest of the table noticed that something had gone wrong.

Vanessa watched the two of them with an expression of majestic weariness.

Natalia held a laugh behind her napkin. Gi-Gi did not bother holding anything.

“Daddy, are you two competing?”

Kobe swallowed what was in his mouth and denied it instantly.

“No.”

Baek Si-on said:

“He is.”

Kobe glared at him.

“I’m not.”

Baek Si-on looked down and cut his next piece.

“You accelerated.”

“……”

Sometimes men are exactly this ridiculous.

No audience required.

No rules required, either.

The moment one man’s eating speed jumps, the other man automatically files it as a challenge.

❧ ❧ ❧

Three steaks later, Kobe finally put down his knife and fork.

He leaned back in his chair, expression complicated.

He had not lost.

Above all, he would never admit to losing.

“Do you always eat this much?”

Baek Si-on shook his head:

“You grilled this one yourself. That’s why.”

Scooter, beside them, put on a sudden, strange look.

Why did that sentence sound so familiar?

Kobe grinned a full row of white teeth, his mood visibly climbing.

He tipped his chin at Vanessa.

Vanessa, who clearly knew exactly what he wanted, nodded, rose, and went inside.

Before long she came back out with several chilled bottles.

Kobe took one and handed it over.

“Try this. Cuts the richness.”

Baek Si-on took the bottle and looked at it.

the bottle · handed over with expectations attached

BodyArmor

Coconut water base. High potassium, low sodium. No artificial colors, no artificial flavors.

A brand Baek Si-on has never heard of — yet.

He did not recognize the brand at all.

He twisted the cap and took a sip.

First impression: nothing like the syrup-in-water sports drinks on the market, the kind that leave a sticky sweet film on your tongue.

Clean. Restrained on sugar.

And at the very end, a faint coconut note.

“Well? How is it?”

“Good.”

That was the sentence Kobe had been waiting for.

“I knew you’d like it.”

He picked up his own bottle and swished it.

“The base is ten percent natural coconut water, with fruit-and-vegetable extracts and cane sugar. High potassium, low sodium.”

“No artificial colors. No artificial flavors.”

Baek Si-on turned and looked at Scooter.

Scooter had just drunk a mouthful of his own.

He met Baek Si-on’s eyes and gave a shrug one centimeter wide.

The meaning was clear.

Let him finish.

Sure enough, Kobe arrived at the point quickly.

“I’m an investor in this brand.”

He said it quite plainly.

“My goal is to kick Gatorade off its throne.”

Scooter’s fingers stopped on the bottle.

Here it comes.

He had known Kobe could not have summoned anyone for a steak and nothing else.

Kobe kept going:

“I heard from Steph that you signed with Under Armour.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“And what is Under Armour’s brand story? Challenging Nike’s dominance.”

Kobe leaned forward an inch.

“So basically, we are the same kind of people.”

“Not quite.”

Baek Si-on shook his head:

“I’m a spokesman. You’re a shareholder.”

Kobe laughed.

“Which is exactly why I’m not planning to pay you an endorsement fee.”

“I want you to take equity in the form of an endorsement. If the brand rises, the return will be bigger than any contract I could hand you.”

Scooter’s heart dropped an octave.

What was BodyArmor’s actual size?

A sports-drink startup that could not fill a full shelf run across California.

National market share: somewhere under one percent.

And Pepsi, on the other side?

Five million a year to start. In cash. The wired-into-your-account kind.

Plus Pepsi could sponsor the drink supply and offline promotion for Baek Si-on’s entire North American tour.

the choice, as the manager’s blood pressure saw it

Pepsi North America — annual, cash, wired$5,000,000, starting
Pepsi tour sponsorship & retail promotionbundled, ready to sign
BodyArmor equity — a brand under 1% of the marketno fee. equity only

Placed side by side, any normal person knew which one to pick.

Scooter began signaling with his eyes, furiously.

Left eye, one blink: do not say yes.

Right eye, one blink: Pepsi. Five million.

Both eyes at once: I am begging you.

Kobe read Scooter’s expression, and read Baek Si-on’s non-answer, and set his own bottle down on the table.

“A while back, Alibaba went public. I made a good piece of money on that.”

“You can trust my eye.”

How much Kobe had made off the man from Hangzhou, Baek Si-on did not know.

But the bottle in his hand — that, he had genuinely never heard of.

In the future memory he relied on, BodyArmor had never made the kind of Asian-market noise that Gatorade, Red Bull, or Monster had.

Maybe it did fine in America eventually.

Maybe Gatorade squeezed it into the corner of the shelf within a season or two.

He didn’t know.

And not knowing meant this was one choice memory could not cheat on.

Then a thought arrived.

The film people he’d loved in his last life had a way of producing one baffling disaster every few years.

He used to assume they needed the money.

Now he wondered — maybe they knew the script was rotten.

It was just that some lunch had been eaten.

Some favor had been banked.

Some elder of the craft had asked.

And even the clearest head leaves a small irrational fingerprint on some choice or other.

Of course, endorsement-for-equity was not the same as starring in a rotten film.

It would not eat six months of his life, and it would not dirty a résumé.

Worst case, it was one bad business call.

That was a price he could pay.

Besides — the man doing the asking was Kobe.

Baek Si-on looked up.

“Okay.”

At that, Scooter’s eyes stopped blinking altogether and simply closed.

Wonderful.

A five-million-dollar Pepsi cash contract, defeated by steak and coconut water.

Then, turning it over, he suddenly found one thing to be grateful for.

Thank God Baek Si-on had never built a personal friendship with Taylor.

Otherwise, with this man’s “a friend asked, so it’s done” reflex, the thing Scooter feared most was guaranteed to happen one day.

Kobe, for his part, had frozen for a second too.

He had backups prepared.

For instance: distribution was about to expand.

For instance: they were already in talks with James Harden.

For instance: the sports-drink market was shifting, and young consumers no longer reached for Gatorade on pure reflex.

But before any of it left his mouth, Baek Si-on had already nodded.

“Baek. I did not misread you.”

Kobe put out a fist.

Baek Si-on raised his hand and bumped it.

“Any more steak?”

Kobe froze for a full second.

Then he burst out laughing.

“Didn’t you just eat three pieces?”

“That was lunch.”

“And this is?”

“Pre-signing product evaluation.”

Kobe laughed even louder.

He stood up and picked the tongs back up.

“Good.”

“Today we evaluate until you’re satisfied.”

❧ ❧ ❧

With the last steak finished, Baek Si-on finally set the knife down.

Kobe studied the empty plate with an expression that could not make up its mind.

“You’re sure you’re not some pro athlete?”

Baek Si-on wiped his mouth with the napkin.

“I just respect food.”

Kobe turned toward Vanessa.

“Hear that? THAT is how a guest behaves.”

“……”

When they left, the whole family walked them to the gate.

Gi-Gi hugged her basketball and asked, unwilling to let go of the moment:

“Will you come again?”

“I will.”

“Then next time will you watch me shoot?”

“Done.”

Kobe cut in instantly:

“Don’t over-praise her. She already thinks she can make the Lakers’ starting five.”

Gi-Gi looked up, indignant:

“I already can.”

Kobe looked down at his daughter, the pride on his face entirely unhidden.

“See that? A Bryant kid.”

Baek Si-on looked at the ball in Gi-Gi’s arms.

“Next time, I’ll bring you a song built for shooting practice.”

Gi-Gi’s eyes lit up all at once.

Kobe went on instant alert.

“Nothing too hyped.”

“Why?”

“She’s loud enough already.”

Gi-Gi puffed her cheeks; Vanessa shook her head, laughing.

Goodbyes done, Baek Si-on got in the car.

The door shut the ocean wind outside.

The car pulled slowly off Newport Coast.

Up front, Scooter finally let out everything he had been holding the whole way.

“The exact equity number — I’ll negotiate it for you from here.”

“All you have to do, once it’s settled, is sign, shake hands, take the photo, and stand there smiling.”

Baek Si-on leaned into the back seat and watched the road ahead.

“I thought you’d be upset.”

Scooter flipped the sun visor down against the light.

“You said yes so fast. Kobe is moved. I am wounded.”

“But it is what it is. I’ll stitch the wound closed, then go get you a few more points.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“And Pepsi?”

This time Scooter sounded almost relaxed.

“What Pepsi fears isn’t you saying no. It’s you signing with Coca-Cola.”

“Think about it. You and Taylor are killing each other on the Billboard chart right now. If the two of you showed up in the same Coke ad, Pepsi’s entire marketing department would resign in a body.”

“But you picked BodyArmor — you went after Gatorade, Pepsi’s own turf, the sports-drink aisle. Honestly? Pepsi is probably delighted.”

Baek Si-on heard him out, then asked:

“You’re that bearish on the brand?”

Scooter turned around, wearing the fond look of a manager confronted with his artist’s naive business instincts.

“Baek.”

“Gatorade is not a bottle of drink.”

“It’s locker rooms, TV commercials, supermarket shelves, college sports, pro leagues, vending machines — and the color a whole generation of Americans reaches for without thinking, the second practice ends.”

“The brand you drank today still can’t get shelf space in half the convenience stores in America.”

“So I’ll squeeze out the best number I can for you.”

“If it rises someday, you’ll make a lot.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

Scooter was quiet for a moment.

“Then you’ll at least come out of it with a valuable friendship — and five steaks from today’s lunch.”

Chapter Thirty-Four

Acting Is Design, Singing Is Instinct

June 28 Evening Hapjeong-dong, the booth The narrative takes

Baek Si-on walked into the booth for the third time.

This time was different. No ding-dong, no tick-tack, none of the pretty little traps that laid his voice bare. What was left on the lyric sheet was all narrative.

The track came on in his headphones.

He didn't rush in. He waited two beats.

And on the off-beat of the third bar, he opened his mouth.

“The sensor door at two a.m.——”

The sliced Ding-dong popped out of the arrangement to catch it.

“Spitting out a receipt printed with snacks——”

The Tick-tack fragments dropped in between the lines, no longer fighting his voice for the space.

“The street lamp drags my shadow long — long as a joke.”

At this line, something entered Si-on's voice that wasn't there before.

Not technique. Technique he had; the original member's foundation was enough to set every note cleanly where it belonged. But what showed up on this line had nothing to do with technique.

It was picture.

He was an actor. He didn't need flashy runs to say “this person is lonely.” He only needed to stand in front of a microphone, remember the street lamp at two in the morning, remember his own shadow stretched long across the pavement, and then say the line in the flattest voice that would carry it.

“Another day, is it — with no terminal station?”

That one dropped at the tail. A very tired person asking a question no one was going to answer; the voice went light on its own at the end.

Outside the glass, Jae-joon's fingers left the mouse. He leaned back, laced his hands behind his head, and watched the waveform jump on the monitor.

“The evening wind rolls in——”

The slit hoo sat behind it, the tail of a wind.

“It's summer — so why are my fingertips still cold?”

He sang this one twice. The first time it sounded good, but good wasn't the same as right. The second time, he called up the instant he'd stepped out of his uncle's studio: the end of June, the sun just set, standing at the curb waiting for a car, the wind coming in and a row of goosebumps rising across the back of his hand.

Jae-joon hit save.

“No matter how far I walk——”

His voice changed here. Through the verse he'd held it in, but at the chorus he let it move outward — up from the throat, into the chest, into the shoulders, finally spreading the range he was born with. The warm base color never left, but the resonance opened its doors.

“This city's neon——”

“They're all smiling at me asking: hey, where are you going?”

“I've turned out every pocket — can't find the key called “home”——”

Jae-joon pushed the volume up a notch.

“It's fine — I'll keep walking. Before the world turns upside down, before the street lamps go out——”

“Take the wandering as my Way Back Home.”

The booth went quiet for a few seconds. Jae-joon blew the last waveform up and watched the crest of its amplitude.

Si-on pushed the door open and came out.

“How was it?”

“Chorus passes.”

Jae-joon held up two fingers.

“But verse two needs to be redone. The line “saying goodnight to a stray cat by the road” — you sang it too well.”

Si-on's brow moved.

“Too well?”

“Means you were performing.”

Jae-joon sat forward.

“The picture in that line is a person crouched by the roadside talking to a stray cat. That person is so tired they've started saying goodnight to cats. They wouldn't have any leftover feeling to spend making the line beautiful. What you just did — the voice was too pretty, the breath too even.”

That assessment was interesting.

A moment ago, when they'd cut the onomatopoeia, the problem had been that Si-on's voice wasn't “light enough.” Now, on the narrative lines, the problem had flipped to his singing “too well.” The first was a limit of his natural timbre. The second was a professional habit getting in the way.

He'd acted for more than a decade. In front of a camera, every breath, every pause, every rise and fall of the voice was designed. That ability to shape a performance was his weapon — but in front of a microphone it could cut too clean.

Si-on nodded. He turned and went back in, stood at the mic again.

One pass.

“……”

“Boss Baek.”

“Mm.”

“Your throat's in better condition than I expected. Pitch needs no fixing, breath's steady, timbre's recognisable. That's above average for an idol.”

In the booth Si-on didn't answer, waiting for him to finish.

“But the most interesting thing about you isn't your voice.”

Jae-joon swung the chair around to face the acrylic window where Si-on stood.

“Other people sing melody. You sing pictures. You know what temperature each line should be, where to drop quiet, where to leave it a little rough. That's worth more than two extra octaves.”

“But your problem is right there, too. You design everything. Some places, if you could forget for a second that you're an actor and just let the voice do the talking, what comes out will be more right.”

Si-on took his headphones off and hung them on the mic stand. He stepped out of the booth and picked up the iced americano off the desk, took a mouthful.

“Continue?”

“Continue. Two more runs of the chorus. I want material to stack into a harmony track.”

Si-on set the cup back down, turned, and went back into the booth.

June 28 11:00 p.m. Hapjeong-dong, the studio Five and a half hours of a song

It went on and on, until eleven at night.

One song, three minutes forty seconds, five and a half hours of recording. Every line sung over and over, no count on how many times. Some cleared in three takes; others took a dozen before Jae-joon nodded.

Somewhere in the middle, Si-on caught a pattern. The more he set his jaw and “sang it properly,” the more retakes it took. The more he loosened, stopped thinking so hard, let go — the faster a take passed.

Way Back Home · vocal trackingHapjeong-dong · 28 Jun · 18:00 → 23:10

Take 04 — Verse 1 · KEEP

Take 07 — Pre-Chorus · KEEP

Take 09 — Chorus · KEEP

Take 11 — Verse 2 · RETAKE (too pretty)

Take 14 — Verse 2 · KEEP

Rule the session found: the harder he tries, the more retakes. The looser he gets, the faster it passes. Acting is design — singing is instinct.

When he came out of the booth for the last time, his throat was raw, smoking.

Jae-joon organised the tracks on the computer, marking which were usable, which were backups, which were slicing material. A dozen-odd tracks lined up on the timeline, a mess of green and orange and blue.

“That's enough material.”

Jae-joon saved everything and closed the software.

“Mixing and mastering, I'll do myself. Three or four days.”

Si-on nodded.

“Thanks.”

“You're the one who did the work.”

Jae-joon picked a cigarette off the desk and stuck it between his teeth.

“I just pressed a few keys.”

Si-on knew that was a courtesy. For five and a half hours, Jae-joon's focus hadn't wandered for a second. Twice, while Si-on sang, he'd looked through the acrylic and seen the man outside with his eyes closed, tapping the rhythm against the desk, his lips moving soundlessly with the melody.

That wasn't “pressing a few keys.”

“Let me know when the final's done.”

Si-on picked up his phone and headed for the door.

“Oh — Boss Baek.”

He turned.

Jae-joon had the cigarette in place, the lighter raised halfway, and stopped.

“The feature idea — you really won't consider it?”

Si-on stood in the doorway. The motion-sensor light in the stairwell flared as he pushed the door, white light at his back, throwing his shadow across the studio floor.

“Let's talk about it later.”

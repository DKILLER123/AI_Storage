Chapter Thirty-Three

Cutting the Voice Into Pieces, Reassembling It Into Breath

June 28 2:40 p.m. Baek Jeong-hun's studio The export and the submission

June twenty-eighth, two-forty in the afternoon.

Baek Jeong-hun locked the last cut point on the timeline and exported the file.

The progress bar crawled for fourteen minutes, and he stood in front of the computer the whole time. He didn't sit. Not out of some ritual — purely because he was afraid that if he sat down, he'd never manage to stand back up again.

The export finished.

One hundred seventeen minutes, thirty-eight seconds. Four point seven gigabytes.

He double-clicked it and watched the opening from the top.

The screen went black.

No music. Only a man's muffled cursing, and the dull thud of something hitting a wall.

Then the crack of a door.

A thin sliver of light leaking through the gap, the camera dropped low, down at the eye level of young Sang-hun. On the other side of that crack, a man's fist rising and falling. A slipper lying upside down on the floor. Then a pair of little girl's feet running into the frame from the right.

Baek Jeong-hun closed the player.

Enough.

He knew the rest better than anyone. The sister falls, the blood running toward the crack between the floorboards, Sang-hun lifting her and running out, the mother chasing him past the mouth of the alley, the sound of brakes, and then a collision so loud it swallowed the brakes whole. The white light of a hospital corridor. The flat and final line of a heart monitor.

All of it was in his head, frame for frame.

He didn't need to watch it again. He'd only start wanting to change things.

A rough cut was a rough cut, not a final cut. What Venice's first round wanted was the skeleton of the story and the director's vision; grading and sound design came later.

Jeong-hun opened his mail, found the exchange with the Venice selection committee from three days earlier, and pulled up the online submission page. Filled the form. Director's name, running time, genre, synopsis. He deleted and rewrote the synopsis twice, then settled on two lines of Korean, and translated them himself into English. Sent.

A little bar crawled along again.

Upload complete.

He stared at the words “Submission Received” on the screen, and lifted both hands off the keyboard, clenched them, and let them go.

Not excitement.

Just that his hands needed to do something, or he wasn't sure where to put them.

He looked over at the sofa. Si-on was lying along it on his side, one hand tucked under his head, the blanket Eun-ah had brought last time pulled up to his chest, breathing shallow and dead asleep. He couldn't say when he'd fallen asleep. At one o'clock he'd still poured his uncle a glass of water.

Jeong-hun didn't wake him. He stood, pushed the swivel chair gently back under the desk, and by the door he softened his steps, turning the handle slowly.

The door shut. Footsteps faded down the hall.

✿ ✿ ✿

June 28 5:10 p.m. Baek Jeong-hun's studio A screen that said Submission Received

When Si-on opened his eyes, he had no idea what time it was.

The room was dark. The curtains were still the way they'd been left, sealed shut. He blinked twice, waited for his pupils to adjust, and turned his head.

Nobody at the cutting desk. Both monitors black, the standby lights one red one green, blinking their small warnings in the dim.

“Uncle?”

No answer.

The air conditioner's compressor hummed once, as if that were his uncle's way of replying.

He pushed the blanket off, sat up, and twisted his neck to the right. Two cracks. The sofa arm was too tall, and sleeping on his own arm had left his left hand half asleep; he shook it out, stood, and went to the bathroom first. The light hit him and made him squint again. When he washed his face he looked at himself in the mirror: a red line creased into his cheek from the cushion, running from his cheekbone down to his ear.

He came back out to the cutting desk and nudged the mouse.

The screen came up on the Venice film festival online submission page, and dead center sat a line in English:

Submission Received — Thank you for your entry.

Under it, a timestamp.

June 28, 14:59.

He checked the clock in the corner again: 17:10.

Still early.

He dialled Jae-joon's number.

One ring. Picked up.

Baek Si-on → Jung Jae-joon · early evening · Hapjeong-dong

“Boss Baek.”

“This a good time to come record?”

The creak of a chair rolling over floor, then two keystrokes.

“Now?”

“Now.”

“Come.”

Si-on hung up. At the door he turned the AC off, killed the lights, and locked it all.

Out of the building, the light hit him so much brighter it was almost a physical thing. Late June, the sun had swung past noon and off to the west, but the heat hadn't loosened its grip — the asphalt still breathed its heat back up at him.

He flagged a car at the curb.

“Hapjeong-dong.”

June 28 Evening Hapjeong-dong, room 401 The live room, and the lyric sheet

Hapjeong-dong, 401.

When Si-on knocked, speakers were playing something inside. The door opened.

Jae-joon stood in the doorway and jerked his chin toward the live room.

“Pushed the arrangement out a version. Listen first.”

Si-on walked in. Jae-joon dropped back into the swivel chair and clicked a few things. Sound came out of the monitors: the synthesizer pad laying down first, carrying that granular texture they'd tuned last time, then the drum machine, then the bass. Si-on stood in front of the speakers and listened for about forty seconds. Jae-joon hit pause.

“Right direction?”

“Right. But the kick could be duller.”

Jae-joon spun back, tweaked a parameter. Played it again. The edge of the kick blurred, as if someone had thrown a thin cloth over it.

“That?”

“That.”

Jae-joon saved it, then swung the chair around:

“All right. Booth. First, run the whole thing once with the words. Don't care if it's good or not — I want to hear your chemistry with the lyric.”

Si-on picked up the printed lyric sheet off the desk and read it. He'd been over those lines so many times by now he could say them blind. But on A4 paper they felt different than on a phone screen — more real, somehow. Like they'd stopped being an idea and become a thing.

He walked into the live room, up to the microphone, and put the headphones on. The backing track flowed in, the synth pad filling his head top to bottom.

Jae-joon's voice came through the monitor:

“Start whenever you're ready.”

Si-on closed his eyes. Opened them.

The intro ran four bars, and the verse entrance arrived. He opened his mouth.

“The sensor door at two a.m., Ding-dong, Ding-dong—”

The instant the first line left him, he knew it was wrong.

Not off-key. The pitch was actually fine — at least within the range he could control. It was the two Ding-dongs. Out of his throat, those two syllables were neither quick nor clever, and certainly not “loneliness wrapped in cuteness.” They sounded like a father reading a picture book to his kid — and a father doing it grudgingly.

He pushed on anyway.

“Spitting out a receipt printed with snacks, Tick-tack, Tick-tack—”

Worse. The low end of his voice pressed those two syllables down into something like a pendulum striking a coffin lid. Pre-chorus.

“The evening wind rolls in, Hoo-hoo—”

Out in the control room, Jae-joon's fingers rested on the mouse, and the look on his face was complicated.

“……”

The last note fell and the booth went quiet for a few seconds.

Si-on pulled the headphones off and came out. Jae-joon leaned back in his chair, saying nothing. Si-on didn't ask “how was it.” The playback in his own headphones had already given him the answer.

“Cut all the reduplication.”

Jae-joon spoke first:

“Like I said, vocal slicing. You track the onomatopoeia on its own — every sound three times — and I'll cut it up afterward.”

Si-on nodded and turned back toward the booth.

This time he wasn't singing the whole song. Just standing at the mic, word by word, saying them.

“Ding-dong.”

“Again.”

“Ding-dong.”

“Again. Lighter. More breath through it.”

“……Ding-dong.”

“Tick-tack.”

“Hoo—”

“Shorter. Hoo. Not hoo—.”

“Hoo.”

“Round and round.”

“……”

That went on for about twenty minutes. Dreary as an assembly line. Three takes of each word, some went to five or six, and as long as Jae-joon didn't call stop, he kept going.

When he was done, Jae-joon had him come out, put his own headphones on, and started working at the computer. Si-on stood watching for a moment. On the screen, his isolated words were being lifted out one at a time, the waveforms blown up, precisely boxed and clipped with the mouse.

A “Ding” got cut in half. The consonant D at the front kept, the vowel at the back stretched, pitch-shifted, and stacked with a layer of reverb. Then it was joined with a “dong” from another track and wedged between beat three and beat four of the arrangement.

When it came out of the speakers, what Si-on heard was no longer a man clumsily saying ding-dong. It was a fragment of voice set inside the electronic texture.

“That's vocal slicing.”

Jae-joon took the headphones off.

“Your raw material, through my hands, becomes a part in the arrangement.”

Si-on listened to it twice.

“Good. The reduplication gets handled that way.”

Chapter Six

One Million Won Outside a Barbecue Joint

Anyang · evening A barbecue joint on the main street The last day of the standing shift

The barbecue joint sat on a narrow shopping street in Anyang, wedged between a phone-screen protector shop and a convenience store. It was past seven in the evening, the dinner rush in full swing, every table packed.

Grease smoke poured out of the exhaust fan, mingled with the charred smell of pork belly and the sting of garlic. The whole street smelled like it.

Baek Si-on pushed the door open.

The noise hit him all at once — voices, the sizzle of fat on a hot grill, a table somewhere shouting a drinking game. He scanned the room.

Kim Se-jeong was at the farthest table, clearing dishes, apron askew across her waist, sweat on her forehead, bangs plastered to her skin.

Si-on didn’t sit. He stayed by the door and waved her over.

When Se-jeong looked up and saw him, the colour left her face.

“Come out for a second.”

“I’m not off yet.”

“You are now.”

Se-jeong dumped the last stack of dishes into the bin, yanked her apron off, and flung it onto the counter, then walked over with her jaw set.

The chef stuck his head out from the kitchen, about to call her back.

Si-on turned and looked at him.

The chef pulled his head back in again.

Businessmen, they have a sense for it.

✿ ✿ ✿
Outside the barbecue joint The shopping street Neon light and two long shadows

Customers drifted past. The neon sign of the screen-protector shop flickered on and off, stretching the two of them into shadows that grew and shrank.

“What? Say it.”

Se-jeong crossed her arms, chin lifted.

Si-on reached into his pocket and pulled out a wad of cash. Counted it. Eight hundred thousand won. Then he pulled another two hundred thousand out of his other pocket, and together it was one million won, exactly.

He held it out.

Se-jeong looked down at the money and didn’t take it.

“What is this?”

“The account is settled. The contract’s shredded too. Nobody’s coming for you again.”

Si-on pushed the money into her hands.

“This million is your food money.”

Se-jeong’s head spun. Sixty-plus million won of debt, just… gone? And then this, another million?

She’d fed him kimchi-bap for days, and it had cost her, at most, thirty thousand. What kind of food money was a million?

Si-on didn’t give her time to process it. He shoved his hands back in his pockets and turned to go. Two steps, then he stopped and glanced back over his shoulder, one side of his face lit by the neon, the other in shadow.

“One more thing.”

“Your idol is worth chasing.”

Se-jeong’s expression froze. Two days ago this man had been sprawled on her sofa, telling her that chasing a star was the luxury of a slow suicide. Suddenly he’d changed his tune?

Before she could make sense of it, Si-on had set off walking. Han-teuk darted out from behind a utility pole a little way down the street and jogged after him.

“Hey!”

Si-on didn’t turn, but he slowed.

“What’s with the attitude?!”

Se-jeong clutched the money and shouted at his retreating back.

“When I debut, even if you beg I’m not giving you an autograph!”

Far away, that back seemed to pause for a beat. Then a hand came up and waved, once, and he kept walking.

The streetlamp at the alley mouth dragged his shadow long across the ground, all the way to Se-jeong’s feet.

“Waiter!”

Someone called from inside the shop.

She wiped her face, tucked the money away, and turned back inside.

“Coming, coming —”

The charcoal was still burning. The meat was still grilling. The days had to keep going.

But they didn’t feel quite as heavy as before.

✿ ✿ ✿

Han-teuk and Si-on walked back along the shopping street. The neon fell away behind them. Ahead, the road narrowed, and a row of tilted electric scooters stood parked on either side.

Si-on walked a few steps, then spoke.

“You got a girlfriend?”

Han-teuk’s steps faltered, just slightly. He glanced reflexively back toward the barbecue joint, then at Si-on, and in three seconds his brain ran the whole calculation.

“N-no.”

His voice came out a little thin.

“Perfect.”

Si-on clapped him on the shoulder.

Han-teuk’s heart rate climbed.

“Drive me somewhere.”

“…”

Han-teuk stood there, and his expression moved through anticipation, confusion, disappointment, and relief, before settling into the dead-eyed look of a man who had seen it all before.

So asking if I had a girlfriend was just to make sure I had no plans tonight and could be a free driver?

“Where?”

“Mapo.”

“That’s a ways…”

“I’ll buy you barbecue.”

Han-teuk thought about it. Today he’d been choked, dragged half across Seoul, walked into a loan-shark office, watched a contract get shredded, and then stood behind a utility pole for twenty minutes outside a barbecue joint.

One meal did not seem unreasonable.

“Alright.”

✿ ✿ ✿

Han-teuk’s car was a silver Hyundai Elantra with who-knows how many kilometres on it, the back seat piled with unopened boxes of albums and rolled-up posters. The standard kit of a junior agent.

Anyang to Mapo, about forty minutes on the expressway.

The rush hour had just ended. Traffic was light, and the streetlights ticked past one after another. Han-teuk said nothing, driving with focus.

Si-on sat in the passenger seat, his gaze drifting over the scenery sweeping past, not landing on anything in particular.

An image surfaced in his mind.

A Recollection · the funeral home

The boy with his hand on a fruit knife, trembling.

His mother wrapping her arms around him from behind, sobbing, telling him to let it go.

And then the knife falling.

It hit the floor of the mourning hall with a sound that was small and somehow far too loud. Old Choi hadn’t even lifted his eyes.

The boy’s hand was shaking. The younger Si-on noticed it, the way you notice rain starting.

Si-on cracked the window a finger’s width. The wind came in, a little cold.

The script is right. The character is right. The theme — violence handed down, generation to generation — is right. But there’s something off about it. I need to see my uncle.

✿ ✿ ✿

Forty minutes later, Yeonnam-dong, Mapo. Baek Jeong-hun’s studio was on the second floor of an old residential building, with a stationary shop (closed) on the ground floor.

Pushing the door open, a wave of cigarette smoke met him.

Studio · Yeonnam-dong · pre-production

The room wasn’t big. One large table took up half the floor, scattered with storyboard drafts, scene sketches, and stickies in every colour.

The wall was pinned with a full character-relationship chart, red and blue lines crisscrossing like a spider web.

Ashtrayseven or eight butts
Coffee cupgone dry

Baek Jeong-hun sat behind it all, pencil clenched in his teeth, frowning at the storyboard.

He looked up at the sound.

“Si-on? What brings you here?”

Si-on strode across the room and planted both hands on the desk, leaning down over it, staring at his uncle from above.

“Samchon. The script has to change.”

Baek Jeong-hun’s back pressed into his chair. Not out of fear. Instinct. The posture, the tone, the way the pressure came down from above —

for one dizzying second, it was like being a schoolboy again, standing in front of his brother’s desk after a failed exam. That old fear of being owned by an older brother, the fear he thought he’d long buried.

It hadn’t been buried at all. Some things get carved into your bones.

“Ch-change what?”

“The era.”

“The era?”

“It’s 2014 now. Collection-by-brutality gets you arrested. I tested it.”

Si-on ran through his few days at the collection agency at speed. The eat-them-alive school. The be-a-pest school. The humiliate-them-into-bankruptcy school.

Nobody touches you. They all use soft knives now.

“Your script has Sang-hun beating a man bloody in the street. Try that in 2014 — three passers-by call the police and he’s done.”

Baek Jeong-hun had felt it, too. Half the reason he’d been staring at the storyboard all these days was that some scenes felt wrong even as he drew them, and he couldn’t say why. Now his nephew had named it in a sentence.

The problem was that it felt false.

“What do you want to set it in?”

“2002.”

Si-on said it plainly.

“Back then, debt collection by force still had room to breathe. In 2014 he wouldn’t survive three scenes.”

He paused.

“Samchon, your story is about violence passing down the generations. But violence has to live in an era where it can still be passed on.”

Baek Jeong-hun picked up a pencil and wrote “2002” in the margin of the storyboard. Then he just stared at the two digits.

2002.

The World Cup. The Red Devils had painted Gwanghwamun Plaza red, millions of people going wild in the street.

Cut to a shadow in an alley. A middle-aged man pressed against a wall, blood running from his mouth. In the distance, the sound of a goal being celebrated.

The country celebrating. In a corner, someone bleeding.

His eyes lit up.

“Si-on, you—”

He didn’t get to finish.

There was a knock.

“Knock, knock, knock.”

“Dad?”

Baek Jeong-hun’s expression swung from sudden inspiration straight into father mode.

“Eun-ah? Come in.”

The door opened. A girl of seventeen or eighteen stepped in. Oval face, large eyes, hair in a ponytail, dressed in a loose t-shirt and sweatpants — the look of someone fresh from dance practice, hadn’t had time to change.

Baek Eun-ah

Si-on’s cousin · SM trainee

Still carrying the smell of the dance practice room

A seventeen-year-old trainee who still has dance sweat on her, and the bridge between her uncle’s film and the people who live inside it.

Loose tee, sweatpants, damp ponytail — still carrying the smell of the practice room.

Seventeen, and already dressed for a day that started before sunrise.

“Cousin…?”

Baek Eun-ah’s gaze travelled from Si-on’s crew cut to his floral shirt, from the floral shirt to his hands slouched in his pockets, and stopped on his face. Her mouth hung open, and stayed open for a long while.

This was her cousin?

No. Impossible.

The Baek Si-on she knew had a smile that curved his eyes into crescents, a voice soft as cotton, and never corrected juniors who got his name wrong. The last phone call before his discharge had been him asking her is it hard at SM, are you going hungry.

And this man in front of her…

“Long time no see.”

Si-on straightened up and smoothed his floral shirt, trying to look less like a career criminal. It didn’t help.

Behind Eun-ah stood another person. A hoodie pulled up to the eyebrows, a mask covering half the face, only a pair of eyes showing.

jin-ri · the art of being nobody disguise · night

Hood to the eyebrows, mask to the cheekbones — a person wrapped tight around herself.

Only the eyes give it away. Eyes are the one thing a mask can’t teach to lie.

Baek Eun-ah snapped back to herself and stepped aside.

“Sunbae, this is my cousin… the one who… the…”

She stalled. How to introduce him? He was an idol but he flopped and then went to the army and now he looks like a gangster, this is my cousin?

Baek Eun-ah’s mouth opened and shut twice, then she settled on the safest version.

“…my cousin, Baek Si-on.”

Better to say too little than to say too much.

The person beside her reached up and pulled the black mask down off their face.

A face came into view.

Features so delicate they felt almost unreal — as if someone had drawn them with the thinnest brush, line by line.

Si-on’s expression didn’t change.

But inside his pocket, his fingers curled, just slightly.

Choi Jin-ri.

Stage name: Sulli.

Choi Jin-ri (Sulli)

Singer · Actor · formerly of f(x)

The beautiful twenty-year-old living through the longest snowstorm of her life

One of the most beautiful twenty-year-olds in the whole country — and the one Baek Si-on knows the ending for. She says her name is Choi Jin-ri, not Choi Sulli.

One of the most beautiful twenty-year-olds in the whole country, living through the longest snowstorm of her life.

And the end of that snowstorm, Baek Si-on knew.

He was the only one who did.

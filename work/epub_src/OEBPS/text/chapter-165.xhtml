Chapter One Hundred Sixty Five

The Big Screen Goes Feral

December 18 · showtime, and everything after the first chord Sixty thousand voices · one hush gesture · two junior groups inherited Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium · the thank-you concert, live A headliner discovers he has been demoted to backup singer in his own stadium, a vlogger’s voice cracks on camera, a mother does arithmetic on a phenomenon, a director learns what the big screen is for, and a rookie learns what the big screen does.

When the intro started, Baek Si-on was already set to open his mouth for the first verse.

What he had not accounted for: the crowd had beaten him to the job. By a full verse.

“Here we go! Here we go!”

“……”

For one moment, Baek Si-on genuinely wondered whether he had come to the wrong post tonight.

He was not the lead vocal.

He was the backing track.

To be fair, ‘Legend’ had gone near-anthem in Korea. Variety shows used it as walk-on music. School sports days used it as the opening blast. Gym trainers put it on and their clients found two extra reps from somewhere. Even elementary schoolers could hum the chorus. It was, after all, the first song in Korean pop history to top the Billboard Hot 100.

And tonight’s sixty thousand were nearly all Baek Si-on fans to begin with.

The chorus wasn’t even in sight yet, and whole sections had already shifted into combat mode.

the stands · one vlogger’s footage · cam shaking

Up in the seats, Kim Min-jun’s footage had started to shake. Not the equipment. The equipment was fine. The operator was the problem. He had planned to hold some professional YouTuber composure and capture a clean wide of the stage; then Baek Si-on opened his mouth, and the man joined the scream with his section like he had trained for it all year.

“Bang bang!”

“Bang bang!”

“Won’t stop till we are legends!”

By the third line his voice had visibly cracked on the recording. Nobody laughed at him. In a sixty-thousand-voice singalong an individual voice gets swallowed whole — and precisely because it gets swallowed, everyone shouts harder.

The broadcast director cut a wide.

Sixty thousand light sticks rising and falling together in the dark. The cold-white light rolled from the floor up to the top tier and fell back with the beat, wave after wave.

The stadium stopped being a building. It was one enormous instrument, and something was playing it.

…

Professor Yoon Hye-ja had seen Baek Si-on perform before.

But there is a difference between watching through a screen and sitting in the middle of sixty thousand people who are all shouting your son’s name.

She looked up at the center of the stage. The stage light came down on him. The big screen put his face on every corner of the bowl. The floor was shouting. The front rows were shouting. The stands were shouting.

Even the girl beside her — the one wearing his down coat — had a hand at her mouth, shouting along with the rest of them.

And Yoon Hye-ja arrived at a very concrete conclusion.

“Red-hot singer” had stopped being a usable label for her son a long time ago.

He had become a phenomenon.

First Korean actor to take a Best Actor at one of Europe’s three great festivals. First Korean singer to top the Billboard Hot 100. Either line on its own would feed an entertainer for a lifetime in this industry.

He carried both.

Next to the precision-machined, assembly-line idols, the road he had walked was simply a different shape. With raw talent, he had put on a demonstration for every senior and junior in the business — the unreasonable kind of demonstration, the kind with no lesson plan, where the only instruction is: this is also possible.

So tonight these people hadn’t come for a free concert.

They had come to watch a milestone happen in his career with their own eyes.

Free had never meant cheap.

It meant the reverse. Because it was free, every light in the bowl, every cushion on the seats, every hot drink in every hand, every light stick in the dark read like a reply.

I saw you.

You saw me back.

…

The first song, ‘Legend’, ran its full length.

The cheering inside the stadium came in like tide, wave after wave, and refused to recede. Baek Si-on stood at center stage with the microphone and tried three separate times to open his mouth; three times a fresh scream out of the stands slapped it shut.

Sixty thousand people were running hot like they had all just won the year-end lottery. The tax-free kind.

Finally, Baek Si-on raised one hand and put a finger to his lips.

Shh—

The stadium went quiet in one stroke.

From the floor to the second tier, the sound was pressed down like someone had put a whole hand on top of the bowl.

What was left: a few stray scream-tails that hadn’t gotten the memo, drifting in the air, embarrassed.

Baek Si-on looked out at the bowl, gave it two seconds, and spoke.

“I’m glad to see everyone here tonight. And thank you for coming out in weather like this.”

He let his eyes travel up the stands.

“It’s December, and it’s only going to get colder. If anyone starts feeling unwell, or genuinely can’t take the cold — don’t force it.”

A stir went through the seats. He kept going, level:

“The whole show is being recorded by tvN, it’s streaming live tonight, and there’ll be an edited rebroadcast after. Nobody here is going to miss it.”

“Getting home safe matters more than hearing every song.”

The bowl answered instantly. From somewhere on the floor:

“We’re not leaving!”

“We’re NOT leaving!”

Then more, wider:

“Not going home!”

“Not going home!”

“NOT going home!”

The chant stacked itself up layer by layer. Baek Si-on stood on the stage and listened for a moment. Then he nodded.

“All right.”

“Then we keep going.”

The piano intro arrived on the next second. The lighting slid off ‘Legend’’s hard white and down into cold blue.

Baek Si-on stood center stage, head lowered, and laid the first line down soft:

‘Sign of the Times’ · first lines

“Just stop your crying, it’s a sign of the times”

“Welcome to the final show”

“……”

In Baek Si-on’s catalogue, ‘Sign of the Times’ counted as a deep cut. From the first note it carries grief. The lyric keeps insisting everything will be all right — and the more it insists, the more it sounds like a person guarding a scrap of dignity in the worst week of their life. It belongs at 1 a.m. on headphones, alone. It does not belong in a sixty-thousand-seat stadium.

That was exactly why he’d programmed it.

Next up was Red Velvet. He had just detonated the entire venue with ‘Legend’. Hand them the stage at that temperature and four barely-debuted girls would be starting a fire with wet matches — the drop would read as their failure, and their slot would play as a cold-open to nobody.

So he was bringing the room down himself. On purpose.

The piano ran on. Baek Si-on’s English is clean, his enunciation cleaner. Not everyone in the bowl could parse every line — but the feeling inside the voice and the melody skipped past language entirely and landed.

People went quiet. People looked down. People held their light sticks a little tighter and found their eyes going hot.

A stadium that ten minutes ago had been trying to take its own roof off was now, absurdly, too quiet.

Singing the last line, Baek Si-on heard the director’s voice, low, in his ears:

“Cutting to the audience — play along.”

He heard it. The big screen switched feeds.

Choi Jin-ri’s face, four meters tall.

Baseball cap, swallowed whole by a down coat several sizes past absurd, caught mid-listen in the front row. The surrounding sections turned around in a chain; phones came up in a wave.

Choi Jin-ri froze like a hit deer — then ducked her head and lifted her light stick to screen half her face, Properly Shy.

Baek Si-on looked up at the big screen and spoke into the microphone:

“Camera director.”

“Please stop filming my audience member into wanting to leave.”

The laugh went through the stadium like wind through wheat. On the big screen, the tips of Choi Jin-ri’s ears went visibly red.

The director, a professional, cut away at once.

Next feed: Taeyang, artist seating, waving at the lens with a grin. The cheering got louder.

The feed moved on. Taeyeon and Tiffany, seated together. At the cut, Tiffany waved both arms like a crossing guard; Taeyeon smiled and gave one polite nod to the camera.

“Thank you to Girls’ Generation sunbaenim for coming as tonight’s audience.”

Tiffany shouted something back at the stage. Too far, too loud — the words dissolved. The director gave her the close-up anyway. She cracked up at her own shot and folded sideways into laughter.

After that: Girl’s Day, Yoo In-na, and down the artist rows.

At every cut, Baek Si-on gave the screen a line or two. Never more. Just enough to bring each section of the bowl back to life.

The temperature ‘Sign of the Times’ had pressed the room down to climbed back, degree by degree, to concert operating range.

Once the room was back, he added one more: ‘Human’. Brighter than ‘Sign of the Times’, more restrained than ‘Legend’ — the exact song to park the stadium in warm without going loud again.

When it ended, Baek Si-on took half a step back and the lights sank with him. He raised the microphone.

“Next — please welcome Red Velvet, with a song that pays tribute to their sunbae group, f(x).”

“‘Chu~?’”

Applause rolled. He handed off the stage to the four girls and was already walking fast for backstage.

He had a costume change to win.

★ Red Velvet · ‘Chu~?’ · tribute stage · 3:19

The lift rises. Four silhouettes come up through the floor, already in pose before the light finds them.

In fairness, these four were not strangers to big rooms. They had stood on the S4 World Championship final stage — also a stadium in the tens of thousands.

But that stage was a different animal. At Worlds, the crowd came for the games. The songs were intermission. Most of the room’s attention was somewhere over the players’ booth, not on them. Here — everyone in the building came for the show. Sixty thousand pairs of eyes, landing on them with actual weight. The pressure had mass tonight.

“Do it do it Chu~”

Formation walk. And honestly? Not their sharpest work. The three younger ones were carrying visible nerves — nothing technically wrong, just their own colors not fully out of the wrapper yet.

Behind the monitors, the director watched for a few seconds and made the veteran’s call.

“Cam seven — stay on Irene.”

“Face close-up.”

The big screen switched.

Bae Joo-hyun’s face arrived, four meters tall.

She had swapped her stage heels for flats tonight — from the wide shots, she lost a few centimeters of vertical authority. The moment the lens pushed in, the exchange rate stopped mattering. Cold-white light landed on that face, and there was nothing on it to correct.

Plenty of the room had been lukewarm about a rookie girl-group slot right after Baek Si-on. Then this face happened.

The stadium’s mood went visibly, measurably soft.

There was no helping it. In front of that face, human principle sometimes takes a short personal leave.

‘Chu~?’ ended, and the four didn’t leave the stage. The lights flipped — the intro of their debut song, ‘Happiness’, rolled in.

Where the tribute stage belonged to f(x), this one belonged to them, and you could hear the difference. Kang Seul-gi’s movements settled. Son Seung-wan’s expressions opened up. Park Soo-young beamed through the chorus — the bright, brand-new-girl-group energy finally switched on.

As for Bae Joo-hyun — by now the director had fully internalized the operating manual.

Wides for formations. Mediums for choreography. The instant a key beat landed: hard cut to Bae Joo-hyun’s face.

This was not favoritism. This was art direction.

Because rookie nerves come in two models: the kind where the audience tenses up with you — and the kind where the audience thinks, aigoo, even her nervousness is pretty.

The performance ended with the four of them at center stage, breathing light.

“That tribute just now — ‘Chu~?’ for the f(x) sunbaes. And one of f(x)’s own is sitting out there tonight. Sulli-sunbaenim — are you satisfied with your juniors’ tribute?”

The stadium laughed. It was too sincere to be promotional — closer to a student suddenly called to the blackboard to solve the one problem she’d skipped.

The big screen — a professional with instincts — happened to be on the two-shot: Baek Si-on and Bae Joo-hyun. One turned his head slightly to look at her. One stared forward, answering with full sincerity.

The two faces, side by side, were frankly excessive.

The screaming ratcheted up again.

In the truck, the director eyed his monitors and muttered:

“You put this footage in a drama, the writers’ room finishes thirty episodes by sunrise.”

“One more. Standing up there — is there anything you want to say to anyone?”

“Thank you for giving us this stage.”

A perfectly standard answer. Standard to the point of being the model answer printed in every rookie’s survival handbook.

But after she finished, her eyes stayed on Baek Si-on’s face a half second past the sentence.

Half a second is short. Short enough that most of the bowl would read it as a junior’s polite gratitude for a senior’s kindness.

Baek Si-on glanced at her.

“Even more leader-like.”

Bae Joo-hyun laughed. The director wasted nothing: the big screen put Bae Joo-hyun looking up at Baek Si-on and smiling onto sixty thousand pairs of eyes.

In the seats, Choi Jin-ri’s light stick stopped moving.

That look on the screen looked… familiar.

Because she was fairly sure she had watched her own face make it, once or twice, at moments like this.

…

The interaction ended, the four bowed again and cleared the stage.

The intro of ‘Bones’ hit, and a stadium still marinating in Bae Joo-hyun’s cheekbones got hauled bodily back into Baek Si-on’s tempo. The song had been forged in an overseas comment-section war; the melody walks in with its own barbaric confidence — not convinced? then sit down and listen to the end.

Before the chorus even arrived, the floor had started on the syllable-mangled chant the entire internet had beaten to death:

“Ahjumma, press down!”

First a few sections. By the second chorus, the entire bowl.

Baek Si-on stood at the end of the thrust stage and listened to sixty thousand people take an English rock song and, with wholly unreasonable enthusiasm, canonize it as a national homophone meme — and a rare, shallow helplessness surfaced on his face.

After ‘Bones’, the low end of ‘Way Down We Go’ pressed the air flat. White light switched to dark gold, and the rear screen opened onto a night highway — no cars, just streetlight after streetlight sliding forward, forever. Baek Si-on’s voice walked the drum line like a man keeping just this side of the center line. Half-raised light sticks around the bowl sank without their owners noticing; the song towed the room into a different pulse.

By the time ‘King’’s black-gold rig came up, the stadium was entirely his again. On the big screen, the outline of a crown strobed once through the dark. The drums dropped. Eight follow-spots split the stage from both wings. Baek Si-on stood at the center and barely moved a step — and the whole stage still seemed to rotate around him. The bowl was singing the chorus back at him before it arrived.

…

Three songs later, under the lift platform, seven boys were already standing by.

“Next — please welcome BTS.”

The bowl ran hotter for this than it had for Red Velvet’s slot — and not by accident. At the World Championship, BTS had danced behind Baek Si-on. A meaningful chunk of his fanbase still remembered their faces.

Also: tonight’s attendance skews female, a young boy group arrives with its own weather system, and nobody in the building was about to fight physics.

Their first number was ‘Danger’. The drums clicked in, seven bodies split the formation wide, and good chunks of the floor were already screaming along.

Backstage, mid-change, mic pack in his teeth, Baek Si-on listened to the noise with his jacket half-on. Park Ji-hun sorted his in-ear cables beside him, and couldn’t help saying:

“Those kids can really dance.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Mm.”

Park Ji-hun gave him a look.

“Did you ever dance like that?”

Baek Si-on thought about it.

“No.”

“Then what were you doing back when you debuted?”

“Surviving.”

Park Ji-hun: “……”

Two songs later, Baek Si-on was back out.

The routine ran the same as Red Velvet’s: the introduction, the camera time, every member self-introduced, the banter. Whatever raising-a-junior-group obligations exist, he wasn’t going to run the full protocol for one and shortchange the other.

Seven names fell into a sixty-thousand-seat stadium, one after another.

They were not yet the group that would one day make the whole world shout those names back.

But tonight, at least sixty thousand people heard all seven of them properly for the first time.

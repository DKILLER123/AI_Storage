The Gangnam Tax Office’s official social account was, on ordinary days, unread.

Its content ran to filing-deadline reminders, electronic-invoice guides, and the occasional repost of a National Tax Service policy explainer. Its followers were mostly accounting-firm interns and the self-employed, at tax season.

But at 4:17 PM on March 30, the account published a post.

Official account · Gangnam Tax Office

posted · March 30, 4:17 PM

“Excellent Youth Taxpayer of the Republic of Korea” — Certificate of Appreciation. Presented today. The first commendation plaque our office has issued this year.

The photo: Baek Si-on shaking hands with the deputy director. Baek Si-on holding the model-taxpayer plaque; the deputy director beside him smiling harder than the winner.

For the first fifteen minutes after posting, the like count stood at twenty-three. There were three comments — two of which were a tax-office employee’s thumbs-up.

At the sixteenth minute, Baek Si-on’s fans arrived.

The entertainment media followed fast. The headlines circled a small set of keywords — model taxpayer, honest taxes, a positive image for people in culture and the arts.

Entertainment desks · within the hour Baek Si-on receives model-taxpayer commendation — a top artist’s social responsibility From Venice Best Actor to Billboard champion: Baek Si-on honored again today — for paying taxes Baek Si-on appears at Gangnam Tax Office; fans say: even his tax payments look like award ceremonies

None of those headlines were exaggerated.

Then the financial media entered, and the first one to genuinely shove the story sideways was the online finance channel under the Korea Economic Daily.

Its headline:

— BEHIND THE TAX BILL: BAEK SI-ON’S INCOME MAY HAVE PASSED 30 BILLION WON —

They had no way near S.W Studio’s internal ledgers, but financial reporters have their own folk methods.

The estimate, as assembled by folk method

Public endorsement fees — added in.

Television appearance rights — added in.

Digital and physical sales — estimated at industry averages.

Ad shoot fees, brand event fees, licensing — filled in at market rates.

Corporate-tax scale — back-derived from the commendation tier and anonymous interviews with industry tax professionals.

Verdict: a number that made even the entertainment reporters go quiet. Conservative: 20 billion won. Aggressive: 30 billion.

The article even stressed, specially:

— This estimate covers Baek Si-on’s annual commercial scale in the Korean market only. It excludes the North America tour, Billboard rights income, the Under Armour global endorsement, and other overseas projects. —

Very soon, another financial outlet turned the estimate into a table.

2014–2015 · Korean celebrity commercial income, estimated · as tabled

Column one — Korea-domestic income, estimated: 1. Baek Si-on. 2. EXO. 3. Kim XX — the phenomenon-level actor advertisers couldn’t refuse after My Love from the Stars. Then Jun XX, BIGBANG, Girls’ Generation, IU, Lee Min-ho, on down the line.

Column two — global commercial scale, estimated: one name, pulled alone, a long way ahead of the field.

Media’s conservative figure for column two: above 150 billion won. The sum of number two through number six, combined.

The first column alone was enough to ignite debate. On the strength of 2014, EXO and Kim XX had become two names the Korean entertainment industry could not route around — one a top boy group, the other an actor drowning in endorsement offers since My Love from the Stars. And Baek Si-on, in the Korea-domestic column, already out-weighed them both.

But what truly detonated the forums was the second column.

In Naver’s real-time search, “Baek Si-on taxes,” “Baek Si-on income,” “Baek Si-on 30 billion,” and “Baek Si-on 150 billion” took turns climbing.

That last line pushed public sentiment another layer toward the positive.

The Korean public’s relationship with high-earning celebrities is complicated. Earn a lot, and you are envied. Earn a lot and evade taxes, and you are cursed into the ground. But earn a lot, earn more abroad, and get commended by the tax office for honest payment — that is very hard to curse.

And Baek Si-on’s money told an unusually beautiful story. He did not harvest his own country’s fans. He went to America, to Europe, to the Middle East, earned his money there, brought it home, and paid tax on it in Korea. The narrative fit the national-sentiment template the Korean public loves better than a key fits its lock.

Insight, of course, was not going to pass this up.

Son Nam-won rewrote the headline himself.

The body, meanwhile, dead serious: from the founding of S.W Studio — how personal income was normalized, corporatized, made transparent; how tax planning separated Korean and overseas income; how evasion disputes were avoided while the Korean-side obligations were carried voluntarily.

After it ran, even finance readers who never touched entertainment news forwarded it.

By early evening, a finance channel’s nightly program had made the story into a three-minute special.

The anchor stood before an electronic screen — behind him, a structural diagram of Baek Si-on’s income. Korea-domestic income. Overseas touring. Music rights. Brand endorsements. Equity partnerships. Lines spidering outward from Baek Si-on’s photo like a web.

The anchor’s summary:

“Baek Si-on’s case shows a new individual-IP model emerging in the Korean entertainment industry.”

“Simply put —”

❧ ❧ ❧

8:20 in the evening.

Jin-ri had just forced the third takeout container to the very bottom of the trash bag.

Tonight’s dinner did not entirely count as delivery, because she had participated in many of its processes.

The participation ledger, as self-assessed

Unwrapping — performed.

Plating — performed.

Reheating — performed.

Sesame — sprinkled.

Scallions — arranged.

Soup — transferred to a pot and boiled until it bubbled.

Every store-branded plastic bag — crushed into one mass and pressed into the deepest stratum of the trash bag.

Aggregate verdict: this could by no stretch be called simply ordering delivery. At minimum: half self-made.

Jin-ri stood at the dining table, seriously inspected the results, and felt a thoroughly irrational surge of accomplishment.

Hara unnie had been right yesterday. Paying by card is participating in production.

Well, today she hadn’t just paid by card. She had also heated. That was deep participation.

Blin crouched on a chair, watching her shuttle between the table and the stove with cold detachment.

Jin-ri turned to him.

“What do you think?”

Blin did not respond.

“It looks great, right?”

Blin lowered his head and licked a paw.

Jin-ri elected to interpret this as approval.

And right then, the doorbell rang.

She ran to the mirror by the entryway and checked. Hair — in place. Lip color — surviving. The collar of her knit sweater — normal.

Good.

She drew one deep breath, walked to the door, and opened it.

Baek Si-on stood outside.

She had been about to say you’re back — but the words hit her teeth and she swallowed them hard. Too much like a drama.

So instead:

“You came.”

Baek Si-on looked at her — and smelled the apartment first.

“Smells great.”

Jin-ri’s heart went soft; she was about to smile, and then decided, suddenly, to tease him:

“I haven’t showered yet.”

Baek Si-on gave her one look.

“I meant the food.”

“……”

The smile stopped on Jin-ri’s face. She raised a hand and thumped him.

“THIS is the moment where you say I smell great.”

Baek Si-on’s shoulder rocked lightly with the thump.

“You smell great too.”

“Too late.”

“Shall I start over?”

“No chances left.”

“This strict?”

“Early romance is strict.”

“……”

Once it was out, Jin-ri froze before he did.

Romance. She had just said it. Out loud. And perfectly naturally.

…Fine.

“Let’s eat first.”

“Mm.”

Baek Si-on walked to the table, let his gaze travel across the dishes, and asked:

“You made this?”

Jin-ri nodded.

“Mm.”

Baek Si-on looked at her. She looked at him. Two seconds later, a little guilty, she added:

“I participated in most of the processes.”

Baek Si-on caught the key word.

“Most?”

“Eat first.”

Jin-ri hastily handed him chopsticks:

“It’s bad cold.”

Baek Si-on took them, searched the dishes, and selected the smallest piece of spicy stir-fried pork on the table.

He held no illusions whatsoever about Jin-ri’s cooking. Actresses who grew up on camera usually had badly crooked skill trees, and cooking was not a required course in her age bracket. So Baek Si-on proceeded with caution. He had even pre-selected the expression he would use to encourage her work.

The pork went in. The flavor was, unexpectedly, harmonious.

Jin-ri had been staring at him the whole time; the moment his brow lifted she hurried:

“Well?”

Baek Si-on took another bite.

“It’s delicious.”

“Really? It’s… okay, right.”

Jin-ri worked hard to sound matter-of-fact, then turned to scoop rice. Baek Si-on tried the sweet-and-sour pork.

“This too.”

Jin-ri kept her head down over the rice cooker, and her mouth-corner could not be suppressed at any price.

She felt she ought to act mature about this. It was, after all, only a meal. Strictly speaking, not even cooked.

❝

But this “delicious,” landing in her ears, cost more than many compliments ever had.

— the dinner table; the smallest piece of pork, chosen on purpose

She set the rice down in front of Baek Si-on.

“Then eat more.”

“Okay.”

Jin-ri sat across from him and watched rice, soup, ribs, and spicy pork travel into his mouth in some rhythm she couldn’t parse but that was unmistakably efficient, and her mood improved bite by bite.

This was different from being called pretty. Different from receiving a gift, too. It was just the feeling that all of her chaos had been worth something.

“Tiring day?”

“Not really.”

“Did you eat lunch?”

“No.”

Jin-ri frowned, and a rib landed in his bowl at once. Baek Si-on eyed the growing skyline of his bowl.

“Are you feeding me until I can’t leave the house?”

Jin-ri said, small-voiced:

“That would be fine too.”

‘Producers’ wrapped for the day at nearly nine in the evening.

“Everyone, thank you for your hard work!”

“Lee Ji-eun-ssi, thank you for your hard work!”

“See you tomorrow!”

“See you tomorrow.”

Bowing to the responses, Lee Ji-eun walked toward the van.

Her shooting state these days really was good. The crew assumed she had studied Cindy hard.

It wouldn’t be entirely wrong. It also wouldn’t be entirely right.

Cindy’s core emotion was a particular species of cold: the cold of someone who cares, intensely, and absolutely refuses to admit it. The eyes may look at you; the face may give you nothing. The mouth may speak; the tone must feel like charity. Inside, floodwaters. Outside, an ice sculpture.

And Lee Ji-eun had recently been enjoying a stable supply of exactly that emotion.

The supply source was a person who, from Milan to America, had messaged with a frequency best described as vanished.

So she had sealed the displeasure away, taken out a small ration of it daily, and spread it evenly onto Cindy’s lines.

The results were outstanding. Baek Si-on’s silence had, in a certain sense, become her free masterclass in acting.

Jeong Han-teuk sat in the driver’s seat, scrolling his phone. At the sound of the door, he turned by reflex.

“Good work.”

Lee Ji-eun pulled open the rear door and got in.

“Mm.”

She leaned back and closed her eyes.

Today’s last scene had taken three takes. The director had approved the first two; the third she had requested herself, because in take two, Cindy’s head-turn had run five degrees too far.

Five degrees. No one else could see it. She could.

Baek Si-on could probably see it too.

…Don’t think about that person.

The car pulled out. And then Jeong Han-teuk made a mistake — the kind of mistake that, in the after-action review, he would personally file under “one of the greatest judgment errors of my career.”

“Ji-eun-ya. Si-on is back in Korea.”

“He told you?”

Han-teuk had not yet registered the danger signal.

“No. A state agency did.”

The car sat quiet for about three seconds.

Three seconds is not long. But it was long enough for the back of Han-teuk’s neck to start cooling.

“…A state agency?”

“The news.”

Han-teuk unclipped his phone from the mount and handed it back. Lee Ji-eun took it.

The push notification, as it landed in the van

Maeil Business — BEHIND 3.2 BILLION WON IN TAXES: BAEK SI-ON, ONE PERSON GROWING INTO A COMPANY

Photo: the Gangnam Tax Office’s official shot.

On any ordinary day, Lee Ji-eun would have sneered — what a poser — or said the headline read like a listed company’s press release.

Tonight she was in no mood for either. She handed the phone back to Han-teuk, and said:

“So now he announces his homecomings through state agencies?”

Han-teuk gripped the wheel, abruptly recognized that something had gone wrong, and attempted repair.

“Uh… maybe his schedule was brutal, and coming home was only for the tax filing — he’ll leave right away, probably didn’t want it to affect—”

“Do I look like I care about taxes?”

Han-teuk shut his mouth.

He should not have kept talking. Correct.

Lee Ji-eun looked down and opened her own phone. The screen lit. She opened her contacts, and her fingertip stopped on one name.

Baek Si-on.

She knew he wasn’t the kind of man who reports his whereabouts. That wasn’t the problem.

The problem was this: he had come back to Korea — appeared in the news, paid the nation its tax, let the financial press write him up as a multinational that walks — and he had not sent her one line.

I’m back.

❝

He could put a John Lennon guitar in her hands. He could send hot chocolate and a support truck to her set. He could kiss her back in a Milan hotel room. But he could not remember to tell her he was in the country.

— the contact list; the finger, hovering over one name

Lee Ji-eun suddenly found it very funny.

She dialed — Cindy’s expression already fitted to her face.

For the man on the other end, who was at that moment eating dinner, this was obviously not good news.

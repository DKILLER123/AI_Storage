Chapter Forty-Eight

Baek Jeong-hun: I Thought You Weren't a Baek

July 24 Evening Yeonnam-dong, Baek Jeong-hun's studio The man the whole country was hunting

Meanwhile, in the studio in Yeonnam-dong.

The "genius director" the whole Korean media was frantically searching for was, at this very moment, sitting in front of a computer, one hand propping up his temple, the other hovering over a mouse, staring at the same single shot on the timeline for forty minutes.

No champagne. No celebration. He hadn't even opened Naver.

He was fine-cutting.

The rough cut had been tiring, sure — but at least it had a direction. Fine-cutting was different. Fine-cutting was a question of whether every single shot stayed or went, whether every cut point was off by half a second in one direction or the other, whether a pause was a beat too long or a beat too short. It was the kind of thing that drove a person bald.

Baek Jeong-hun could sit at the computer for a whole afternoon over the fate of a single shot. Not because he couldn't decide. Because he couldn't bear to.

Every shot had a reason to exist.

The close-up that had taken seventeen takes to get right. That long take where Baek Si-on had improvised. The other one the cinematographer had waited forty minutes for the natural light to fall just so.

It was also why two weeks had passed and the film had advanced less than forty minutes.

When Si-on pushed the door open and came in, he was staring at a three-second shot, lost.

“Uncle.”

“……Si-on's here.”

“Mm. Eat some watermelon.”

Si-on set down a plastic box of cut watermelon. It was from the fruit stand downstairs, sliced into wedges by the owner, toothpicks already stuck in.

Baek Jeong-hun took a piece, bit into it, and the juice ran down his chin. He wiped it with the back of his hand and kept his eyes on the screen.

Si-on dragged a folding chair over and sat beside him.

“How's it going?”

Jeong-hun chewed, voice muffled.

“Don't ask.”

Si-on looked at the screen. The timeline was a dense mess — video track, audio track, annotation track, the colour tags scattered everywhere, red green yellow grey, like a palette somebody had stepped on. The progress bar was stuck at thirty-seven minutes, the rest still in rough-cut form.

Si-on didn't probe the spot he was stuck on. Instead he let his eye travel the whole layout and offered:

“Uncle, why not try cutting backwards from the end?”

“Lock the tone of the ending first, then reverse-engineer the pacing in front of it. Once the destination is fixed, the path shows itself.”

Jeong-hun looked at him for two seconds.

“That's not bad.”

He put the toothpick in the corner of his mouth and dragged the cursor to the very end of the timeline. The rough-cut ending. The picture lit up.

Sang-hun, played by Si-on, lay collapsed in the alley. On his face was the blood from the hammer swung by Yeon-hui's younger brother. Eyes half open, pupils unfocused, breath coming in shallow, uneven pulls.

From far away came the sound of a crowd cheering after a goal.

Cut.

Gwanghwamun Square. Tens of thousands of people in Red Devil T-shirts poured into the street — horns, cheers, waves of the Taegeukgi all folded together into one tide. The whole country celebrating. Somewhere else, in a corner, someone was bleeding. The juxtaposition of the celebration and the violence. The irony loaded to the hilt.

But now Jeong-hun had a new idea.

He pulled another set of shots from the archive.

On the monitor · the new ending · 0:27

A narrow alley. Yeon-hui's younger brother, in a dark-blue work jacket, a baseball bat in hand, pacing in front of a private household's iron gate.

Collecting a debt. Behind the iron gate, a woman's voice, faint, crying. The brother raised the bat and struck the gate. Clang. The metal dented inward.

Wide shot. Yeon-hui at the mouth of the alley, two supermarket bags in hand, just back from the market. She looks at her brother's back, stops, and the bags slide slowly out of her hands.

Cross-dissolve. In her unfocused gaze her brother's figure blurs. The outline changes — the shoulders widen, the height stretches, the way he holds the bat goes from savage to savager.

Sang-hun. She sees Sang-hun in her brother. And then black.

No music, no grading, the sound still the rough field recording. Twenty-seven seconds.

Jeong-hun played the whole thing from the top. No music, no grading, the sound still the rough field recording. Twenty-seven seconds.

Si-on watched it through, sitting beside him.

There it was. The point had been made.

Violence didn't stop when one person died. The man who swung the fist fell, but what he left behind fell like seed into the bones of the next generation.

The rough-cut ending had been irony. The celebration in the square held against the blood in the alley. The fine-cut ending was fear. A viewer would watch those twenty-seven seconds, walk out of the cinema, and be unable to stop thinking: what about the next one? And the one after him? When does this cycle ever end?

Jeong-hun didn't ask Si-on whether the ending was right.

No need to ask. He knew himself.

He saved the markers and began to blend the earlier scenes into the new ending, fingers moving between keyboard and mouse, faster than before.

About ten minutes later, Si-on spoke up.

“Uncle, there's something I should tell you.”

“Mm?”

“I gave Dispatch the exclusive on you.”

Baek Jeong-hun's hands stopped. He turned the chair around to look at him.

“When are they coming?”

“In a bit.”

Si-on paused.

“You don't mind?”

Jeong-hun looked at his nephew's face for a second, then laughed.

“The way you say it, you'd think you didn't carry the Baek name.”

He shut himself away in this studio cutting films all day, but that didn't mean he'd lost touch with the outside world. He knew everything Si-on had been through these past few days. Baek Eun-ah sent him a status update at least three times a day.

But he couldn't help. He didn't understand public-relations strategy, didn't know any media people, couldn't slap back at the internet. Editing was the one thing he could do, and he couldn't let himself get distracted, because this film also carried Si-on's future.

If there was one thing he could do: trade one exclusive interview for his nephew's name back. That deal was so cheap it was almost embarrassing to call it helping.

“Let Dispatch interview me, sure.”

Jeong-hun picked up the last piece of watermelon off the desk and put it in his mouth.

“Your uncle's face never had much commercial value anyway. Lending it to them costs nothing.”

He bit into the watermelon. Juice ran to his chin again. This time he didn't wipe it.

“But you—”

He pointed at Si-on with a toothpick.

“—later, help me tidy the desk. Dispatch is coming to take photos, and I don't want them walking away thinking this director lives in a rubbish pile.”

Si-on glanced at the instant-noodle tubs, the empty coffee cups, the ashtray, and the plastic box dripping watermelon juice on the desk.

Without a word, he stood up and started cleaning.

July 24 Evening The studio, twenty minutes later The desk, and the smell that wouldn't leave

Twenty minutes later.

The instant-noodle tubs were cleared, the empty coffee cups washed and set aside, the ashtray emptied and wiped clean.

But the smoke was still there. Ten-some days of cigarette smoke, seeped into the plaster of the walls and the fibres of the curtains. That wasn't something twenty minutes with the window open could clear.

Si-on stood in the middle of the room and tipped his head up, sniffing.

Mm. Still strong.

He was weighing whether to run down to the convenience store and buy a can of air freshener, at least so a Dispatch reporter wouldn't walk in and think he'd wandered into a private mah-jong parlour.

His phone rang. He pulled it out. Lim Geun-ho.

Lim Geun-ho → Baek Si-on · evening · Yeonnam-dong

“Actor Baek, we'll be there in about twenty minutes.”

“Good.”

“Also, the debt-collection clearing piece is already up.”

“Thank you. See you in a bit.”

Four hours, for an entertainment story, was enough to complete the entire evolution from “exclusive” to “common knowledge.”

First hour: Insight, alone.

By seven on the dot.

The headline was that Insight flash.

The picture was Baek Si-on in Sala Grande, holding the Volpi Cup, full-length.

September 6 8:00 am SBS · Inkigayo, the communal dressing room The last stage of “Happiness,” and a borrowed phone

SBS.

Music-show backstage.

Eight in the morning.

The fluorescent tubes over the communal bunk were still that miserable white, laying a layer of not-enough-sleep grey-green on everyone’s face.

Today was a recording day for SBS’s Inkigayo.

It was also the last stage of Red Velvet’s debut song, “Happiness.”

Four girls resting in the communal bunk.

Bae Joo-hyun — Irene — sat in the innermost place. Beside her, Son Seung-wan was slumped over the table, both hands under her chin, eyelids fighting.

Kang Seul-gi sat on the floor stretching. Park Soo-young was pressing her legs for her.

The daily life of a rookie promo period.

Early rise, hold, rehearsal, record, back to the company, practice, sleep.

Repeat.

Alive in a way that did not look at all like the exhausted idol who had been sunk in the stage-attitude controversy.

A strange thought drilled into her head.

It seemed…

People who got close to Baek Si-on — their luck got better?

Baek Eun-ah.

The girl who had once eaten gimbap with them had shed her skin and become the exclusive manager of a Venice Best Actor.

Choi Jin-ri.

Mocked by the whole internet, even put on pause and shelved by the company.

Acted in his film.

Now wearing the highest haute couture, standing on the red carpet of the oldest international film festival in the world, smiling like a princess with no cloud over her.

As if there were a causal law that did not care about reason.

Get close to this person, Baek Si-on, and luck would rebound off the floor with extreme exaggeration.

Bae Joo-hyun took her gaze off the screen.

“Nothing.”

She shook her head, a little self-mocking.

How could there be an absurd principle that getting close to someone made your luck better?

That was all strength they had accumulated behind the camera, plus a little unreplicable opportunity… wasn’t it?

September 7 12:00 noon Venice · Hotel Cipriani, the suite A cup on the nightstand, a lump on the back of the head, and a mouth that needed three brushings

Hotel Cipriani, the suite.

Noon.

Baek Si-on was woken by the sun.

The curtains had not been pulled fully. A beam of Italian September midday light came straight through the gap and split, with precision, across his left eyelid.

He put the back of his hand over his eyes and waited about ten seconds.

Then slowly moved the hand, squinted, and swept the room.

Hotel suite.

On the nightstand, a glass of water poured at some unknown time, and beside it, the Volpi Cup.

The gold of the cup threw light, quietly, in the sun.

Baek Si-on looked at it for two seconds.

Confirmed it was not a dream.

Sat up in the bed.

A dull pain came from the back of his head.

Not the swollen pain of a hangover. The kind you get from hitting something hard.

He reached up and rubbed.

Found a small lump.

No idea when he had hit it.

No memory at all.

The taste in his mouth was a disaster of another order.

The sour of prosecco, the bite of grappa, the lemon-cloying of limoncello — three liquids that should never have coexisted in the same digestive system had fermented in his mouth overnight into a composite aftertaste that was difficult to describe in human language.

If it had to be described.

It was roughly pouring a bottle of expired lemon cleaner into a copper pot that had held brandy, and then rinsing with that pot.

Baek Si-on sincerely suggested that Italians stop inventing new spirits.

The existing categories were already enough to send a Korean’s taste buds into intensive care.

He got out of bed, barefoot on the carpet, and went to the bathroom to brush his teeth.

Twice.

Still a taste.

Brushed again.

Barely acceptable.

Knock. Knock.

The door.

“Cousin, you up?”

Baek Eun-ah’s voice came in from the corridor.

Baek Si-on dragged his feet over and opened the door.

Baek Eun-ah stood in the doorway.

Looked him up and down.

Wrinkled her face.

“Do you know how you got back last night?”

Baek Si-on leaned on the doorframe and thought.

Memory began to blur after the node of “urinal-escaped, found Choi Jin-ri sitting on the stone rail looking at the sea.” After that it was a black fog that tasted of prosecco.

“No.”

Baek Eun-ah put both hands on her hips, drew a deep breath, as if she were writing the opening title of an epic disaster documentary.

“When Jin-ri eonni messaged me I had already lain down. When Park Ji-hun and I ran over, you were face-up on eonni’s lap, snoring.”

“The three of us took turns dragging you to the dock. In the middle you woke up once, said, ‘Parma ham tastes better torn by hand,’ and went back to sleep.”

“On the boat you threw up once. It was still eonni grabbing you by the back of the collar that kept you from going into the sea.”

“…”

“If you came to help me remember what happened last night—”

Baek Si-on lifted a finger and pointed down the corridor.

Out.

“Of course not.”

Baek Eun-ah took her hands off her hips.

“Vogue is coming to shoot at two this afternoon.”

“Shoot?”

“Mm. With the other winners. Golden Lion, Best Director, Best Actress, those.”

She took out her phone and glanced at a memo.

“But my dad’s Lion of the Future isn’t on the shoot list. That grade doesn’t clear Vogue’s threshold for this feature.”

“Oh.”

Baek Si-on leaned on the doorframe. His head was still a hangover muddle, but he knew very well what the weight of “Vogue” was.

Director’s note · the other machine behind the carpet

This kind of shoot is not a last-minute ornament on top of the honour.

It is the other mature commercial machine the three major festivals have run for decades, alongside the artistic one.

The committee hands history-making honours to the makers — that is the cultural asset on the surface.

The real underwriters, hiding behind the red carpet, will, as a matter of course, ask the fashion magazines they work with to bring this new crop of winners into a studio.

“Ask” is the polite word. The accurate word is “require.”

The magazine side is happy to cooperate.

They also need these new art aristocrats to lift the magazine’s grade.

As for the winner himself.

A set of fashion pictures executed by a top global team, the finished work translated into a dozen languages, published in more than thirty Vogue regional editions.

The set going up at the same time on the site and on social, global cumulative exposure counted in “hundreds of millions of views.”

A resource of that grade is one no agency can buy, at any price.

Three sides, each taking what they need.

No one loses.

Baek Si-on glanced at the clock on the wall.

Twelve oh-eight.

Less than two hours.

“Cousin, you need Park Ji-hun.”

Baek Eun-ah scanned his present state up and down again.

The image of a newly minted Venice Best Actor on the morning after a hangover could probably rank in the top three least-bearable pictures of this festival.

“I know.”

“Very. Much. Need.”

She said it one word at a time.

“I know.”

Baek Si-on held the door handle, about to close it.

Baek Eun-ah turned and walked two steps down the corridor.

Looked back.

“Right. Cousin.”

“Last night, the video of you standing on the dining table singing — someone put it on Instagram.”

Baek Si-on’s hand stopped on the handle.

Baek Eun-ah’s eyes were bright as two little bulbs.

“Views already broke a hundred thousand.”

“…”

Baek Si-on had not yet organised a complete reaction when Baek Eun-ah had already turned, laughing, and run, the slap of slippers getting farther down the corridor.

Watching her back go.

Baek Si-on closed his eyes and tried to fish more detail out of last night’s prosecco-flavoured black fog.

Fragments floated up, frame by frame.

Dining table.

White cloth.

Torn bread.

A glass on its side.

The saxophone in his ear.

Then himself, standing in front of a few hundred film people, standing between Parma ham and tiramisu, pointing at Choi Jin-ri and singing a love song.

No.

This was not him.

How would Baek Si-on stand on a table and sing a love song at a woman?

And point.

Fortunately this was Venice.

What were Europeans best at?

Romantic annotation.

A newly minted Best Actor, at the closing dinner, standing on a dining table to improvise a song, then at the climax of the chorus pointing at the female lead of his own film.

In the European system of reading, what was that called?

That was called paying homage to the muse.

An artist expressing his most sincere respect to the source of his inspiration.

Baek Si-on ran that phrasing around in his head once.

Good.

Homage to the muse.

Nothing else to do with it.

If there had to be something else to do with it.

Then it was the alcohol.

The atmosphere.

The saxophone.

The catalysis of Venetian moonlight.

One more iron proof that Italians should never invent new spirits again.

September 7 2:10 pm Hotel Cipriani · third floor, a suite facing the lagoon Two hundred and thirty-seven frames, and a queue of business cards

Two ten in the afternoon.

Third floor of the hotel.

A temporary studio had been put up in a large suite facing the lagoon.

Park Ji-hun had just used an hour and a half to save Baek Si-on’s hungover face.

Hot and cold towels alternating on the eyes to take the swelling down. Concealer. Set. Hair done again.

The result that came out was within five percent of the person who had held the Volpi Cup under the Sala Grande follow-spot yesterday.

The shoot ran about an hour.

Vogue Italia · winners sitting

TURN LEFT · CHIN DOWN · EYES HERE

Photographer, English, the whole way. No wasted words.

“Turn left. Chin down. Eyes here. Good. Again.”

Fifty-five minutes. Two hundred and thirty-seven frames.

When the photographer put the camera down, the curve at the corner of his mouth said everything.

Baek Si-on’s camera sense did not need teaching.

After the shoot, a short social interval.

A long table in the rest area outside the studio: espresso, sparkling water, a few plates of biscuits.

The editor-in-chief of Vogue Italia, Franca Sozzani, walked in.

Franca Sozzani

Editor-in-chief · Vogue Italia

Real-name counterpart · Italy · more than twenty years at the top of the fashion chain

A woman in her sixties with a signature fall of blonde waves. Editors-in-chief do not appear at every sitting. A Volpi Cup Best Actor sitting is not every sitting. She had come from another room on purpose, and the card she put in his hand was not courtesy. It was a judgment: worth investing in.

She had come from another room. Clearly she had come specifically to see Baek Si-on.

An editor-in-chief does not appear at every shoot.

But a Volpi Cup Best Actor shoot is not every shoot.

“Mr. Baek.”

She put out her hand.

The handshake was exactly the right pressure.

“Congratulations. I was in the hall last night at the closing. The moment Tim Roth read your name, at least three editors beside me took out their phones and started sending mail.”

Baek Si-on smiled, politely.

“This set will appear in the October issue. The main pages of the Italian edition, and it will be distributed to the regional edition desks worldwide.”

She took a card from the bag she carried and held it out.

On the back: her private email, and an Italian mobile number.

“If you have any fashion-related collaboration in mind in future, or need exposure on this side of Europe, contact me directly.”

The tone of that sentence was not courtesy.

It was a person who had sat at the top of the fashion industry chain for more than twenty years, making a “worth investing in” judgment on a new face that had just entered her field of view.

Baek Si-on took the card, glanced at it, and put it in the inside pocket of his suit.

“Thank you, Ms. Sozzani.”

Immediately after, the brand PRs.

Armani’s global PR director walked over, offered a card, said, “Giorgio very much admired the effect of you wearing our clothes on the carpet.”

Cartier’s Europe PR manager offered a card, and hinted that a global-ambassador casting was in progress for next year.

A representative from Jaeger-LeCoultre offered a card, hinted at nothing, and only said, purely, “Your wrist suits our watches.”

About fifteen minutes, front to back.

Baek Si-on’s inside pocket had several more cards in it.

Behind each one, a commercial channel worth several million dollars.

He did not need to ask.

They queued up to give.

The whole process, two pairs of eyes watched from the side, from the first frame to the last.

The SM manager.

And Choi Jin-ri.

The two of them stood in the doorway area of the suite, and had been there since the shoot started.

The manager’s purpose in bringing Choi Jin-ri today was very clear:

A Vogue sitting gathered the top layer of global fashion contacts — photographers, brand PRs, magazine editors. Hook any one line and it would be a qualitative leap for Choi Jin-ri’s future fashion resources.

SM called the wind and rain in Korea.

The manager had assumed Europe was the same.

It was not.

Very much not.

He had stood with Choi Jin-ri on the outer rim of the shooting area for more than an hour, from the start of the shoot to the end of the shoot.

In the middle he had tried, in the gap when a brand PR was changing sets, to walk up and offer a card.

The French woman from Cartier took it politely, glanced at “SM Entertainment” on the card, said “nice to meet you,” and went back to looking down at the playback.

The Jaeger-LeCoultre person was even cleaner.

Smiled, set the card on the table, turned, and went to talk to a colleague.

No one was rude.

No one cared, either.

Evidently, SM had no additive on this shore of the Adriatic.

Seven minutes later he watched Baek Si-on take a card from Armani’s PR, say something, smiling, the other person also smiling, the two of them shaking hands.

As if chatting with an old friend in his own living room.

The manager stood ten metres off, his mouth pressed into a line.

The same people.

The same occasion.

He offered a card and was ignored.

Baek Si-on walked over, and the other side took a card out first.

Where was the gap?

It was position.

When Baek Si-on stood in front of these brand people, he was not “an Asian artist come to network.” He was “an object they needed to work with.”

The A and B of the relationship were reversed.

The manager was silent for a while.

Then walked over beside Baek Si-on.

Waited until he had finished with the Armani person and turned to drink water, then stepped in.

“Mr. Baek.”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

“There is something I wanted to trouble you with.”

The manager’s posture was very low, lower even than after he had been upgraded to first class at Incheon.

“Jin-ri… has never had many overseas resources on the fashion side. Walking the carpet in Venice this time counts as a little international exposure. But if she could build some contact with these brand people, for her later development—”

“You want me to introduce her?”

The manager’s sentence was cut off by Baek Si-on.

Not impatience.

Saving time.

“Yes. If it’s convenient.”

Baek Si-on screwed the cap back on the bottle, turned his head, and beckoned to Choi Jin-ri, standing a few steps away.

She saw the gesture and walked over.

Baek Si-on turned and took a step toward the Vogue editor-in-chief.

“Ms. Sozzani.”

Franca Sozzani turned her head and saw there was one more person beside Baek Si-on.

“This is Choi Jin-ri, the female lead of The Green Fly. We walked the carpet together. We took a standing ovation together in Sala Grande.”

Sozzani’s gaze moved from Baek Si-on to Choi Jin-ri’s face.

Looked her over, top to bottom, then put out her hand.

“I saw your performance in the film. Very real.”

Choi Jin-ri took it.

“Thank you.”

The Korean-English accent was still there.

But the smile was clean.

Sozzani’s hand let go. She did not offer her own card.

But she turned and said a sentence in Italian to the assistant beside her.

The assistant nodded, drew a card from a folder, and handed it to Choi Jin-ri.

The name on the card was not Sozzani’s.

It was the contact for the deputy editor of Vogue Italia’s fashion section.

A grade lower than the one given to Baek Si-on.

But the channel was open.

The manager stood five metres off, watching this.

The thing he had waited more than an hour for and not gotten, Baek Si-on had opened with one sentence.

Chapter Thirty-Two

These Words Only Know IU's Voice

June 25 Baek Jeong-hun's studio Something off, and no name for it

Si-on read the lyrics through once, then leaned back on the sofa, thumb resting against the edge of the screen.

Good.

It really was good.

The mood was right. The pictures were right. But something, somewhere, was off — and he couldn't name where.

It wasn't a feeling he could put a finger on. Just that, reading line after line, a fine chill kept running up the back of his neck, the kind you get when an air conditioner points at a spot it shouldn't.

He read the whole thing again.

Still wrong. Still unnamed.

He thought about it, then opened his thread with Jae-joon and forwarded the screenshot.

Look at these words. Tell me if anything's off.

Sent, and waited.

One minute. Two. Three.

The little “read” marker appeared, but no reply.

Si-on watched the screen. Read-and-not-answered meant one of two things: he was busy, or he was trying to figure out how to say something he didn't want to say.

The phone went off at five minutes. A call. He picked up.

Jung Jae-joon → Baek Si-on · night · Hapjeong-dong

Jae-joon's voice came out of the earpiece with an extra layer of something held back, like a man trying very hard to keep a lid on it.

“IU wrote this?”

Si-on's finger tapped once against the phone case.

“How'd you know?”

Jae-joon made a low sound, almost a laugh, almost a sigh.

“The personal style's not hiding. Onomatopoeia as the base, reduplication for the rhythm, loneliness shoved inside a nursery-rhyme cadence. There aren't three people in this country who write like this, and she's the most recognisable of them.”

Si-on made a sound of assent and waited for the rest. Jae-joon paused.

“But the thing I'm more curious about right now is something else.”

“What?”

“How are you planning to sing it?”

Si-on didn't catch up in time. The seam in Jae-joon's voice split open.

“Ding-dong ding-dong, tick-tack tick-tack, hoo-hoo — ah, I've come back to where I started.”

He recited the onomatopoeia flat, then went on, voice gone dry:

“Boss Baek. You're going to get behind a mic, with that voice of yours, and sing this?”

“Or is it a contrast-gag thing? Cold, hard man beltin' a children's song? I'll grant you, that's a lane with no competition—”

Si-on didn't hear the rest. He'd already hung up.

He sat there looking at the screenshot of the lyrics, the curl of the keyboard light, and started from the top line again. Ding-dong. Tick-tack. Hoo-hoo. Round and round. Warning-warning. Empty, empty.

He closed his eyes and ran the picture through his head: himself standing in front of a microphone, delivering those words.

“Ding-dong, ding-dong~”

The image was so calamitous he couldn't bear to run it a second time.

No wonder. That was the chill at the back of his neck earlier. It wasn't that the words were bad — it was that the words were too her.

In IU's mouth, these lines were quick and teasing, loneliness wrapped snug in cuteness. In his mouth, they'd be a car wreck. A man who'd just spent two months living off debt-collection rounds and walked straight out of a violent film he'd acted in, leaning into a microphone to whisper ding-dong ding-dong, tick-tack. That wasn't a contrast gag. That was a public disturbance.

He looked at the screenshot a long time.

A thought surfaced from the corner of his head.

Did IU take a shine to this song?

He had no proof. But those reduplications, those onomatopoeia, that habit of folding loneliness into the cadence of a children's tune — it read like it was tailored to her own voice. As though, while writing it, she'd had her own throat in mind.

Suspicion, though, wasn't evidence. She'd written it to his brief, and every line fit the picture he'd described: the convenience store, the street lamp, the empty can, two in the morning. Not one image out of place.

The expression of it was IU's, not his. That was all.

He locked the screen, unlocked it, locked it again. Three times.

Then he made a decision. Stop worrying about the motive, solve the technical problem first.

He redialled Jae-joon. Two rings, picked up.

Baek Si-on → Jung Jae-joon · night · Hapjeong-dong

“Boss Baek.”

“Ask you something.”

“Go.”

“The reduplication and onomatopoeia in the lyrics — can they be handled?”

There came the creak of a chair on the other end, as if Jae-joon had leaned back into it.

“Three options.”

“First one's vocal slicing.”

“What?”

“Cut the vocal up after it's recorded. Not use it in one piece — take scissors to it.”

“What scissors?”

“……Scissors on the software. You cut a finished vocal line into single-word fragments, then rearrange, pitch-shift, layer them, and stuff them into the arrangement as a texture. You've heard electronic tracks with a voice in them you can't quite make out? That. So the ding-dong and tick-tack never need to be sung straight.”

Si-on thought about it. He had heard that — in convenience stores, in cafés, that shimmer of a human voice floating above the beat like broken glass.

“So the onomatopoeia just stops being me singing.”

“Right. Sliced, it isn't a performance anymore. It's a sound effect. It's got less to do with your timbre and more to do with my arranging.”

Si-on nodded, though Jae-joon couldn't see it.

“That works. Second?”

“Sing it straight in your low register. Normally a voice like yours would be too heavy for these sounds — but if we stop chasing lightness and go the other way, sub-bass route, chest resonance, pressing the onomatopoeia down, the result could be a whole different animal.”

“And the third—”

Jae-joon drew the words out:

“—feat. IU directly.”

“Hand all the reduplication and onomatopoeia to her. Her voice is built for it. You take the verse and chorus, the narrative part, and she takes the hooks that need to be light. Two voices, one cold one warm, one heavy one light. Full contrast.”

Si-on leaned back on the sofa and looked up at the ceiling. Feat. IU. From a commercial angle, it was the most devastating of the three. A debut track from an unknown, featuring the hottest female soloist in the country — the phrase “IU featuring” alone would push the song onto a trending list before it ever released. From a practical angle, it was the hardest. Because it came down to whether she'd even agree. And given the way things stood between him and Lee Ji-eun, if he went asking her to feature, the reply he'd likely get wasn't yes or no. It was: please learn to use formal speech before you come to me with a collaboration.

He ran the three options through his head.

“Do the first one.”

“Vocal slicing?”

“Right. Slice the reduplication and onomatopoeia. Verse and chorus I'll sing normally. And run the second option's low-register treatment at the same time — cut two versions, we compare.”

“The third?”

Si-on thought for a second.

“Not for now.”

“Alright.”

Jae-joon didn't press for the reason.

“When are you coming in to record?”

“In a few days. My uncle's on a deadline — I have to stay with him.”

“Fine. I'll push the arrangement forward on my end. You come in when you can and we go straight to the booth.”

“Good.”

He hung up, put the phone down on the armrest, closed his eyes.

The three options were still circling inside his head. The safest wasn't always the best. The loudest wasn't always the right one. He wouldn't know which until he got behind the mic.

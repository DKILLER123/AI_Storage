Chapter Eleven

This Back Isn’t Quite Right

April The shoot room Make-up before the second concept

Both poster concepts — the one at the alley mouth and the one in the water — were shot without a hitch. That afternoon came the first real scene.

Si-on sat in the makeshift rest area. “Rested” was generous: two folding chairs at the corner of the alley and a plastic crate with a few bottles of water on top. Baek Jeong-hun wandered over with his thermos and glanced at the storyboard in Si-on's hand.

“How’s the prep?”

Si-on closed the board.

“Nearly there.”

“First scene, the waking. You good?”

“Yes.”

Baek Jeong-hun nodded, turned toward the cinematographer, then stopped and looked back.

“Don’t be nervous.”

“I’m not nervous.”

“I mean don’t make me nervous.”

✿ ✿ ✿

Make-up was done in the room where they were shooting. It wasn’t really make-up — no foundation at all. The artist just took a spray bottle and misted Si-on’s forehead and temples, conjuring the clammy sheen of a nightmare sweat.

Once ready, Si-on lay down on the mattress, left hand flat over his chest, right hand loose at his side. The cinematographer hoisted the camera above him, shooting down at his face from a high angle.

In frame, Si-on’s eyes were closed, the water beads on his forehead catching a thin sheen from the desk lamp. Baek Jeong-hun sat behind the monitor, headphones on, gave the frame a look, and nodded.

“All departments ready.”

The sound man raised the boom. The script supervisor stepped in front of the lens with the slate. Everyone in the alley fell still. Even the wind seemed to cooperate and dropped.

“Scene one, shot one, take one.”

“Action!”

The clapper snapped. And Si-on’s first scene of this life began.

Scene 1 · shot 1 · take 1 · the waking

A face on a ruined mattress

Nothing moves. Just a sweat-damp face, eyes shut, on a mattress. Quiet enough to hear the room tone in the boom operator’s headphones.

Then his eyelid jumps.

The brow gathers, millimetre by millimetre. The fingers of his right hand curl, then let go.

Breath turning shallow, quickening. Chest rising faster. Nostrils flaring faintly.

Seven, eight seconds of this.

Then — the breath stops.

Chest goes still. As if something in the dream swallowed it.

One second. Two.

Eyes snap open.

Pupils contract under the lamp-light, focus scattered. He holds there three seconds before the chest rises again. The first breath is deep, slow, and comes out through slightly parted lips, trembling.

Baek Jeong-hun sits behind the monitor, silent, watching.

“Cut.”

Baek Jeong-hun called it. The script supervisor looked at the director, then at Si-on. Baek Jeong-hun stared at the monitor, quiet a moment, then looked up.

“Good. Prep the next shot.”

Si-on lay there, eyes open, not getting up. He was keeping the emotion. The next shot ran immediately on from this one — Sang-hun wakes, scans the room, and finds his father lying in the next room.

Normally this would be a single continuous take. The terror of waking from a nightmare, the bewilderment of scanning around, the fury at seeing his father — three layers of feeling, uninterrupted. But they’d called “Cut,” forcing the emotion apart, for one reason: the crew was too broke. The next shot needed a slow camera pan, a left-to-right sweep giving Sang-hun’s subjective view of the room. And their single cinematographer, who was at that moment straddling the camera like a compass above Si-on’s head, had to pull down, re-rig to Si-on’s eye level, swap the lens, re-rack focus, re-light. A three- to four-minute job at best.

Three, four minutes. For an actor holding the state of just waking from a nightmare, those minutes were harder than the shoot itself.

So Si-on didn’t move. He could hear everything — the metal legs of the tripod scraping, the sound guy clicking the boom back into place, Baek Jeong-hun murmuring with the gaffer about the new light angle — “tilt the lamp five degrees left.” But he refused to think. Not about whether the take was any good. Only about Sang-hun. About what Sang-hun saw every time he woke: the same stained ceiling, the same broken room, the same man passed out beside him, the man who ruined his life.

“Alright, that’s it.”

The cinematographer’s voice came from beside him. Si-on waited two seconds for the director’s voice.

“All departments ready.”

“Scene one, shot two, take one.”

The slate snapped. The camera, now at Si-on’s eye level, began a slow pan. In frame, Sang-hun’s world opened bit by bit — first the stained ceiling above, a crack at the corner running down to the wall like a dried-out river.

Then the camera swung right. Beside the mattress was an overturned beer crate serving as a nightstand. On it, a lamp with no shade, its bulb bare, next to a flattened cigarette pack and a disposable lighter. Further right, against the wall, several black plastic bags bulging with old clothes.

That was all Sang-hun owned.

The pan kept going, settling on the room next door, which had no door at all — a bed of bedding too filthy to read its original colour, and in it a human shape. Curled on his side, face to the wall, empty soju bottles scattered around him.

Sang-hun’s father.

In the frame, Si-on’s eyes fixed on that back. The light in them changed — from the fog of waking, to the recognition of who that was, to the rage that flooded up after the recognition. Three layers of feeling, like three gates opening in turn.

“Cut.”

Baek Jeong-hun called it. Si-on sat up on the mattress but didn’t stand. He knew what came next. The cinematographer started a third re-position — this time to the doorway, for the full-body shot of Si-on charging off the mattress.

Two minutes later, everything was set.

“Scene one, shot three, take one.”

The slate snapped. About a second and a half of quiet. Si-on stared at that back, the violent rise and fall of his chest stopping abruptly. Then —

“Ssibal, how the hell can you sleep?”

The words tore out of his throat, and the script supervisor’s pen dropped. Not from shock. The weight in the voice was too much. Anger is hot; it sprays outward. But the thing in that voice was cold, falling. Si-on shot off the mattress, barefoot on concrete, in a yellowed vest and boxers, through the doorless frame, throwing himself at the curled man.

“Cut!”

Re-rig. The next pair of shots ran twice. Not because Si-on had done anything wrong. An indie film has no stunt coordinator, no prosthetic make-up, no budget to fake a convincing-beaten face. Violence had to be built out of over-the-shoulder cuts.

First take, camera behind the father. Frame only showed Si-on’s front — the swing, the kick, the grab of the collar, every blow carrying real weight and momentum, but every strike stopping a few centimetres short. The camera shot only his upper body and face; the point of impact sat outside frame.

Second take, camera cut to behind Si-on. Frame showed only the father’s reaction — head snapping aside, body falling back, arm rising to shield his face. The two takes, cut together in post, became a punch thrown, cut, a head whipped aside — one fluid motion.

Last shot. The father curled on the ground, a swollen-red bruise on his face — the make-up artist had used a little blush and shadow, rough but serviceable. And yet there was no hatred in his eyes. Only a resigned wetness. He was crying. The kind where tears run out on their own while the expression barely changes. As if he’d grown used to being beaten.

The camera tilted up slowly. Si-on stood at the doorway, back to the lens, shoulders heaving, right fist still clenched. A few seconds of breathing, then he turned and walked out.

April The set The first look at the take

The scene was done. Si-on grabbed a crew jacket and slipped it on, walking quickly to behind the monitor to watch the playback. Choi Jin-ri, who had been sitting beside the director watching, stood at once and offered her little stool. Si-on didn’t stand on ceremony; he sat, eyes on the screen.

Baek Jeong-hun rewound the takes and ran them from the top. Si-on watched closely. Not with the actor’s self-examination, but hunting for a flaw.

Shot one, fine. Shot two, the pan, fine. Shot three, the cursing, fine. The two fight takes, match-cut correct, fine. Last shot —

Si-on frowned.

“Director, my back here — something’s off.”

Baek Jeong-hun paused it, freezing the frame on Si-on’s back as he turned to leave. In the frame the back was upright — shoulders open, spine straight, the step heavy but the posture held.

“What’s wrong?”

Si-on pointed at his own back on screen.

“Sang-hun is a man who beats people habitually. Physically, someone who punches all the time keeps their muscle tension chronically high — that makes them unconsciously hunch, round their shoulders.”

“And more importantly, psychologically.”

“He beats his father — it feels like revenge, like release. But after it, does that heal him? No. After it, he’s not only exhausted; he drops into a hollow self-loathing. That back shouldn’t be this straight. It should be slumped.”

“What do you think?”

By the time the words were out, Baek Jeong-hun’s hand was already on the rewind.

A moment later.

“Run it again.”

Si-on nodded, stood, and turned to say “You sit” to Jin-ri — but the words didn’t come. Because he saw her, standing beside the folding chair, and her whole body was off.

Director’s Eye · on the set

She wasn’t looking at the monitor, or at anyone. Her eyes were open, but the focus had gone out of them.

Shoulders turned inward, chin nearly to her collarbone. The posture belonged to someone else, not Choi Jin-ri.

The back is a whole biography if you know how to read it.

Si-on watched two seconds, said nothing, and quietly stepped around her, back into the shooting area, giving Baek Jeong-hun a “ready” signal.

“Scene one, shot five, take two. Action!”

“…”

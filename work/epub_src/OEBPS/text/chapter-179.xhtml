Chapter One Hundred Seventy Nine

I’ll Be There to Save the Day

December 31 · 10 p.m., the lights go down One minute of sellout · one splice no studio approved · one a cappella breath in a hall built for echoes · one countdown shouted into a phone camera The Temple Theatre, Los Angeles · New Year’s Eve, from the first roar to the gold confetti A deputy editor finds a headline in the third row; a singer pays his debts in encores, unreleased choruses, and dumplings; a girl shouts herself hoarse from the mid-right section and hears one word meant for exactly one person; and a year ends the way the best ones do — with everyone’s hair full of gold.

At ten o’clock, the lights went down — and the roar of three thousand people nearly took the roof off the old Moorish Revival building.

In the third row, on the aisle, sat two spectators who did not quite belong.

The deputy editor of TIME, and his photojournalist.

For days they had trailed Baek Si-on through radio stations and commercial appearances, had kept vigil outside studios, and had come away with plenty of material.

But all of it was side profile — the Asian interloper moving through the machinery of the American music industry.

Tonight was different.

Tonight was the first time they would watch him, on American soil, make good on his popularity.

Three thousand tickets.

Sold out in one minute.

For a Western A-lister, that number would be nothing special.

Taylor could do it.

Bruno Mars could do it.

Adele could do it.

But Baek Si-on was Asian.

A Korean singer, an Asian man, in one of Los Angeles’ core venues — and three thousand Americans had willingly handed him their New Year’s Eve.

Watching the figure wash in stage light, the deputy editor suddenly had a title.

TIME · the deputy editor’s notebook · third row, aisle seat

Next Generation Leader.

Written before the encore, before the confetti, before the magazine’s art department had any say in it.

Baek Si-on’s current trajectory and circle of influence could no longer be captured by a phrase as small as Asian pop idol.

What he was doing was this: in a market that had never truly opened its doors to an Asian face, he was building his place with work instead of gimmicks — one brick, then another brick, then another.

The deputy editor wrote the line into his notebook, and looked up at the stage.

Baek Si-on stood at the microphone, guitar slung on his shoulder, watching the crowd. The cheering ran on for a long time. He let it run before he spoke.

“I hear some of you paid three times face value to scalpers.”

He steadied the mic stand.

“Keep the money next time. If you honestly have that much to spare — go buy some coconut water. It’s good for you.”

The house exploded into laughter. Everyone knew he had signed the sports-drink endorsement two days ago; the joke needed zero subtitles.

Then he sang everything he had.

The Temple Theatre · the setlist — everything he has, in one night

‘Legend’ — the opener

‘Bones’ — hot on its heels

‘Sign of the Times’ — and the hall goes quiet

‘Love Yourself’ — three thousand phone lights at the first note

Between songs: no forced banter, no milked sentiment. One or two lines that made people laugh, then straight into the next track. The pacing held like a metronome.

When the last song was done, Baek Si-on lifted the microphone off the stand and walked to the very front of the stage.

“That’s all my songs.”

The house wailed.

“But you haven’t had enough yet, right?”

The cheering detonated all over again.

He walked to the edge of the stage and crouched down.

“Then let’s play a game.”

“You shout titles. I’ll cover them. Anything.”

“As for the copyright — Scooter’s been looking awfully idle lately. The man could use the work.”

The catcalls doubled. Three thousand people began screaming song titles at the stage with everything they had.

Middle of the house, right-hand section.

Choi Jin-ri had her hands cupped around her mouth and was shouting with the last of her strength. But three thousand voices, poured together, made her volume a single cup of water decanted into the ocean.

Beside her, Yoon Hye-ja glanced over. Choi Jin-ri noticed nothing at all; every ounce of her attention had been slammed stage-ward.

On stage, Baek Si-on tilted his ear to the noise for a while — and out of the tangled chaos he caught the word that was being screamed more than any other.

“Furious 7!”

He picked up the microphone.

“‘Furious 7’?”

The answer nearly brought the roof down. Two days of news had whipped the internet’s appetite right to the edge.

Baek Si-on pretended to think it over.

“That one is still an unreleased track. Studio rollout plan, confidentiality clauses — there’s a whole apparatus attached.”

A collective wail of grief.

“But.”

He let the pause sit.

“You all came all this way. I can’t send you home with empty ears.”

“A piece of it, then.”

The cheers erupted a third time.

“One condition.”

He raised a single finger.

“You can listen. You can memorize it in your head. It does not end up on the internet.”

A babel of overlapping promises rose at once.

Baek Si-on turned and walked to the band, trading a few low sentences with the guitarist and the keyboardist.

‘See You Again’ had a chorus of only a few lines — and the rap verse, he could not sing with Wiz’s flavor no matter how he swung at it. Forced, the effect would have been strange.

So he decided to splice another song into the seam.

To prove his worth these past days, Charlie Puth had shut himself in the little black box of a studio and written a dozen demos in one sustained sprint. He had not quite reached the terrifying peak-era output of the milk-tea-sipping Heavenly King surnamed Zhou — but it was more than enough to make Los Angeles’ paid-to-do-nothing slacker producers so ashamed they’d want to jump into the Pacific.

And the one Baek Si-on picked was the most finished of the dozen.

‘One Call Away’.

A song about loving someone at a distance — two people separated by miles, with nothing between them and the missing but a phone call.

The melody is light. The lyrics are warm. It suits New Year’s Eve.

And it suits a certain someone who flew thirteen hours from Seoul, sat in the stands shouting until her voice went raw, and had not managed to beg so much as one hug.

Baek Si-on came back to the microphone.

The piano went first. The restless crowd — primed all evening for the Furious 7 theme — went, at the sound of that intro, miraculously quiet.

It was the most ordinary piano figure in all of Hollywood scoring, the one everyone has heard a thousand times too many. But when Baek Si-on opened his mouth, the air in the entire theater changed.

‘See You Again’ · the promised piece

“It’s been a long day without you, my friend”

“And I’ll tell you all about it when I see you again”

“……”

Some of the Americans below had no idea what the Fast and Furious movies were even about. They couldn’t have told Paul Walker from Vin Diesel in a lineup.

They didn’t need to know.

Those two lines of melody, and the almost-broken tail of the note on “my friend,” were already enough to make a perfect stranger’s eyes sting.

That is what music is for, sometimes.

Language is the redundant part. Melody is the bridge. And the feeling inside a voice is the one passport that crosses every border without a visa.

Front row, a white kid in a baseball cap was still holding the BodyArmor he’d collected earlier, its cap half-twisted, the boy frozen in place.

He had come to hear ‘Legend’.

Instead, an unreleased movie theme had pinned him to his seat and put him through a memorial service ahead of schedule. Christmas was just past, the New Year not yet begun, and life had suddenly been arranged very heavily by an Asian singer.

Was that reasonable?

Not especially.

But it sounded good.

And just as every emotion in the building was about to follow those two lines down into the deepest part of the valley, the keyboardist’s hands rose off the keys.

The piano stopped.

The bass stopped.

The drums stopped.

The entire band might have been muted by a single finger pressing one button.

Three thousand people looked at one another, uncomprehending.

Baek Si-on took the microphone off its stand and stepped forward. No accompaniment. Nothing but his voice.

‘One Call Away’ · a cappella, no safety net

“Call me, baby, if you need a friend”

“I just wanna give you love”

“Came in from across the distance”

“Reaching out to you, so take a chance”

His voice in the bare, open theater had nothing on it at all. Every entering breath, every small vibration of the vocal cords, lay stripped and exposed in front of three thousand people.

‘One Call Away’ · the promise

“No matter where you go”

“You know you’re not alone”

Here the melody stepped upward. The piano rejoined it, laying a thin layer of chords down in the low register.

‘One Call Away’ · the title line

“I’m only one call away”

“I’ll be there to save the day”

The deputy editor of TIME whipped his head toward his photographer.

“OMG!”

“The way he rides the emotion from one song into the other — it’s frictionless! That is a director’s instinct! Genius!”

He glanced at his photographer, who was gazing at the stage in a rapture of his own, and frowned.

“Did you get that on camera?”

The photographer looked wounded.

“He said no filming…”

The deputy editor: “……”

He looked at the stage. He looked at the photographer. In the end he swallowed the words then film it with your soul. TIME had its standards — they did not extend to coaching a man, out loud, to defy an artist’s one request.

On stage, the guitar and the drums eased back in, the soft notes arriving exactly in time to carry his rising tail.

‘One Call Away’ · the last word

“Superman got nothing on me”

“I’m only one call away”

“……”

Across the house, the moment Choi Jin-ri heard Superman got nothing on me, her heartbeat missed its footing.

Superman.

That word was too particular. Too particular to file away as an ordinary lyric and let it pass.

That passage written in a diary. The secret only she knew. The childish, utterly sincere little fantasy about a Superman that she had once, secretly, saved just for herself.

How could he sing that word?

Was it a coincidence?

Or — was this song never for the three thousand people in this hall at all?

The stage light fell on Baek Si-on. Below, the house was a drift of moving silhouettes, three thousand faces swaying in crossed bands of light and shadow. And in one of those instants, Choi Jin-ri was certain that she and he had found each other’s eyes.

She didn’t know whether it was an illusion. Maybe it was just the sweep of a follow spot manufacturing a misunderstanding.

But a girl’s heart has never run on evidence.

❝

A heart that had to submit paperwork and sit through approvals before it was allowed to flutter — that wouldn’t be called falling for someone. That would be called an administrative process.

— mid-right section, somewhere around the word ‘Superman’

❧ ❧ ❧

“……”

The last note came down. Baek Si-on moved the microphone away from his mouth. The theater stayed quiet for about two seconds.

Then the sound of three thousand people hit the stage like a tsunami.

“One more time!”

“Sing it again!”

“BAEK!”

“One more time!”

“……”

Baek Si-on stood at center stage and looked down, smiling a little. Not much of a smile. But enough that several front-row fans pressed their hands to their chests, wearing the expressions of women just back from minor first aid.

He raised the microphone.

“I can’t sing this one again.”

Instant wailing. He waited it out before adding:

“Because that’s all of it I know.”

The house cracked up in one piece. The band was laughing back there too. Baek Si-on turned to look at the keyboardist, who spread his hands in an expression that said, plainly: don’t look at me — I was dragged into this crime on short notice, same as you.

The crowd started calling other titles. He picked a few and sang them in short pieces — some classic rock, some old pop. Time, inside emotion that steep, moved at roughly twice its usual speed.

At 11:59 p.m., the big screen above the stage switched to the countdown frame.

Baek Si-on pulled his phone from his pocket, opened the front camera, and turned the lens around — onto himself and the crowd behind him. Three thousand faces crammed themselves into one small screen. Some were laughing. Some were waving. Some were jamming glow sticks toward the lens with everything they had.

“TEN!”

He called the first number into the mic, and the house caught on in the same instant, roaring it back in unison.

“NINE!”

“EIGHT!”

“SEVEN!”

“……”

“THREE!”

“TWO!”

“ONE!”

“HAPPY NEW YEAR!!!”

Every light in the theater’s rig blazed on at once, and gold confetti jetted from both sides of the hall, settling into everyone’s hair, onto everyone’s shoulders, into everyone’s open palms.

2015.

Arrived.

People in the crowd embraced. People screamed. People kissed. Baek Si-on stood at center stage watching all of it, waited a few seconds, then brought the microphone back to his lips.

“Thank you for handing me the last day of 2014.”

The reply came back several decibels higher.

“In Asia, on a holiday for sending the old year off and welcoming the new one, people eat a traditional food. For luck.”

The American audience went dead quiet, eyes locked on the stage, patiently awaiting the details of some mysterious Eastern ritual.

“Dumplings.”

“It’s a filling wrapped in dough. It looks a little like…”

He thought about it.

“A little like a tiny pillow somebody pinched shut.”

Laughter rolled across the floor.

“So in a bit — ticket stub gets you one at the door.”

After a beat of silence, the whole house burst into the happiest cheering of the night. To these ticket-buying Americans, this was the greatest Easter egg of the season: a concert, and then mysterious Eastern food on the way out.

On stage, Baek Si-on thoughtfully added a public-service note:

“If dipping it in vinegar isn’t your thing, you can try the sports drink from earlier. I’ll admit that pairing sounds very strange.”

The audience collapsed in waves.

Hollywood gossip rags · tomorrow’s edition · as already typeset in Scooter’s head

“ASIAN SUPERSTAR POISONS 3,000 NORTH AMERICAN FANS WITH MYSTERY PASTRY”

Method, per the article’s imagination: aged vinegar crossed with coconut water. Casualty estimate pending tomorrow morning.

❧ ❧ ❧

Behind the stage, Scooter watched the monitor feed and massaged his temples in resignation.

He had exactly one wish left tonight: that none of the dumpling-eating Americans spent tomorrow discovering, personally and repeatedly, what aged vinegar mixed with coconut water does to a digestive system.

Otherwise the gossip rags would run the poisoning headline by lunch. At the picture his own head had just typeset, Scooter closed his eyes in pain.

Out front, the cheering rolled on and on.

Choi Jin-ri sat in the drifting gold, watching the bowing silhouette inside the spotlight, her eyes curved into two pretty crescents.

Whether or not Superman had been a misread — this New Year’s Eve could not have been better for her.

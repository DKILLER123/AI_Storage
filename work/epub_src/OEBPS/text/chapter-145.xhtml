Haute Couture? Serve It Mix-and-Match

AMAs day · late morning Round three of Franco-Italian profanity, interrupted Los Angeles · the Four Seasons, Beverly Hills · the eighth-floor corridor Two men fighting usually stop for three reasons: exhaustion, conscience, or a third party walking in.

Two people fighting usually stop for only three reasons: the strength runs out, the conscience wakes up, or a third party walks in.

The first two take time.

The third works on the spot.

At that moment, Marc and Vincent had reached the stage where the mixed French-Italian profanity was on its third rotation.

Ding.

The elevator arrived.

The doors opened, and a third task force walked out.

At its head: Adrien, the head of public relations for Balmain North America. Behind him, two assistants pushing a garment rack.

The moment they cleared the elevator, they saw the state of the corridor: the two North American public-relations directors of Tom Ford and Gucci, elbowing each other in the ribs, their racks jackknifed together, four assistants plastered to the walls not moving a muscle.

One of Adrien’s assistants poked her head around and asked quietly:

“Director — what do we do?”

Adrien took his eyes off the battlefield, examined the corridor’s geometry, and said:

“Go around. That side.”

The assistant nodded instantly, and the three of them hugged the opposite wall, steering the Balmain rack wide of the war zone.

Marc and Vincent stopped at the same instant.

They watched the Balmain rack swing around the far corner.

The next second, both men let go almost simultaneously.

Marc looked down and straightened his collar. Tugged his cuffs back into line. Combed the stray strand at his temple flat with one finger.

Vincent hauled his tie back around from behind his shoulder and retied the Windsor from scratch.

The four assistants woke as if from a dream — bending to gather the fallen dust covers, re-draping the exposed suits, checking hangers, trueing the wheels.

Fifteen seconds, and both racks had recovered the full dignity of we are haute couture, not bumper cars.

Outside Baek Si-on’s suite, three garment racks now stood in a row.

Tom Ford. Gucci. Balmain.

Three public-relations directors stood in a line in front of them, and nobody moved.

Because whoever rang the bell first had, in momentum terms, already conceded half a head — it amounted to telling the other two, I want it more.

So all three hands came up at once.

Ding-dong.

The door opened.

Baek Eun-ah took in the three immaculately suited, perfectly composed brand publicists with their racks, then looked down at the small tuft of black velvet thread the corridor carpet had not fully surrendered.

She was silent for a beat.

And pretended not to have seen it.

Marc spoke first.

“Good morning, Miss Baek.”

His smile was pure grace. Nothing about it suggested that two minutes ago he had been driving his elbow into another man’s ribs.

“Tom Ford has prepared three looks for tonight’s AMAs stage and the red carpet — including the upgraded edition of the silver-grey satin-line suit Mr. Baek already wore at Staples Center, the one the entire internet has been discussing.”

Vincent followed immediately.

“Gucci has prepared two red-carpet looks for Mr. Baek — inspired by Michelangelo’s David and the aristocratic tailoring of the Florentine Renaissance. The fabric is exclusive hand-loomed velvet.”

Adrien smiled faintly.

“Balmain would like the moment Mr. Baek steps into the spotlight tonight to tell everyone he is a champion — not an executive who just got off work.”

Marc looked at Adrien.

Vincent looked at Adrien.

“……”

Very good.

The war had escalated.

She stepped aside.

“Please come in.”

The three brand contingents filed in, and the suite’s living room became a small-scale menswear battlefield. Baek Si-on sat on the sofa watching three rows of black dust covers drawn open one after another, while all three directors delivered their pitches at once — Marc in English, Vincent in English salted with Italian, Adrien in English salted with French — three accents interweaving into a single pot of language stew.

Baek Si-on sat. Eyes closed. Temple throbbing.

After about thirty seconds, he opened his eyes and looked once at Baek Eun-ah.

She understood in a heartbeat.

“I’ll go get Ji-hun oppa.”

A few minutes later, Park Ji-hun arrived — and the three publicists, seeing the person who actually decided the look, swivelled their firepower onto him at once.

Park Ji-hun listened for roughly five seconds. Then he turned to Baek Eun-ah.

“Translate for them.”

“Say what?”

“Shut up.”

“……”

“You’re serious?”

“Completely.”

Baek Eun-ah glanced again at the three publicists. One of Tom Ford. One of Gucci. One of Balmain. Men before whom Korean fashion-magazine editors bowed at the waist. And now Park Ji-hun wanted her to translate: shut up.

She took a deep breath. Fine. Work was work.

She rendered it in English, as politely as the language allowed:

“Stylist Park asks that everyone please hold off on talking for the moment — he needs to look at the clothes in silence. Should anyone wish to keep talking, he suggests you’re welcome to wait outside.”

Marc’s jaw moved a fraction, as if lining up a rebuttal; he swallowed it whole.

Vincent’s eyebrow twitched once; nothing came out.

Adrien’s expression never changed at all; he simply, quietly, took half a step backward.

The living room fell silent.

Baek Eun-ah had a delicate little epiphany. She had always assumed luxury-brand publicists sat fairly high up the food chain. Today she learned the true apex predator was the stylist who could silence them. And the stylist’s power flowed from one source: Baek Si-on.

Park Ji-hun ignored everyone’s faces. He walked to the racks.

Tom Ford first: he pinched the shoulder line between two fingers, ran the suiting between thumb and forefinger. Then Gucci: he lifted the deep-green velvet coat, drew out an ivory silk shirt and checked the collar. Balmain last: the black-gold embroidery was genuinely beautiful — beautiful to the point of being loud.

He beckoned.

“Boss Baek — come try them on.”

Baek Si-on rose from the sofa.

the suite · three racks, opened in order fitting one

Gucci Deep-green velvet suit, buttoned to the second button, over a Botticelli-print silk shirt — the whole temperament turns soft: relaxed, vintage, gentle. Less a red carpet than a private party somewhere in Italy, one arm already around an heiress he has charmed into forgetting the time.
Tom Ford Silver-grey double-breasted, wide shoulder, nipped waist, trouser lines like a blade — the air pressure in the room changes the moment he stands in front of the mirror. Power. Muscle. Aggression. Male hormone and social class made physical at the same time.
Balmain Long black coat, gold embroidery driving from shoulder to chest, clasps flashing with real menace, heavy work at cuff and waist — impact strong enough to reverse-kill the flashbulbs on both sides of the carpet. Right for a concert. Right for the King stage. Right for announcing from a high platform who doesn’t have work tomorrow. For a red carpet, too loud — it reads as arriving to buy the AMAs.

The deep-green velvet and the Botticelli shirt — fitting one, softness first.

All three directors read his face as he tried them — reading faces being the first skill of their profession — and every one of them saw it: the stylist’s eye had already landed on Tom Ford. So had Eun-ah’s.

Baek Eun-ah drew Park Ji-hun aside and dropped her voice into Korean.

“Ji-hun oppa.”

“Mm?”

“We can’t let Tom Ford start thinking Oppa is their fixed asset. Eggs, one basket — and this particular basket also brawls with Gucci in hallways.”

Park Ji-hun considered.

“So — pick Gucci?”

“Can’t we pick all of them?”

“……”

He turned back to the three racks — and this time he didn’t look at them brand by brand. He looked straight through the three rows of clothes, deconstructing and re-dealing.

From Tom Ford’s side: the silver-grey double-breasted jacket and its matching trousers. From Gucci’s: an ivory-white silk shirt, collar cut looser — just loose enough to shave the kill-aura off the Tom Ford silhouette. From the Balmain rank: a gold-embroidered brooch and the black-gold narrow belt.

“This one.”

The room went still. All three directors froze at once.

Marc frowned first — then saw the main architecture was still Tom Ford, and softened a degree.

Adrien saw his own brooch and belt selected: not the full look, but present on the body. Barely acceptable.

Vincent’s face was the best show of all.

“You can’t wear it this way.”

It finally burst out of him.

“Haute couture carries exclusivity. A complete look is a brand’s narrative. Taking it apart and reassembling it like this is vandalism of brand integrity.”

Baek Si-on, in the middle of undoing a cufflink, looked up at him.

“That’s your rule. Not mine.”

Vincent was stopped cold.

Truthfully Baek Si-on didn’t fully understand why Park Ji-hun had decided to serve him couture à la carte. But he knew one thing: this was the man’s rice bowl. Styling belonged to the stylist. He was responsible for wearing it.

Marc finally laughed. He extended a hand toward the door.

“Vincent — you can take your sacks and roll.”

“You—!”

Vincent pointed at him. It wasn’t that he couldn’t stomach mixing per se — Gucci had played that game too. What truly hurt was that Gucci’s final selected item was the shirt. The shirt! The shirt worn inside the suit! What could a camera sweep even see? A collar? A cuff? The faint shimmer of silk when he lowered his head?

He held it in for a long moment, then couldn’t help pointing at the ivory silk shirt.

“At least change the jacket to Gucci.”

Marc’s laugh went cold.

“In your dreams.”

Adrien offered, unhurried:

“If you two intend to keep this up, I suggest Mr. Baek simply wear full Balmain tonight. At least we have never turned a corridor into the Colosseum of Milan.”

Marc and Vincent turned to look at him together.

Adrien raised both hands.

“I’m merely proposing a peace plan.”

Park Ji-hun ignored all of them. He hung the final assembly on a single rail.

the final look · three houses, one body decision

Tom Ford The silver-grey double-breasted suit and its trousers — the hard architecture, the power line.
Gucci The ivory-white silk shirt, collar two buttons open — shaving a degree of the suit’s commercial menace off at the throat.
Balmain The black-gold narrow belt and the gold-embroidered brooch — lifting the whole assembly one floor toward “stage” and “champion.”

The final assembly — Tom Ford architecture, Gucci at the throat, Balmain at the waist.

Not any single brand’s complete narrative. Very much Baek Si-on.

Baek Si-on studied the mirror for a few seconds.

“This one.”

All three directors shut up at once — because the person who could actually decide had spoken.

Marc adjusted his expression first.

“Tom Ford is honoured to be the main body of tonight’s look.”

Vincent picked it up instantly:

“Gucci will appear in the position closest to Mr. Baek’s skin.”

Adrien wasn’t about to be left behind:

“Balmain will be why Mr. Baek is remembered under the flashbulbs.”

“……”

Very good.

All three houses had found their angle of self-consolation.

Nobody had won outright. Nobody had lost clean. By fashion-industry standards that counted as peace. At the very least, nobody would be elbowing anyone’s ribs in a corridor again.

AMAs day · 2 p.m. One rented Cadillac, one unscheduled motorcade Los Angeles · the Four Seasons · the hotel entrance The plan was a hired Cadillac. The plan did not survive contact with Hyundai.

At two in the afternoon, the Cadillac Scooter had rented for Baek Si-on was already waiting out front — black bodywork, doors polished to a mirror, a driver in full chauffeur livery standing at attention.

Ariana Grande had arrived too. Today’s red-carpet build was exquisite: a short white dress, long boots, a ponytail wound high — a small pop bomb, pre-fed on flashbulbs.

Scooter Braun stood by the car confirming the departure plan.

“You ride with Baek. It concentrates the carpet traffic, and the media will be far happier to max out SB Projects’ presence tonight.”

Ariana Grande turned her head and smiled.

“So I’m your traffic tool?”

Scooter Braun spread his hands.

“Sweetheart, you have always been a very expensive traffic tool.”

She rolled her eyes — and right then the hotel’s revolving door pushed open, and Baek Si-on walked out. The silver-grey Tom Ford jacket carrying the wide shoulder and the waistline; the Gucci ivory silk shirt open two buttons, draining the look of its coldest boardroom authority; the Balmain black-gold brooch and narrow belt pressed at the centre, leaking a first preview of the King stage’s throne-room authority.

Scooter was about to nod with satisfaction when a convoy of black cars came around the hotel entrance and stopped. The lead man was out and moving before the engines settled; he reached Baek Si-on and bowed.

“Mr. Baek. After you.”

He opened the rear door of the middle vehicle.

“……”

“……”

The Cadillac driver went blank too.

Baek Si-on looked at the flagship of Korean automotive engineering — the Equus — and said:

“I endorse the Aslan.”

The man explained instantly:

“Mr. Baek — the Aslan is a Korea-exclusive model, so there is no Aslan fleet available for deployment overseas at present.”

“The Equus is the Hyundai Group’s highest-grade executive sedan, and the closest overseas substitute to the Aslan’s positioning that we are able to provide.”

“……”

Baek Eun-ah could parse this dialect of corporate diplomacy now. Translated: the Aslan never entered the American market, so please accept a more expensive car.

Scooter Braun looked at the four Equus sedans, then at the rented Cadillac beside them.

“Baek.”

“Mm?”

“Is Hyundai planning to escort you from the hotel door all the way to becoming the President of South Korea?”

Baek Si-on let that one pass. He turned to Ariana Grande.

“Together?”

She blinked — then laughed. The particular upward laugh of a small woman looking up at a very tall one.

“Sure. I’ve never ridden a Korean state car.”

Scooter watched her climb in, looked once more at his rented Cadillac, held a silence for two full seconds — and told the driver, decisively:

“You can have the rest of the day off.”

The driver’s face went empty.

Capitalism is cruel. Even cars get laid off without notice.

AMAs day · the drive Palm shadows, one Instagram, and five thousand likes Los Angeles · the Equus, Beverly Hills toward the Nokia Theatre The rear seat is wider than advertised, and the passenger is already working.

The Equus’s back seat was wider than Baek Si-on had imagined. Thick cushions, superb sound insulation, leather that didn’t bite, air-conditioning set to exactly right. He leaned back, and one honest thought surfaced:

I’ll say this much — the presidential-grade car really is better than the Aslan.

A sentence that must never reach the Hyundai Aslan team, or the freshly mended relationship would crack down another seam.

The convoy pulled out of the Four Seasons colonnade, four black Equus merging into Beverly Hills toward the Nokia Theatre, palm-tree shadows falling across the windows in strips. Ariana Grande tilted her head at him.

“Are you a very big deal in Korea?”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

“What do you mean?”

“There’s the helicopter, and the convoy, and your country’s President pinning a medal on you, and now a car company sending a motorcade to fetch you to the AMAs.”

AMAs day · thirty minutes on The convoy turns snake, and the queue begins Los Angeles · the Nokia Theatre perimeter · the holding zone Two hundred metres from the door, in line, per the organizer’s order of descent.

Thirty minutes later, the Nokia Theatre’s perimeter. The convoy had left the hotel in a straight line and arrived at the AMAs as a snake. Ahead of the red-carpet entry lane sat the security corridor; every artist-bearing car queued in the temporary lot in the organizer’s order — who descended first, who last, who mid-show, who in the finale slot, all decided by guest rank and tonight’s rundown.

Guides waved the four Equus into position about two hundred metres from the main entrance. Kang, the bodyguard, confirmed by walkie from the lead car with Scooter’s Equus:

Baek Si-on gave a small mm.

Ariana Grande yawned a small yawn.

“Last Grammys I waited twenty-two minutes. Twenty-two minutes staring at a car roof. Genuine hell.”

Baek Si-on glanced at the Equus’s ceiling — brown leather laid with silver, a hidden ring of ambient light at the centre. It was, in fairness, a little more interesting than the Four Seasons’ ceiling.

He did not say so.

The entrance had been orderly enough. Then this convoy appeared, and the press zone visibly shifted. Normally one car delivered one artist plus a few assistants. Tonight: four black state sedans in single file — less a pop-awards arrival than a national delegation that had rerouted through Los Angeles. The reporters went from puzzled to thrilled. The fan barrier began to seethe. Some rose on tiptoe. Some raised phones. Some had already started booing.

AMAs day · the same hour Through the thick door, into the quiet Los Angeles · the Nokia Theatre · the backstage corridor One greeting, one forced shoulder-bump, and a pitch that dies in the hallway.

After the wall photos, staff steered them toward the backstage corridor at once. Outside, the carpet was still detonating — flashbulbs, chanting, the hostess’s interview voice stacking layer on layer. Inside the thick door, the sound lost most of its body, leaving a distant low-frequency tremble.

Ariana Grande let go of his arm.

“I have to go change for my set.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Me too.”

They were about to split toward their rooms when a Black rapper came up the corridor from the far end — black jacket, chain, sneakers, the particular looseness of hip-hop posture. The instant she saw him, Ariana Grande’s working smile turned into something sweeter and more private.

“Sean!”

Big Sean came over, arms open, and hugged her; they kissed, quite naturally.

Baek Si-on’s expression didn’t change. An American backstage has very American standards. He adopted local customs and studied the AMAs wayfinding board on the wall.

She turned back, took Big Sean’s hand, and made the introduction:

“This is my boyfriend, Big Sean. Sean — this is Baek Si-on.”

Big Sean’s eyes lit up.

“Yo! Shiwen!”

He flipped on the familiarity of a man who had known you for three years, hand out, body already driving forward — clearly setting up the American bro shoulder-bump. Baek Si-on had no bump in him. He extended his hand.

Big Sean froze for half a beat — but nobody in hip-hop fears an awkward moment. He simply seized the offered hand, pulled it toward himself, and force-completed half a handshake-shoulder-bump. Baek Si-on’s shoulder tapped his, lightly. He stopped. Looked at him.

Baek Eun-ah’s eyelid jumped. In Korea, anyone who dared yank Baek Si-on like that would be murdered three times over by a manager’s eyes alone. But this was America, where backstage social distance sometimes ran as close as a shared kindergarten.

Big Sean, oblivious to the shift in the air, patted his arm with enthusiasm.

“Bro, I am sitting on some absolutely insane beats right now.”

“You sing the hook, I come through with a verse.”

“And if you’re down, we can even pull Justin in — the three of us would own next year’s entire chart!”

Baek Si-on blinked through the barrage. His impression of Big Sean was shallow: the man had rapped on Justin Bieber’s “As Long As You Love Me,” and beyond that he had no file. So when a virtual used-car salesman of a beat pitched “insane beats,” “Justin,” and “owning the charts” at him in one breath, he genuinely couldn’t keep up.

Ariana Grande was mortified down to her toenails. She had asked her boyfriend to say hello — not to launch a floor show of venture solicitation in an AMAs corridor. She tugged Sean’s arm gently.

“Sean — now really isn’t the time for this…”

“No worries, Ariana.”

Baek Si-on finally spoke, looking at Big Sean:

“If there’s a serious proposal, have your team send the offer to my manager, Scooter.”

For someone of his temperament, routing you to the manager was already a tactful rejection. But Big Sean missed it entirely — he even nodded, delighted.

“Course, bro! I’ll talk to Scooter.”

He pointed at Baek Si-on.

“Trust me — this is gonna be crazy.”

Baek Si-on gave one nod.

“Have a good show tonight.”

Then, to Ariana Grande:

“I’m off.”

She exhaled, and the smile came back.

“See you out there.”

He turned and followed the staffer toward his dressing room. Big Sean’s voice trailed after him:

“Yo, King! We’ll talk!”

Baek Si-on didn’t look back; he lifted a hand a few degrees, which counted as an answer. Past the corridor’s corner, the sound finally faded. Baek Eun-ah lowered her voice beside him.

“Oppa — do you know him?”

“No.”

“Do you want to work with him?”

“No.”

The answer came with not one gram of hesitation. She nodded. Understood: American rapper, project pitch, failed.

Baek Si-on had of course not known that the man he just declined would one day take a Billboard 200 number-one album. Even knowing, he wouldn’t have said yes. Collaboration isn’t a thing you owe to whoever will be famous later, whoever holds resources now, whoever can summon Justin on the phone. He disliked being swept along on somebody else’s enthusiasm.

He disliked, even more, being pulled by three sentences in a backstage corridor into a so-called chart-dominating project whose beats he had never heard, whose contract he had never seen, and to which he had no connection at all.

Charts can be ruled.

But you don’t just climb into any car that stops for you.

Especially a car whose steering wheel, as far as he could tell, was held by a man who had force-bumped his shoulder within one minute of meeting him.

Chapter Eighty-Seven

A Presidential Telegram Isn’t as Hot as Soju

The evening of the return Budae-jjigae, then duty-free Mapo · Jeong-hun’s · then Yeonnam-dong Four hundred fifty million. A Blue House telegram. A lecture at K-Arts.

Mapo. Baek Jeong-hun’s house. Budae-jjigae bubbling in the middle of the table. For a director who had just sent a first film into Venice main competition, he should have been one of the busiest people in Korea today. From landing to now, a few hours: Showbox, Lotte, the distribution giants almost killing his battery. Everyone urgent on The Green Fly’s domestic expansion. And the acquaintance-directors who had never looked at this ragtag outfit in the independent circle were lining up texts, under the pretext of a welcome drink, asking in the open and in the dark for a sip of soup under this new Venice darling.

Jeong-hun put the phone on silent and threw it on the tea table. Pushed every engagement. He did not want to hear anything. He wanted to sit at his own table, quiet and comfortable, and have a family.

“Not picking up?”

“Mm.”

Wife Kim Hae-yeon looked at him once, green glass bottle, filled his small cup with Jinro.

Kim Hae-yeon

Baek Jeong-hun’s wife · Baek Eun-ah’s mother

OC · soju, a new house, wait for you

She pours. She kisses a corner of a mouth with a daughter still across the table. She asks how much the overseas rights actually clear. Then she tells him to come.

“So this overseas rights thing. How much do we actually make?”

“Overseas agent takes a fixed twenty. Then subtract noona’s principal, and the profit split she’s owed by ratio. Odds and ends all in. Clean in my hand, about four hundred fifty million won.”

Hae-yeon heard the number, nodded.

“Looks like we can think about a new house.”

“Fine. You look. Sign when you decide.”

The words down. She turned and kissed the corner of his mouth. Jeong-hun ducked half an inch, embarrassed, eyes desperate toward the other side of the table.

“The child’s here. Hold it.”

Hae-yeon looked at Baek Eun-ah. Bowl in her hands, two eyes round over the rim, watching them.

“Eun-ah.”

“Mm?”

“Go to your hyung’s.”

“…”

Jeong-hun coughed.

“All right. You go wash. I’ll finish the drink.”

That was to Hae-yeon. She took bowls toward the kitchen, bent at his ear as she passed.

“Waiting for you~”

Eun-ah’s eye-roll went to the ceiling. Three hundred sixty degrees. Back. She stomped to the living room, sat into the sofa, remote, television on. Fine. Two minutes first. They needed time to wash anyway.

KBS evening news. “…This afternoon, actor Baek Si-on and director Baek Jeong-hun arrived at Incheon International, warmly received by a large number of press and fans…” Cut to airport. The instant Si-on came through the automatic doors, flashes from both sides, dozens of mics from every direction. Eun-ah watched hyung on screen walled in by reporters, then her own figure beside him, pushing a trolley, squeezing crooked through the crowd. Image management still a long road. Cut again.

KBS · evening news

Blue House telegram

The Blue House this afternoon sent an official telegram of congratulations to Mr. Baek Si-on and Director Baek Jeong-hun. The President highly praised the two filmmakers’ outstanding achievement at Venice, saying they had greatly raised the cultural standing of the Republic of Korea and shown the world the depth and force of Korean culture.

“Appa!!!”

Eun-ah toward the table.

“Look! The President named you!!!”

Jeong-hun turned to the screen. Not a scrap of being officially flipped as a favourite. A disdainful snort.

“Hypocrite politicians.”

“Appa, you don’t take it at all? They at least praised you on national news.”

“Standard whitewash.”

Eun-ah looked at her father, a little unhappy. Like most Korean young women her age she still had a worship of Madam Park. First woman president in Korean history. A woman who broke a ceiling. That she had walked to that place at all was, she felt, already remarkable. As for governing, policy — not-yet-twenty Eun-ah did not have much judgment. Jeong-hun put the chopsticks down, looking at a daughter not yet much beaten by society.

“I ask you. What does art cinema talk about?”

“Social reality. Where does that come from? Defects in the system. Inaction by the people in power.”

“You think a president, seeing a film that exposes the sores under her rule take an international prize, would sincerely congratulate?”

Eun-ah had nothing. His logic, she felt, had no hole. The resentment was just too large.

“Appa, you’re a bit contrary.”

Jeong-hun could not be bothered to keep pulling this deep question with a brat. Waved as if shooing a fly.

“Go find your hyung.”

“I’m going!”

Eun-ah off the sofa, television off, coat from the entry, sneakers, door. Before leaving she looked back at Jeong-hun.

“Old angry youth!”

Door slammed. Jeong-hun heard the footsteps going down the corridor, shook his head. Hae-yeon, hearing the noise, put her head out from the bath.

“She gone?”

“Gone.”

“Then come here.”

“…”

✿ ✿ ✿

Same evening After Mapo Yeonnam-dong · Yoon Hye-ja’s table Duty-free. A faculty meeting that clapped. Dean Choi wants a lecture.

Yeonnam-dong. Professor Yoon Hye-ja at the table, in front of her the haul Si-on had carried back from Charles de Gaulle duty-free. La Mer cream. Chanel No. 5. Two bottles of SK-II. Sulwhasoo ginseng set. Hermès scarf. She looked at each.

“De Gaulle?”

“Mm.”

“You know how much cheaper this is at Lotte duty-free?”

“…Eun-ah already said.”

“Don’t buy in Europe what Korea can buy. Exchange plus tax. You threw away hundreds of thousands.”

“Got it.”

“Bought is bought. I won’t return it.”

Sulwhasoo to one side, Hermès to the other, sorting extremely clear. Keep, keep. Give, give. A professor’s work always had order. Si-on opposite, watching her finish the haul, waiting. He knew what he was waiting for. Praise would not come. Something else would. Indeed. Last bag folded, beside the chair. Water he had poured, a sip. Then she opened.

“You said my name at the ceremony.”

“Mm.”

“You know what happened these two days?”

“No.”

“Monday morning I went to school.”

Cup down.

“Professor Park from economics blocked me in the corridor. Asked if she could have your autograph. Her daughter’s a fan. Sixteen. Cried all night at home saying she’d go to Incheon to meet you. Her mother stopped her.”

Si-on: “…”

“Afternoon faculty meeting, Dean Lee stood up in front of a dozen professors and said, specially, ‘Professor Yoon, congratulations. Your son did Korea proud.’”

She stopped.

“The whole room clapped.”

Si-on looked at her face. The professor’s expression did not change.

“I’ve published papers. Supervised graduates. Three teaching-excellence prizes. Nobody ever clapped for me at a faculty meeting for those.”

“My son takes a prize in Italy. They clap.”

“…”

“I’m not unhappy.”

“I need time.”

Si-on heard what was in it. Put plainly: she was not yet ready, in the head, for the identity “a public person’s mother.” Before, she was Professor Yoon Hye-ja. Taught economics, ran projects, took students, went for rank. Her social identity she had stacked paper by paper. Now, suddenly, “Baek Si-on’s mother.” Colleagues’ eyes changed. Not negative. Just too fast. Fast enough that a middle-aged woman used to slow academic life had not had time to adjust her breath.

“I know.”

Yoon Hye-ja nodded.

“One more thing.”

“What?”

“Yesterday afternoon Dean Choi of K-Arts drama called me.”

“And?”

“He said the college has been in an uproar over you winning at Venice. He wants you, when you have time, to come to Seokgwan-dong campus and give a special lecture to the third- and fourth-year elite.”

“Me?”

Si-on pointed at himself.

“To lecture?”

“Mm.”

K-Arts. Korea National University of Arts. The only national arts university under the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism. In popular knowledge the ace school of Korean entertainment was Chung-Ang. Acting and film there had produced too many Hallyu stars — Kim Hee-sun, Ha Jung-woo, Hyun Bin, Park Shin-hye, Shin Se-kyung. Pull the alumni list and you had half of Korean entertainment history. In the pure-art circle, K-Arts crushed Chung-Ang. Chung-Ang taught you how to be a star. K-Arts taught you how to be an artist. Those two sounded close. In essence they were not the same at all.

“Lecture on what?”

Yoon Hye-ja repeated Dean Choi’s original:

“The children are too restless now. Their worship of your son has reached a dangerous degree. Dangerous enough that they’ve started to disdain the representational technique the school has always pushed. They think that set is outdated, useless, not as real as going up and feeling hard.”

Meaning: K-Arts students, having seen Si-on take Venice Best Actor, had reached an extremely dangerous conclusion. No need for “given circumstances” and “emotional memory.” If you were real enough, in enough, feeling hard enough, you could act. Si-on took Best Actor — wasn’t that proof? The thought spreading in the college like wildfire. Dean Choi was urgent. He needed Si-on himself in front of those children, telling them in his own mouth: you understood it wrong.

“Dean Choi thinks I can correct them?”

“Dean Choi thinks those children worship you. What you say, they’ll hear. A professor saying it a hundred times is less than you saying it once.”

“When?”

“You set the time.”

Si-on nodded once.

“All right. I’ll go.”

“Then I’ll answer Dean Choi.”

“Mm.”

Yoon Hye-ja picked up the phone and started a message.

Chapter Two Hundred Nine

Favoritism Needs Technical Merit

Mid-April

Afternoon

The dorm — a shoe cabinet, and three seconds of deliberation

Mid-April. The ‘Ice Cream Cake’ promotions had entered their back half.

There were still schedules, rehearsals, overdubs, shoots. But at least it no longer meant napping in the van in the precise thirty-second windows between one red light and the next, the way it had right after the comeback.

That afternoon, for once, nobody had split the five of them five ways between broadcast stations and event organizers. They stood at the dorm door in a complete formation, about to go record the BodyArmor ad song at Jung Jae-joon’s studio.

Before leaving, Irene spent three extra seconds in front of the shoe cabinet.

The shoe cabinet, as surveyed

Inventory: several pairs for dance practice. Several for daily wear. A few that looked beautiful and were not built for standing in for long stretches of time.

Her gaze swept across all of it and came to rest — very naturally — on the low-top Under Armour collaboration trainers.

Put on. Laced. Stand. One glance down in the mirror: very comfortable. Which was, she told herself, the entire reason for choosing them. The recording booth meant hours on her feet, and ankles must not get tired. Collab models were unremarkable in a work setting. And these had been a gift from a sunbae — wearing them to a recording session in collaboration with that very sunbae was, logically, airtight. The convincing work had been executed to a very high standard. Then she walked out of her room.

Joy was waiting on the living-room sofa. She looked up, gave Irene one scan, and her gaze slid downward — and stopped at shoe level.

“Ohh—”

That dragged-out syllable was packed to the brim with bad intentions.

“Unnie’s wearing that pair today.”

Irene didn’t answer. She simply walked past the sofa to get her bag. She didn’t make it two steps before she felt the gaze behind her — working like a thermometer, pressed flat against her ankle, taking its reading.

“And here I thought Princess-nim would never wear them~”

Irene looked back at her once. Joy’s smile froze at the corners.

Yeri poked half a head out of the hallway and asked, quietly:

“What’s going on?”

Joy adjusted her expression at the fastest speed of her career and turned to her:

“Nothing. Work equipment.”

Yeri nodded. She understood nothing, but her instincts told her this was not the moment to press.

Mid-April

Afternoon

Nonhyeon-dong · the corridor outside the glass

While the manager signed them in at the building’s front desk, the five of them stood in a row in the corridor. Through one pane of glass, the inside of the recording room was visible.

Through the glass, as watched

Goo Hara at the microphone — headphones clamped over half her face, a lyric sheet pinched in one hand, wearing the expression of a person passing through a personal low point.

Baek Si-on at the mixing console, back to the corridor. Expression invisible — but his hand was visible, rising every few seconds to tap one precise, unpitying point.

Jung Jae-joon beside him: waveforms scrolling past line by line on his screen, one section circled, enlarged, replayed — and a headshake.

The glass cut off the sound. It did not cut off the picture.

Knock knock knock — the five of them bowed in perfect unison at the door.

“Sunbae, good afternoon.”

Baek Si-on heard the voices, pulled his headphones off, and gave them one look.

“Sit first.”

His gaze swept across the five of them — and at the pair of shoes on Irene’s feet it stopped, for less than a second. Then it withdrew, and he turned back to the recording room.

“Give us a bit.”

“Yes.”

As she sat down, Irene crossed her ankles lightly and angled the shoes into what she judged to be the most flattering position.

In the booth, Goo Hara’s new take was beginning. ‘2002’. It did not survive long — Jae-joon hit stop almost immediately, and Baek Si-on pressed the talkback key:

“Every time we get here, you try to charm your way through it.”

Goo Hara glared at him through the glass.

“I am charming.”

“The song isn’t buying it.”

Jae-joon, folding the knife in from the side: “Pitch correction can save your intonation. It cannot save your attitude.”

Goo Hara: “……”

She closed her eyes, drew one deep breath, and raised a hand: ready.

The correction loop, as administered

“That line was flat.”

“Too much breath on the entrance.”

“Don’t hide from the tail note.”

“Again.”

Goo Hara, approaching structural collapse: “Nobody has ever ground me down word by word like this in a recording session.” — “Which is why your old recordings turned out merely fine,” Baek Si-on said. — “YAH!”

The Red Velvet members had arrived in the relaxed spirit of “come watch a sunbae at work.” Watching, their expressions gradually changed.

Nobody’s changed more than Yeri’s. The lyric sheet in her hands had acquired a crease from being squeezed. Her eyes ran from the recording room to the back of Baek Si-on’s head and back — until she finally couldn’t help it and gave Irene’s sleeve a small tug.

“Unnie.”

“Mm?”

“Are we… going to get ‘like this’ later too?”

The meaning of “like this,” as enumerated

The phrase was, in fact, extremely rich. Including but not limited to: word-by-word retakes. A sunbae indicating problems with no change of expression whatsoever.

And — the small matter of lines like “pitch correction cannot save your attitude” landing directly on you. Irene glanced at the brand-new maknae. Yeri was genuinely nervous; her lips had pressed into a line. “Don’t be scared.” — “But Baek sunbae looks so strict…” — “If it’s not good, we do it again. He doesn’t eat people.” — “Oh…”

Irene turned back to the recording session. She was, in truth, nervous herself. But that was not something the little ones could be allowed to see.

❧ ❧ ❧

Twenty more minutes passed before the door of the recording room finally opened.

“Huh? You’re all here?”

Goo Hara’s beaten-down expression lit up instantly at the sight of juniors.

“Sunbae, hello~”

The five bowed in a rush.

“Thank you for your hard work.”

“You too, you too.”

Goo Hara waved a hand and said:

“Today I seriously considered, for the first time in my life, whether I debuted on my face alone.”

Several girls lost the fight against their laughter. Yeri, still rigid, felt the corner of her mouth come loose at that one.

“The finished track sounds decent, though.”

A rare flicker of guilt crossed Goo Hara’s face.

“The process involved some… mm… technical assistance. But the timbre is mine at the base, so strictly speaking, it was still me singing. Right?”

❝

Nobody answered the question. The question did not require an answer — answering it would have been the cruelest available option.

— the room, exercising mercy in perfect silence

At that point Baek Si-on’s voice drifted over from the recording-room door.

“Don’t scare the juniors.”

Goo Hara looked back at him and stuck her tongue out in a face. He regarded her, expression flat. She was not the least bit afraid of that face; she turned back and addressed the five girls standing at full attention, earnestly:

“Drinks are on me when you’re done!”

With that she waved, slung her bag, and headed off down the corridor at a light bounce. Two steps out she turned around and shouted one more thing:

“Fighting!”

And then she was actually gone. The looseness she’d stirred into the atmosphere receded slowly as the door closed again.

Baek Si-on looked down at the lyric sheet in his hand, in no hurry to speak. Jae-joon archived Hara’s track and turned to the room.

“Alright — your turn. Who’s first?”

The five looked at one another on reflex. According to their private expectations, today was supposed to be: pick up the part sheets, listen through once, and proceed the standard way — main vocal opens, the others layer in, record it down gradually.

Seung-wan reacted fastest and stepped forward.

“I’ll start.”

Main vocal. Standing first at a moment like this was instinct, and duty.

The door shut. Seung-wan put the headphones on, adjusted the mic height, and nodded at Baek Si-on through the glass: ready. The backing track came up — and the instant her voice entered, the pen Jae-joon had been spinning stopped dead.

The tryouts, as graded

Seung-wan — steady. Extremely steady. Intonation that needed almost no repair, breath economy clean, dynamic arc under control; from the first line through the chorus to the tail, a line drawn in one smooth stroke, no wasted bends, no sudden breaks. Jae-joon, under his breath: “The foundation on this one is excellent.”

Seul-gi — a shade thicker, diction crisp, rhythm solid; a few spots landing slightly heavy. The verdict taken entirely in silence: a few more strokes on the lyric sheet.

Joy — the entire color of the song changing at once. Sweet. Very sweet. A timbre with a smile built into it: whatever she sang, you believed she was in a good mood, and it took no effort to picture exactly how she looked when she smiled. “The sense of placement on that is good.” One “mm”; no dispute.

Yeri — young, bright, a green-apple voice; set at the transition from cold-sweet to tender-sweet, it fit exactly.

And last, Irene.

❧ ❧ ❧

When all five had finished their tryouts, Baek Si-on looked down at the lyric sheet. Jae-joon leaned in, and the two of them traded a few quiet judgments. It didn’t take long — most of it had already been decided the moment the tryouts ended.

Baek Si-on picked up the pen and drew several new lines across the sheet.

“Over here.”

The five gathered round. Seung-wan’s eyes went down the page and stopped.

“The first line… is Irene unnie?”

Irene herself blinked. By the normal conventions of a girl-group song, the opening line goes to the main vocal, or to the most recognizable voice. No honest arrangement math pointed at her first.

Joy leaned in to look too.

“It really is.”

Yeri didn’t dare crowd in; she studied everyone’s faces from the side, secretly.

The opening-line logic, as defended

“This song can’t start at the top and slide down. It has to stack — from light into fullness. That’s what makes the ear believe the song is worth more with every bar that passes.”

Seul-gi and Seung-wan exchanged one look. The two of them understood immediately: all they had to do was arrive at exactly the moment they were meant to arrive — and in that moment, everyone would know. Oh. All that groundwork up front was waiting for this. A far more advanced architecture than opening with a display of technique.

Irene understood it too.

But was it really only “auditory layering”?

Of course it was.

Every judgment Baek Si-on had just made, from the tryouts to the part assignment, had been calm, professional, precise — with not one gram of personal feeling showing.

And so what if it was?

A valid professional rationale, and privately feeling that she had been looked after — these two things had never once been in conflict.

Irene read the assigned lines again.

The first line. The first voice. Also the first face that would appear in the advertisement itself.

It was a position so good that even “favoritism” could be filed under “professional selection.”

Irene thought:

Sunbae had used the single most faultproof method available to save the best bite of the song for her.

The thought was dangerous. She knew, roughly, that she was overthinking it again.

But so what?

Overthinking had never required a sunbae’s permission.

❝

Favoritism, to be allowed to exist, must carry technical merit — and the finest kind is the favoritism that arrives with airtight engineering, so nobody can flag a single fault in it.

— the assignment sheet, favoritism filed under technical merit

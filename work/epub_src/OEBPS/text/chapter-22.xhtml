Chapter Twenty-Two

One Hundred Kilograms of Silence

June Noon Outside the DSP building A street of the dead and the broke

The midday sun sat dead overhead, bleaching the street white. The street trees outside the SM building drooped, a sick green. Now and then a car rolled past, tyres grinding the asphalt and dragging a curtain of heat behind it.

When Si-on pushed out of the building's side door, the back of his flower-print shirt was already a dark, soaked square.

Eun-ah trailed two steps behind, canvas bag slung, footsteps dragging, the toes of her shoes nearly skimming the pavement.

The two of them walked on along the pavement.

Neither spoke.

In the silence Eun-ah stole a glance at her cousin. His gait betrayed nothing — still hands-in-pockets, still unhurried.

She opened her mouth, closed it. I should say, “Cousin, was that too blunt back there?”

That was SM's canteen. Trainees, office staff, managers, producers — who didn't eat there? You'd pointed your chopsticks at a Girls' Generation sunbae and lit into her in front of dozens of people. You'd had your fun.

But you were leaving. Jin-ri wasn't.

She still had to practise in this building, make her comeback, run into the same people day after day. And Kim Hyo-yeon, with that temper — after taking a public loss that big, if she wasn't going to vent on you, who would she vent on?

Four years at SM had taught Eun-ah one rule above all: you could dislike a sunbae, but you could never let one lose face in public. Not because the sunbae was right. Because you couldn't afford the fallout.

But the words turned over in her throat, ground up, rebuilt. She wanted to say it. She wasn't sure. Then she was sure, but had no strength to unpack it. And even if she did, with her cousin's temper, he'd likely answer “hm” and keep walking.

What finally came out was:

“Cousin, I'm sleepy.”

Which was, at least, the truest physical feeling she had right then. Si-on had caught two hours in a jjimjilbang; she'd been up from the football without closing her eyes once.

“Tired? Go home and sleep.”

“What about you, cousin?”

“Gym.”

Eun-ah's feet stopped for a beat.

Gym.

After putting away that whole tableful of food, under a thirty-degree sun, he was going to the gym.

She ran the behaviour through her head once, translating it.

Mm.

He was definitely angry.

But she said nothing more. The exhaustion rolling through her brain no longer allowed her to assemble any sentence requiring a subject, verb, and object. Her eyelids were sagging, her temples throbbing. If she didn't find somewhere to lie down soon, she feared she'd collapse outright on the sidewalk in Apgujeong-dong and make tomorrow's headlines as “former SM trainee fakes a hit-and-run against the company.”

At the crossing, Si-on stopped. He pulled a loose wad of bills from his wallet without counting, and pressed them into Eun-ah's hand.

She looked at the money, about to push it back.

“Go back, get some real sleep, then go to the market and pick up a few things.”

His tone was matter-of-fact, like a supervisor handing off a task. No room for argument.

Eun-ah hesitated for a second, took it, folded it, and tucked it into the side pocket of her canvas bag.

“…Thanks, cousin.”

Si-on didn't answer. He raised a hand and flagged down a taxi. An orange Hyundai Sonata pulled in at the kerb, and he bent to give the driver an address, then opened the rear door and tapped Eun-ah on the shoulder. She folded in, and the instant her seat hit the upholstery her head went slack against the window. Her eyelids shut faster than the car door.

Si-on watched the taxi merge into the traffic and roll off down Apgujeong-ro toward Gangnam-daero.

June Above Apgujeong station A body pays for its owner's sins

The gym was on the second floor above Apgujeong subway station — a chain. Day pass, fifteen thousand won.

He dropped twenty thousand on the counter and walked in without waiting for change. Flower-print shirt still on.

Straight to the treadmill first.

Then he crossed to the free-weights area.

The squat rack.

A hundred kilos.

A man with headphones on the neighbouring station shot him a glance. Probably figuring a guy in a flower shirt lifting iron wasn't something you saw every day.

Si-on ignored it. Down. Up.

The burning as muscle fibres tore filled his head and drowned every other thought.

He needed this pure, dumb gravity. Because a hundred-kilo barbell doesn't argue with you, doesn't weigh right and wrong, and most of all doesn't murmur, in a very small voice, after you've just laid into someone, “She's not actually a bad person.”

You press it down, you carry it. You can't — then you sit under it.

✿ ✿ ✿

June The street The afternoon sun turns crueler

An hour and a half later.

The afternoon sun had turned crueler than noon. When Si-on walked out of the gym, the shirt could no longer be called a shirt. It was a wet rag.

He stood a few seconds in the shade by the door, then walked to the kerb and hailed a cab.

“Yeonnam-dong, Mapo-gu.”

The instant the door clicked shut, the air-conditioning slapped his face. Si-on sank into the back seat, no seatbelt, head thrown back, eyes closed.

He wasn't asleep. He just didn't want to open his eyes.

The cab passed Sinsa-dong, swung onto Eonju-ro, crossed Hannam Bridge — the glint of the river's surface reached him even through his eyelids. Then off the bridge, a left turn, and as it took the Hannam-dong curve, Si-on cracked one eye open.

You didn't loop this way for Mapo.

He glanced at the meter, said nothing, shut his eyes again.

The cab stopped at an intersection, waiting out a long light. The driver gave the wheel a small wrench, and the car gave a slight lurch. Si-on's eyes opened, and, without any intention, he turned his head toward the window.

✿ ✿ ✿

June The grey building A placard in blue and white

At the roadside stood a six-storey building in grey. The outer face was tiled in beige, but a good many of the tiles near the top rows had come away, leaving the raw grey plaster beneath — a face spotted with age. The ground floor was a big entrance hall with sliding glass doors, an expired fire-inspection sticker still fixed to the frame. Above the roof hung a signboard.

Blue backing, white lettering.

DSP Media.

Si-on's gaze caught on it, and then he saw the people below.

More than twenty. Mostly young women, standing on the pavement outside the building, taking up a good stretch of the street. Some held support banners, some yanked a long horizontal placard taut, some crouched at the edge of a planter writing in marker on white card.

The banner was big, the lettering huge — readable even across the four-lane road and through the glass.

Outside DSP Media · afternoon

KARA is not an experiment

Stop the KARA Project.

Respect Nicole and Jiyoung.

KARA isn't a lab rat.

Hands off our name.

KARA · KARA · our name is not for sale

A corner of the street, a squad of fans, and a company that decided a hole wasn't a hole so long as you could fill it with something.

Si-on watched for two seconds.

“Driver, stop here.”

The driver flicked his indicator and pulled over. Si-on handed over a twenty-thousand-won note, waved off the change, and got out.

The heat wrapped around him instantly. After the cab's conditioned air all the way here, the temperature difference hit all at once, and a thin film of sweat broke out on his back.

He crossed to the other side of the road and stood under a ginkgo tree.

Hands in his pockets.

Watching from a distance.

A few of them had red-rimmed eyes. A girl with a ponytail sat on the kerb clutching a hand-written card, dragging the black marker across it letter by letter. Beside her stood a slightly older girl, short hair, wearing a mask and a baseball cap, leaning on a lamppost, a stack of flyers in her fist — few handed out.

Si-on pulled out his phone and opened Naver.

Search bar. KARA.

The results fell down the screen, most recent first. The top few:

Industry wire · live

DSP Media announces survival program “KARA Project” — one of seven in-house trainees to be selected as a new member

Fan statement · circulating

KARA fans issue joint statement: strongly oppose “KARA Project,” will refuse all DSP-affiliated products

Si-on slid his thumb slowly down.

January, Jung Nicole had left. Contract expired, no renewal. The stated reason, “a long-term divergence between her personal direction and the company's plan.”

Translated into plain speech: what she wanted to do, DSP wouldn't let her; what DSP arranged, she didn't want. Six months of talks. No agreement. Gone.

April, Kang Ji-young went too. Same contract expiry, but quieter than Nicole. She said she was going to Japan to study, wanted to act, thanked the fans, and asked everyone to keep supporting the remaining members. The statement was polite, not one heavy word in it.

Two gone, five down to three.

DSP had three paths in front of it — carry on as a trio. Disband. Or add someone new.

They picked the third.

Not just adding — building an entire survival show to do it. They dragged seven of the company's trainees out in front of a camera and made them compete for a slot in KARA. The program was called KARA Project.

Si-on tapped open the comments under one of the articles.

Kamilia_1997

KARA is a name Gyuri, Seungyeon, Nicole, Jiyoung and Hara built by pouring out four years of themselves. You're running a survival battle with it for a bunch of trainees nobody's ever met. Do you even deserve that name?

pearlpeach_2014

How is the person who wins supposed to live with herself? Putting on the KARA name while every old fan resents her — what kind of life is that?

nicole_only

This company can't even manage what it already has, and now it's shopping for replacements like they're livestock.

Si-on read the last comment, lifted his head, and looked across the road at that grey, peeling six-storey building.

DSP Media.

His old company.

That contract he'd signed at fifteen — in a meeting room at the end of the third floor of this very building. Square table, leather chairs, curtains drawn, the fluorescent light a sick blue-white. Two copies. One on file, one in his own hands.

And seated beside him that day, his father, Baek Jeong-hwan.

A Level Five officer in the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism. He'd worn a grey suit that day, the first button of his shirt buttoned neatly. After he signed, he'd clapped his son on the shoulder and said one thing.

“The Korean Wave is national policy. Go eat this wave.”

He'd never gotten to eat the wave.

And then the man was gone.

Si-on slid the phone back into his pocket and let his gaze drop off the blue-and-white sign. The ginkgo's shadow had stretched a little longer in the afternoon sun, and the scattered light fell across his flower-print shoulder, shifting leaf by leaf, one at a time.

He stood in the shade a while longer.

Then he lifted his foot, crossed the road, and walked toward the building.

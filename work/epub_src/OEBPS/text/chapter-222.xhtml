Chapter Two Hundred Twenty-Two

Beauty Begins With Being Seen

The elevator doors opened onto the underground garage.

Baek Si-on walked in front.

Irene followed, holding a distance from him that could be explained either as happening to be going the same way or as following.

A black high-roof Hyundai Starex waited to the left of the elevator bank.

Low-key from the outside: dark privacy glass, polished wheels — a business van, nothing more, if you judged by shape.

The driver slid the door open.

Irene bent to climb in.

Halfway into the bend she discovered bending was unnecessary.

The roof stood at least twenty centimeters taller than she’d priced it.

She walked in upright.

The layout inside was nothing she had imagined. Independent captain’s chairs, wrapped in leather, backrests and leg rests power-adjustable. The aisle between the seats was extravagantly wide — she could have shuffled sideways two full steps in it.

She had spent half a year in Red Velvet’s van.

That vehicle routinely carried five girls, one manager, three styling bags, and at least two pairs of stage shoes with nowhere to live. Sleeping meant a crooked neck on a U-pillow.

Baek Si-on’s van was closer to a hotel room that moved.

Irene settled into a captain’s chair.

Her finger grazed the armrest. Soft leather, fine stitching.

Her peripheral vision found the row of buttons on the side of the seat — but which one owned the backrest and which the leg rest, she could not say.

She didn’t dare press anything.

The seatbelt took a while to find, too; it turned out to be hidden in a slit in the seat’s flank.

Baek Si-on sat beside her, replying to messages on his phone.

He didn’t look up.

He also didn’t expose her.

May 15

Lunch — two hours on the meter

Yeouido · an Italian restaurant with six tables and a half-open kitchen

The drive wasn’t long. The destination was a street near Yeouido.

The Italian table, as Korea built it

Korea had plenty of Italian restaurants, but for years the orthodox ones lived inside hotel annexes. What the public knew as “spaghetti” had long since been nativized into a hybrid of sweet ketchup, cream sauces, and kimchi rice cakes.

Then ‘Pasta’ aired in 2010 — Gong Hyo-jin, kitchens, chefs, the bite of the noodles, the orthodox methods — and suddenly it was all pop conversation. A wave of young cooks went to Europe, and around 2014 and 2015 they came home and opened dining rooms. Seoul’s orthodox options finally thickened.

This place was of that wave: few seats, half-open kitchen, table spacing priced like a rumor of expensive rent.

At midday the room was thin; the front of house was quiet enough to hear the chopping from the kitchen.

Baek Si-on and Irene were shown to a semi-enclosed alcove at the back. The bodyguard and driver took the open seats out front.

Irene sat and accepted the menu.

Opened it. The dish names were all Italian, footnoted by a rank of English descriptions she understood just as well.

Two pages in, her thumb had already begun drifting south, toward the phone under the table.

That was when Baek Si-on spoke.

Italian.

A string of dish names, delivered to the waiter.

Irene’s phone retreated back up her sleeve of a pocket.

After the antipasti and two mains were ordered, Baek Si-on tilted his head.

“Any restrictions?”

“No cilantro.”

Baek Si-on confirmed something in Italian with the waiter, then added a few requirements — nothing with heavy notes, no garlic, no onion.

On-camera this afternoon; complaints from Park Bo-gum were to be avoided.

The waiter wrote it all down.

And Baek Si-on added one last line:

“Same standard, one more set for the three outside.”

The waiter nodded and withdrew.

Irene’s gaze followed him a stretch of the way before coming back.

“You speak Italian too, sunbae?”

Baek Si-on spread his napkin on his lap.

“No.”

“But you just—”

“Only the menu.”

He had spent nearly a month in Venice. During a festival you needed, at minimum, three things clear: what you were eating, what you wanted to eat, and what you refused to let the kitchen put in it.

Beyond that, essentially nothing.

“So sunbae’s Italian vocabulary is roughly one menu?”

“Plus ‘thank you’ and ‘the check.’”

Irene made a small acknowledging sound.

So even omnipotence had borders.

The border sat, approximately, where the menu ended.

❧ ❧ ❧

The antipasti hadn’t arrived.

Irene decided she could not keep silent forever. The longer neither of them spoke, the more the private room resembled a date.

Work was the safe topic.

“Sunbae — may I ask for advice about something?”

Baek Si-on tipped his chin: go ahead.

“There’s the ISAC in August. The company hasn’t decided what events I should enter.”

Her expression was monumentally earnest — a graduate student discussing a thesis topic with her advisor.

“What do you think suits me?”

She already had an answer in her heart. Archery. SM’s seniors had won whole news cycles at the ISAC with bows.

Baek Si-on did not hesitate for even one second.

“High jump.”

Irene’s face froze solid.

“…High jump?”

“You love dancing so much — your vertical must be decent.”

An image assembled itself in her head without permission:

The ISAC, as forescen

Herself in a tracksuit, sprinting down the runway before the entire idol nation and its cameras. Takeoff. Short legs laboring over the bar.

And if the jump failed — the whole person, rolled up into the landing mat like laundry.

The image was too beautiful. She refused to look at it any longer.

“Dance and high jump are completely different things,” she managed, in her own defense.

“Both are jumping.”

Irene bit her lip. The logic was so crude she couldn’t find the seam to attack it.

She looked down and straightened her napkin, and muttered, at a volume calibrated for an audience of exactly one — herself:

“Sunbae is such a bad boy…”

“What was that?”

Irene’s head came up, innocence deployed at full brightness.

“I said vegetables are good for you.”

Baek Si-on studied her face and did not pursue it.

He picked up his water glass, drank, set it down.

“The dances in the email — I’ll be checking.”

Irene: “……”

She had assumed the files were a casual fling of a gesture. Whether she ever opened them again would be a matter of mood; the normal path was for them to sink to the bottom of the inbox and be quietly buried under subsequent spam.

The three words I’ll be checking promoted the spam into formal coursework.

“When?” she probed.

“When I have time.”

That was worse than a date.

Because when I have time meant at any time. Tomorrow. Next week. Some deep night while she stood at the sink with a cleansing mask on, and a message would arrive without warning:

“Have you watched the chapter-three video yet?”

Irene finally understood one thing.

She had just classified Baek Si-on as a bad boy — and he hadn’t misheard.

He had elected to prove it.

She raised her water glass, took a long swallow, and began a profound self-review of why she had danced that routine in the KBS corridor at all.

❧ ❧ ❧

The antipasti arrived.

Thin-shaved raw beef, dressed with arugula and shaved Parmesan, ringed with olive oil and lemon.

At first Irene ate with restraint — knife and fork at regulation angles, bites trimmed small, a diner sitting for an examination that would be graded.

But it was genuinely good.

By the third bite her speed had returned to civilian levels.

When the pasta came she wound a forkful, bit down —

and her teeth met an altogether unexpected resistance.

She chewed twice, brow faintly contracted.

“Is this… undercooked?”

“That’s the orthodox texture.”

She chewed twice more.

“It’s a workout for the teeth.”

Baek Si-on offered no review of her teeth.

Halfway through the pasta, Irene thought of Seung-wan. The leader out here eating orthodox Italian; the main vocalist gnawing canteen trays at KBS.

Morality stung her, briefly.

The sting lasted approximately two more forkfuls of pasta.

Then she resolved to buy Seung-wan something nice tonight, by way of reparations.

❧ ❧ ❧

Meal over.

The waiter laid the bill in a black leather folder on Baek Si-on’s side.

Irene couldn’t see the number.

But extrapolating from the semi-enclosed quiet, the ingredient quality, and the service spec, the total was unlikely to be friendly.

She hesitated — then drew a credit card from her wallet and pushed it to the middle of the table.

“Let me get it today.”

Baek Si-on’s gaze landed on the card.

He extended one finger and flicked its edge, lightly.

The card glided back across the table and stopped in front of Irene, perfectly composed.

“Put it away.”

The flick switched her pride on.

“Why?”

“You can’t afford it.”

“I have a salary.”

“Save it for high-jump shoes.”

Irene: “……”

Back again.

This running gag was not surviving the day.

She put the card away. She did not, however, surrender.

She thought, then said:

“Then next time, it’s on me.”

“Next time? And what are you planning to buy me?”

Irene hadn’t thought that far. She could not possibly treat him at this tier; her credit card would file for its own restraining order.

“When sunbae has time, I’ll find something.”

Baek Si-on signed, and closed the leather folder.

“Deal.”

And so the next solo meeting fixed itself, without either party ever announcing it.

May 15

Early afternoon

Seongsu-dong · the office, lens-first

The Seongsu-dong office door swung open.

Baek Si-on’s first step inside met a phone, held sideways, lens less than thirty centimeters from his face.

He stopped.

Behind the phone: a face in round-frame glasses.

“What’s going on here?”

The glasses girl’s voice shook harder than her camera arm.

“We’re… recording work material.”

“Recording me being late?”

“N-no — building content for the company’s social accounts.”

Baek Si-on’s gaze moved from the lens to the person behind it, and then around the office.

Choi Na-young stood by the sample table. Two more girls sat at their stations, one typing hard at a monitor, one sorting a folder of shade cards. Everyone was conspicuously busy.

Baek Si-on closed the door.

“Explain.”

The glasses girl swallowed and started talking.

They wanted to run a “brand-startup diary” account — short, light videos and photos documenting the build of a new company from zero. Swatches, prototypes, packaging design, office life, the boss’s occasional cameo. Authenticity as the hook; a core following seeded before launch day.

Choi Na-young handed over a sheet of paper: the content directions they’d brainstormed.

Baek Si-on scanned it once.

The brand-startup diary — pilot slate, as pitched

“Week one inside Baek Si-on’s company — what does everyone actually do all day?”

“The boss walks in unannounced: should four new grads bow first, or close their shopping tabs first?”

“Baek Si-on is launching a color-cosmetics brand — but can the boss actually tell a gloss from a lipstick?”

“A brand from zero: we killed twelve more shades today.”

Pitch credit: Choi Na-young, Employee No. 1.

Baek Si-on put the sheet down.

“Whose idea?”

A brief silence from four girls.

Choi Na-young’s hand rose, slowly.

“Mine.”

Baek Si-on did not rate the idea’s sophistication.

He turned to Baek Eun-ah:

“Note it down.”

Baek Eun-ah’s laptop was already open, on reflex.

“What?”

“Bonus for her. Two hundred thousand won, next month.”

Choi Na-young’s hand was still up.

“Two… two hundred thousand?”

Baek Si-on added a line:

“And from here on, every ten thousand followers that account gains, the team gets a million won. Split four ways.”

Baek Eun-ah’s fingers stopped on the keyboard.

“Is there a cap?”

“No ceiling.”

The four expressions flipped in a single cut — from startup with an uncertain future to please hand us the entire internet.

The glasses girl was already re-angling her lens.

The market girl at her station had begun computing what a hundred thousand followers came to in won.

❧ ❧ ❧

The circus concluded, they brought Baek Si-on to the sample table.

Rows of unpackaged transparent tubes, each with a handwritten formula number and shade label — dozens of them, the survivors of days of swatch wars and internal debate. Two color families had made the final cut.

The swatch girl picked up the first tube.

“Shade one. For now we’re calling it ‘Vampire Cherry.’”

Baek Si-on unscrewed the cap and drew a stroke on a swatch card.

The innermost color was a just-bitten, overripe cherry, fading outward into glassy translucence — the sensual core of today’s gradient lip, minus the parched aftertaste of a traditional tint.

“Why vampire?”

“Because it looks like you just drank blood.”

“That does not sound like a cosmetics ad.”

Choi Na-young cut in:

“But young people will remember it.”

The second tube — the market girl lifted it.

“First Peach.”

Positioning: the exact opposite pole. Inner color a medium-saturation peach coral, blurring outward into translucent flesh-pink. Improved the lip without reading as makeup. Built for faux-bare faces, campus looks, the daily commute. Ageless, and forgiving across skin tones.

Vampire Cherry sold memory-hook and nightlife nerve. First Peach sold safety and all-day habit.

Between them, the two tubes covered evening dates and daytime offices, exactly.

Baek Si-on held the two swatch cards side by side, ready to greenlight both as the launch pair —

and Choi Na-young spoke up.

“Actually, the best result isn’t wearing either one alone.”

Baek Si-on looked up.

“Together?”

“Together.”

Baek Eun-ah’s instincts tripped an alarm the instant those words left Choi Na-young’s mouth.

She did not manage to escape.

Choi Na-young was already beckoning, smiling.

“Manager Baek — we need your face.”

“Why is it always me?”

“Because Manager Baek is the prettiest.”

Baek Eun-ah knew the sincerity of that sentence clocked in somewhere near the rounding error of her own monthly salary.

She accepted anyway.

“Begin.”

Choi Na-young laid down First Peach as the base — the light, watery film visually propping up the lip’s outline, gloss pooled at the peaks and the center of the lower lip, the whole mouth reading plumper, faintly puckered with moisture.

Once the light layer had half-set, Vampire Cherry made its entrance — the deep red placed only at the innermost edge, near the mouth’s center, blooming outward across the peach base on the water-glide of the film beneath.

The result assembled itself.

The two-tone lip, as layered

The light shade owned volume and translucence. The dark owned color-the-cheeks and the eye’s focal point.

Two colors fused into a single graduated, water-light fruit-juice lip.

Faster than the orthodox four-step rite — conceal, tint, blur, gloss — and landing perhaps eighty percent of its effect.

Baek Si-on studied Baek Eun-ah’s lips for a while, and nodded once.

“Those two are the launch.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Product direction settled, the meeting moved to price.

The market girl stood at the whiteboard and uncapped a marker.

“Recommended retail: nineteen thousand nine hundred won.”

Then she defended it.

The price, as defended

Nineteen thousand nine hundred still lives in the “ten-thousand-something” mental shelf. One glance at the tag, and the brain files it under the ten-thousand tier — never the twenty. Too cheap reads as teenager swag and erodes the brand; at nineteen-nine, the tube sits precisely in the sweet band above drugstore and below the luxury houses.

The cost side had been run too: CJ Logistics warehousing, packaging, and per-order delivery averaged around twenty-five hundred won; department and duty-free channels skimmed close to thirty percent; packaging, tax, marketing, returns, and wastage took their cuts. A 19,900 tag did not mean 19,900 to the company.

And because the two shades performed best layered — she drew a box on the whiteboard — a two-tube duo set would launch alongside: single at nineteen thousand nine hundred, the pair at thirty-four to thirty-five thousand. The set should feel like a bargain while quietly raising the basket.

Early on, these channels would carry only the base load. The real detonation point was ‘Descendants of the Sun’: if the drama caught fire in Korea and East Asia, the lead actress’s lip could replay the consumption wave that followed ‘My Love from the Star’.

She capped her marker and looked at Baek Si-on:

“Boss — how did it go with the production today?”

Five people in the office rotated toward Baek Si-on in unison.

Baek Si-on’s brain flashed through the 1.2-billion-won Song Hye-kyo plan, the sub-three-hundred-million Kim Ji-won plan, and his own exit line of let me think it over.

He cleared his throat.

“Let’s do the brand name first.”

The employees: “……”

Baek Eun-ah raised one eyebrow.

“So it didn’t go well.”

“The brand name.”

Everyone possessed the instinct to drop it.

❧ ❧ ❧

Choi Na-young picked a marker off the table, walked to the whiteboard, and wrote five letters dead center.

LAVUE.

“French. La vue — sight, the field of vision, what the eye takes in.”

She tapped the letters.

“This business is answering one question: when someone sees you for the first time — what do you want them to see?”

“So the brand thesis can be: beauty begins with the way you are seen.”

❝

Beauty begins with the way you are seen.

— LAVUE, brand thesis, as written on a Seongsu-dong whiteboard before the company had a name

Baek Si-on’s gaze stayed on the five letters.

Choi Na-young added the last layer.

“There’s also an easter egg. Say LAVUE fast and it walks into ‘Love You.’”

She didn’t bother explaining that layer’s commercial value. Everyone present understood: a brand name born with romance wired in would save multiples of its own registration fee across social spread, ad copy, and emotional marketing, for years.

Baek Si-on read it aloud once.

“LAVUE.”

The Chinese market could transliterate it phonetically, or lean on the Love You association and take a freer, meaning-forward rendering.

Choi Na-young continued.

“The launch line, we propose: LAVUE ONE.”

The whiteboard, as written

LAVUE ONE — one step, done. One tube, solved. Generation one.

Full name: LAVUE ONE Water-Film Glow Gloss.

No. 01 — First Peach.

No. 02 — Vampire Cherry.

Light first, dark second: the everyday base sells first, the layering shade follows.

Baek Si-on swept the whiteboard top to bottom.

From Rihanna’s one-liner at the New York after-party — stop letting the big houses freeload off you — to now:

The skeleton, as of today

Contract manufacturer: negotiated.

Warehousing and logistics: landed.

Department store and duty-free channels: reserved.

Price: set.

Hero products: chosen.

Brand name: exists.

Social channel: has a direction.

The skeleton is standing.

He nodded once.

“We’ll call it that.”

Choi Na-young’s mouth twitched first — then she and the others cheered at the same time.

From this moment they were no longer “Baek Si-on’s nameless cosmetics thing.”

They had a name that could go on contracts, on packaging, on a social account’s banner.

LAVUE.

❧ ❧ ❧

The glasses girl was still holding the phone up.

She had recorded the entire arc — the bonus policy, the two launch shades, Baek Eun-ah’s lips, the pricing decision, the christening.

She asked, small-voiced:

“Can we post this?”

The office went abruptly quiet.

Baek Eun-ah recovered first.

“Obviously not.”

Nothing had launched. The trademark registration hadn’t finished running. If any of it leaked now, knockoffs and squatters would be queuing by morning.

Baek Si-on nodded at the glasses girl.

“Cut it.”

“Cut to what?”

“Keep only the part where I walk in.”

Choi Na-young, from the side:

“The walk-in part is shaky.”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

Choi Na-young met his gaze with an expression of profound innocence.

❧ ❧ ❧

The door closed.

Inside, four girls plus Baek Eun-ah lined up facing the entrance, phone lens trained on it.

The door opened again.

Baek Si-on entered his own company for the second time, his stride fractionally looser than the first.

The glasses girl held dead steady.

Choi Na-young whispered direction from behind the lens:

“Boss — smile.”

Baek Si-on stood in the doorway and looked around the room.

This one-week-old team had already begun coaching him on how to walk through his own front door.

He suddenly suspected that the million-won-per-ten-thousand-followers promise had been agreed to slightly early.

Night — post-broadcast

The Red Velvet dormitory · television on, five home

‘Music Bank’ went live and wrapped.

Irene changed out of the MC wardrobe and back into her own clothes, working the backstage corridor with her goodbyes — Park Bo-gum, the PD, the writers, one by one.

Seung-wan’s OST session had also wrapped; the two regrouped in the first-floor lobby.

The manager waited at the door; the van idled at the side exit.

Three people, one car.

Home.

Irene had barely pushed the front door open when Soo-young’s voice from the living room bored into her ear:

“The company says they want me for ‘We Got Married’!”

Irene bent to change shoes.

Seul-gi sat cross-legged on the rug, asking Soo-young with complete seriousness:

“It’s not a real marriage!”

“It’s an imaginary couple.”

“Who’s the partner?”

“Don’t know yet.”

“Then what are you this excited about?”

Soo-young cupped her own face, voice drifting fully into fantasy mode:

“What if it’s Baek sunbae?”

Seung-wan’s side-eye went to Irene instantly.

She had personally witnessed the leader perform an abstract boy-group dance at Baek Si-on at noon, and personally witnessed Baek Si-on pinch the leader’s collar and toss her through a door.

Soo-young now publicly wishing — the information load was hazardous.

Irene’s slipper-changing paused for half a beat.

Then she stood and hung her bag on the entryway hook.

“Wake up. This is ‘We Got Married,’ not ‘Korean Celebrities’ Collective Wish Journal.’”

❧ ❧ ❧

Washing up and homewear went fast.

All five drifted back to the living room. Tonight KBS premiered ‘The Producers’ — the most-watched launch in recent memory.

The TV came on. Before airtime, the ad break.

Soo-young sat cross-legged on the sofa, still inside her imaginary-husband topic, polling the room for candidate lists. Seul-gi guessed three names, all married actors. Yeri recommended marrying the mascot outright.

Then a crisp bottle-cap crack leapt out of the TV speaker.

And a whistle hook followed — familiar past the point of familiarity.

‘Oops’.

“That’s ours!”

Five heads turned to the screen in one motion.

The ad opened on Baek Si-on in black sportswear, standing before a vending machine packed with drink. He pressed the button. One BodyArmor, original reduced-sugar, dropped. Cap cracked. One swallow.

On the beat drop, the space behind him took a bucket of paint to the face.

The spot, as aired

Red, orange, purple, pink, green — five colors flooding in from both edges of the frame. Red Velvet walking out of five corresponding color fields, fruit-saturated outfits, each hoisting her own flavor, expressions engineered for dental damage.

Soo-young remembered actual business first.

Phone out. Melon open.

The real-time chart loaded.

Melon · real-time · 22:00 KST

❧ ❧ ❧

By the time the excitement settled, ‘The Producers’ had been on the air ten-odd minutes, and the others’ attention drifted back to the plot.

Irene leaned into the sofa.

Her thoughts had been carried off by the No. 2 ranking. The ranking led to Baek Si-on. Baek Si-on led to lunch. Lunch led to I’ll be checking.

In three hops, joy had slid into dread of that “ten-million-dollar homework.”

She took out her phone and reopened the forwarded mail.

Tap-step breakdowns. Partner-turn instructionals. Balance drills.

None of it practiceable alone in front of a mirror.

Partner dances required two people.

Her gaze rose off the screen and made a slow circuit of the living room — and landed on Soo-young.

Tallest in the group. Long-limbed. Standing up, still a wide gulf from Baek Si-on’s height — but among the existing options, the closest low-spec substitute on the market.

The recruitment, as framed

“Soo-young-ah.”

“Mm?”

“Once you’re on ‘We Got Married,’ you’ll probably have to dance with your imaginary husband, right?”

Soo-young tilted her head, considered, allowed it was possible. And practice made sense: dates, weddings, anniversary missions — a partner dance in reserve was simple readiness. She sat up straight.

“Teach me, unnie?”

Irene nodded.

At no point in the framing did the word “me” appear. Seung-wan, from the next cushion, clocked the missing information — and, in the interest of continued survival, elected silence. Again.

Irene found the homework’s demo track on her phone and played it out loud.

Two people took the empty patch of living-room floor. The coffee table got shoved aside. Irene set the opening position per the instructional video — one hand raised, the other gesturing Soo-young’s hand to her waist, standard distance held.

Ready to drill the most basic partner turn.

Theoretically romantic.

Execution departed the flight plan immediately.

Soo-young’s performance instinct detonated on contact. Tall, and deep inside her “imaginary-husband rehearsal” persona, she dropped her voice into the magnetic register she had learned exclusively from melodramas:

“My lady. Are you ready?”

Irene’s face collapsed.

“Be normal.”

“Trust your husband.”

The next second, one of Soo-young’s arms cinched Irene’s waist, the other seized her hand, and — like a street thug who had learned the waltz an hour ago outside a club — she spun them both out across the living room.

Irene’s legs scrambled across the floorboards in frantic little steps, fighting Soo-young’s unreasonable stride length.

Seul-gi, Seung-wan, and Yeri watched from the sofa. They attempted, briefly, to observe with scholarly intent.

Inside ten seconds, all three were folded sideways, howling.

Seung-wan finally understood.

The leader’s noon performance for Baek Si-on had not been an accident.

In the discipline of dance comedy, Irene possessed a genuinely stable gift.

Soo-young showed no intent to stop. She deployed the classic domineering-male-lead maneuver — arms suddenly tightening, Irene yanked into her chest —

“Look at no one else. Only at me.”

Irene, ambushed, crashed forward. The two of them nearly went down onto the sofa together.

The living room’s laughter went fully out of control.

❧ ❧ ❧

The ending theme played; episode one of ‘The Producers’ was formally over.

Nobody could have told you how the premiere actually was.

The night, as rated

At least inside the Red Velvet dormitory, the true ratings champion of the evening had nothing to do with Lee Ji-eun, and nothing to do with Cha Tae-hyun.

It was Soo-young, playing the hooligan husband.

Chapter Two Hundred Sixteen

Which Girl Is Worth Tens of Millions?

The moment the livestream popped up, the first thing through the speaker was the stacking chorus of shouting and shutter-clicks from both sides of the red carpet.

The phone screen stuttered on signal wobble.

When it recovered, the camera had just pushed in close.

The first person Irene saw was Justin Bieber.

The most famous bad boy in the world, dressed to the maximum degree of swagger the world offered, standing on the most expensive red carpet in the world.

Then the camera slid half a step sideways.

Baek Si-on stood at Bieber’s right.

A vintage suit in the old-Shanghai style: nipped waist, narrow lapels, a thin pocket-watch chain dropping down the breast.

Irene brought the screen two centimeters closer to her face.

Over on his side, Bieber was posing at full sweep, throwing signs at the lens, every pore on his body shouting look at me.

Baek Si-on, beside him, made no extra movement at all — and every flashbulb was landing on him anyway.

One was stealing the shot. One was collecting the light.

One was burning. One was breathing.

Between them, the stars dressed in every color of the rainbow suddenly looked like they had come to the wrong venue.

Irene thought again of the frame on her phone from before.

How did anyone actually grow from that yellow-haired puppy into this.

❧ ❧ ❧

The red-carpet photo call ended.

The livestream followed a camera position forward through the crowd and found Baek Si-on.

A Korean reporter with a microphone was hurrying after him — New York–based Korean press, or dispatched by a connecting broadcaster, judging by the lanyard. Her expression was torn between excitement and professionalism, the mic held so high it nearly rammed into Baek Si-on’s chin.

“Baek Si-on-ssi! Congratulations! Could I borrow a few minutes?”

Baek Si-on stopped, turned halfway, and gave her a nod.

“Of course.”

The reporter adjusted the mic height first, then started:

“The look today is really stunning. This outfit is so unusual — completely different from everyone else on this carpet. How did you come to choose this direction?”

Baek Si-on glanced down at his own suit, and said:

“Thank you.”

“Honestly, it wasn’t my idea.”

“This was my stylist and the Tom Ford design team, jointly. I was responsible for wearing it here.”

The reporter laughed.

“But the effect is superb.”

“I think so too.”

“You and Tom Ford seem to have real chemistry — every public appearance in Tom Ford has become a talking point.”

Baek Si-on gave a small nod.

“Well — for it to become a talking point, presumably Tom Ford has to be very good at manufacturing talking points first.”

“And their understanding of the male silhouette and line has always been formidable.”

“Tonight I was simply lucky.”

“I happened to be wearing the one that fits me.”

The moment those words landed, the Korean livestream comments began to take flight.

live_02

live_03

Irene killed the danmaku immediately.

The interview carried on.

But an unstable figure appeared at the edge of the frame.

At some point Bieber had drifted around behind Baek Si-on and was making faces into the Korean camera lens. First the tongue. Then a gesture of no identifiable meaning. Then he began mouthing something with extravagant exaggeration — a word, probably, like “Korea.”

The reporter’s and the cameraman’s attention was visibly under assault.

Baek Si-on didn’t look back. He kept facing the lens, pace of speech unchanged.

Seeing that nobody would engage him, Bieber escalated: he dropped one hand onto Baek Si-on’s shoulder, poked his head out from behind him, and tossed the lens a wink.

The reporter was about to lose her composure entirely.

Baek Si-on finally turned his head and gave Bieber one look.

Then, perfectly naturally, he raised a hand and pointed toward the red-carpet entrance, and said in English:

“Look. Selena.”

Bieber’s head whipped around on pure reflex, in the exact direction of the pointing finger.

Over by the entrance, several security staff stood behind the barrier chatting.

A pigeon crossed the steps in flight.

Bieber turned back around.

Baek Si-on was already facing the reporter again.

“Sorry.”

He inclined his head, slightly, to the reporter.

“Please continue.”

The reporter: “……”

The camera: “……”

Bieber’s expression ran the complete arc, from alert to baffled to played, and ended with a slap on Baek Si-on’s back and an English curse too garbled to transcribe.

Irene laughed.

She pressed the back of her hand over her mouth — and then smelled a familiar scent coming close.

Joy’s eye mask was already shoved up on her forehead.

A pair of very bright eyes hovered maybe fifteen centimeters from Irene’s phone screen.

Had been there, possibly, for a while.

Irene jolted. The phone slapped face-down onto her thigh.

“What are you doing?”

Joy said nothing.

She looked down at the phone parked on Irene’s leg. Then up at Irene’s face. Then down again. Then up.

“Unnie wouldn’t want everyone knowing she’s sneaking peeks at Baek sunbae’s livestream, right?”

Irene silently recited murder-is-illegal three times.

On the fourth recitation she added: also, corpses are hard to hide.

“I was not sneaking peeks.”

“Mm.”

“The push notification opened by itself.”

“Mm-mm.”

“I just tapped it, casually.”

“Mm-mm-mm.”

With every extra mm, Irene’s explanation turned one shade paler.

The cabin sat quiet for a few seconds. In the front row, Seulgi rolled over, mumbled something, and sank back under.

Irene and Joy looked at the front seats at the same time. Confirmed nobody was awake.

Irene looked at Joy. Joy still wore the same expression.

Irene struggled for about five seconds.

Then surrendered, flipped the phone over, and lit the screen.

The livestream was still running.

Two heads converged over one screen.

“Sunbae looks so good today.” Joy whispered.

“Mm.”

“And that Bieber is so funny.”

“Mm.”

“Is that what unnie was laughing at just now?”

“…Mm.”

“Then unnie laughed way too loud.”

Irene dug an elbow into her.

“Watch the broadcast.”

Joy had the good sense to shut up.

In the livestream, the next question arrived.

“Baek Si-on-ssi — ‘See You Again’ has become the third Billboard Hot 100 number-one of your life. Could you share what you’re feeling right now?”

Baek Si-on considered for a moment.

“Gratitude.”

“Charlie wrote a beautiful, aching melody. Wiz’s verse gives it another kind of force. And the production team did a great deal of meticulous work on the arrangement.”

“So rather than ‘my third crown,’ I’d rather understand it as: a group of very good people finished something very good together.”

Joy let out a tiny “wa.”

Irene’s hand clapped over her mouth at speed.

The reporter, visibly struck by the answer, rode the momentum into the next question.

“Then — being named to the TIME 100, standing here — what is the biggest thing you feel right now?”

Baek Si-on looked into the lens.

“The responsibility feels heavy.”

“TIME says I have influence. But if I dress badly tonight, they may conclude they picked the wrong person.”

“Fortunately, my stylist and Tom Ford saved me.”

The reporter froze for a beat — and then burst out laughing. The surroundings followed.

Even Bieber, freshly played for the second time, turned his head to double-check whether this man was making fun of him again.

“Sunbae is in such good form today.” Joy murmured, in admiration.

Irene didn’t answer.

But she nodded, once.

Right then the van began slowing, and rolled to a stop below the dorm. The driver pulled the handbrake.

Irene realized they had arrived. She locked the phone into her coat pocket and started waking people.

“We’re here.”

“Up. Come on.”

Seulgi’s eyes cracked open, bleary, and it took her three seconds to establish which building, which city, and which year she was in. Wendy was already hunting for her shoes. Yeri lay flat across her seat with zero intention of waking.

Joy pushed her.

“Yeri. We’re here.”

“Mm…”

“If you don’t get up, unnie will carry you up on her back.”

“…Then carry me.”

In the end it was Irene who hauled Yeri up out of the seat by main force.

The five of them shuffled toward the dorm building. The living-room lights came on. The water dispenser went glug.

Joy collapsed onto the sofa on entry; Seulgi went for the fridge; Wendy yawed toward her room mid-yawn; Yeri detangled her hair while announcing how tired she was, loudly, to no one.

The first thing Irene did in her own room was dig the phone back out.

The screen lit again. The livestream was still on — but the picture had widened to a general red-carpet view now. Other stars were walking. The flashes still crackled; the screaming hadn’t stopped. The host was introducing a Chinese actress who was saying something into her mic like “so many people~”…

Irene watched for a few seconds, then set the phone down beside her pillow and lay flat on her back.

The ceiling was white.

From the bathroom came the sound of a faucet — someone washing up.

She closed her eyes.

Opened them again.

Then picked the phone back up, left the livestream, and opened the video parked in her downloads.

The yellow hair, mid-dance-step, frozen in the frame — waiting, perfectly still, for her to press play.

The same night

Gala hours

The Metropolitan Museum of Art · the China-themed exhibition

Meanwhile, the yellow hair himself was standing in the Met’s galleries, physically preventing Justin Bieber from taking a photograph with a display model of a People’s Liberation Army uniform.

“Justin.”

“Hold on, let me finish the shot.”

Bieber had his sunglasses pushed up into his hair and stood angled beside the deep-green uniform display, phone at a forty-five-degree angle, mouth arranged into what he believed was a cool expression.

Baek Si-on reached out and pressed the raised arm back down.

“If you don’t want to be comprehensively banned tomorrow by a country of one point four billion people, you will stop what you are doing. Now.”

Bieber blinked.

“Why? I just think it looks cool.”

“You also thought some things looked cool last year.”

Bieber opened his mouth — and clearly remembered something.

In the end he put the phone down.

“Fine.”

He shrugged.

“Sometimes you are really like my mom.”

“I don’t have a son your age.”

They kept walking.

The Met’s theme exhibition changed every year, and this year’s theme was China-bound. The galleries displayed costumes, models, and historical imaginings built out of every species of Eastern symbol — some of it scholarly, some of it a textbook collection of the West’s classic misreadings of Eastern taste.

Passing a Chinese porcelain vitrine, Bieber pointed:

“This one’s safe for a photo, right?”

“This one’s fine.”

“You Asians have so many rules.”

“People with deep cultural foundations all have many rules.”

❧ ❧ ❧

The Met Gala’s mechanics were, in truth, simple.

The red carpet was for the media. The exhibition was for the theme. What actually made everyone sit down like obedient children was the formal dinner afterward.

The dinner filled the museum’s main hall. Long tables in white linen, full silver service, centerpieces of white calla lilies in season.

One table reportedly cost a quarter of a million dollars — a little over three hundred million won.

And judging by the room, nobody was eating.

Most people stood holding wine and talking; a minority sat and talked; a smaller minority sat and looked at their phones.

Baek Si-on belonged to the minority of the minority of that last kind.

❝

He sat in the seat Tom Ford had paid for, before him a steak perhaps three fingers wide, beside it two leaves of roasted arugula under some species of truffle sauce — a portion that might not half-fill a medium dog. So Baek Si-on ate Bieber’s portion too.

— the quarter-million-dollar table, as actually consumed

The speeches were brief.

Anna stood on the stage for a few minutes: thanks for everyone’s generosity, thanks to this year’s theme sponsors, thanks to all the designers and artists.

The Sunglasses King went up and said a few words as well. His sunglasses never came off, from first word to last.

Baek Si-on suspected the man could have fallen asleep up there and nobody would have noticed.

When the speeches wrapped, the host took the mic, and the performance segment began.

Bieber went first.

He sang ‘Love Yourself’.

Baek Si-on sat on a high stool beside him, acoustic guitar in his arms, playing accompaniment.

The performances, as sequenced

‘Love Yourself’ — Bieber on vocals, loose with a shred of huskiness, the low register rock-steady; the room switching from social mode to spectator mode the moment the intro landed. Baek Si-on, on the stool, noticed one problem: Bieber’s eyes kept drifting toward one particular table.

‘Children’ — its world premiere, granted by Bieber though the song was unreleased. Big-theme words: responsibility, the era, the young, the future — words that turn weightless in the wrong room, and landed exactly right in this one: a hall of moguls, capital, media, and world-class brands, and the newest TIME 100 honoree singing “what do we hand the next generation” with, frankly, some earned righteousness. Not sloganeering. You have to arrive at this position first, before you’ve earned the right to sing it.

‘See You Again’ — the closer. The intro alone changed the temperature of the room, because the song was currently playing from every corner of the Earth that owned a speaker; under the Met’s dome it worked just as well. Bieber took the rap verse — a voice naturally on the sweet side, which pinched itself without meaning to when it rapped. The effect was, against all odds, good. Probably because the room was too expensive for anything in it to be allowed to look strange.

On the problem from the first line: Selena was sitting where Bieber’s eyes kept going. And clamped over her ears were two hands. Taylor’s. The meaning of the gesture was not ambiguous: don’t listen. The scoundrel is singing.

Baek Si-on’s chord had come within half a beat of slipping.

He controlled it. As the Venice-certified, he had received extremely professional expression-management training: however absurd the scene, he could hold his face. Unless genuinely unbearable. Fortunately, tonight had not yet reached the “genuinely unbearable” threshold.

When the two-man opening set ended, other artists took the stage.

Baek Si-on returned to the seat Tom Ford had bought him, and continued eating.

The finale was Rihanna.

She took the stage in that yellow couture gown that had owned the red carpet, its train pooling behind her a good three meters.

In Baek Si-on’s private estimation, it resembled an egg-infused scallion pancake that had achieved sentience.

The applause at the end ran long.

But the true reason the whole room stood probably had two parts song to eight parts train: a person who sings for ten minutes inside a three-meter yellow train without once tripping deserves a standing ovation on that fact alone.

The formal dinner wound to its end, and the guests spilled outward. Outside the museum the media and the onlookers were still massed, the flashes still firing, fans behind the barriers shouting assorted names.

Bieber walked ahead of Baek Si-on, his condition visibly unstable — guarded against on all fronts by Taylor for an entire evening, mentality cracked. And so, at the exact moment he passed through the doors, he did the one thing no functional person present would have done.

“Selena! You look gorgeous tonight!”

The voice was loud. Loud enough that two supermodels being interviewed on the museum steps turned to look.

Selena hadn’t yet managed a response.

It was Taylor, beside her, who reacted first — smiling, pivoting, and raising her right hand toward Bieber.

The middle finger.

The nail polish was tonight’s red-carpet wine red.

Bieber’s smile froze in mid-air for about two seconds, then retracted very, very slowly.

“Hey, Baek.”

Rihanna’s voice came from behind him.

Rihanna

Singer · the night’s closing act

A three-meter train · two years of hand sewing · advice that was free, this once

Closed the Met in yellow, invited her competition to her own party, and issued the only verdict that mattered: stay away from that family. Building something of her own, with her own name on the billboard for a change.

She had changed her shoes — the red-carpet skyscrapers gone, her feet in flat gold sandals. The yellow gown was still on, its train gathered over an assistant’s arm, the girl jogging to keep up behind her.

“Wait for me a second.”

Rihanna walked up to Baek Si-on and produced a business-card-sized slip of card from her clutch.

“I’m having a small party, out in Tribeca.”

He took it. A hotel’s name, and after it, a room number in handwriting.

“Just friends, nothing formal,” Rihanna added.

Then she glanced past him, at Bieber — currently folded on the steps, wearing the face of a scolded dog.

“Bring him too. Get a few drinks into him before he starts something bigger on the street.”

Baek Si-on pocketed the card.

“Thank you.”

“Don’t mention it. I loved your outfit tonight.”

“And I loved your dress tonight.”

“You’d better mean that. This dress took my designer two years, by hand.”

With that, Rihanna went down the steps on her gold sandals, the train drawing an arc behind her, the assistant switching the train to the other arm as she followed.

Past midnight

The after-hours

Tribeca · a top floor, a double door half-open

Tribeca. The elevator opened on the top floor, onto a corridor of dark-grey carpet. At its end, a set of double doors stood half-open, bass drums leaking out through the gap.

When Baek Si-on walked in with Bieber, the atmosphere inside was a different universe from the museum two hours earlier.

At the Met’s formal dinner, everyone had worn six-figure couture and traded social smiles over white linen and silver. Here, the actresses and supermodels had traded the red-carpet gowns — the ones needing three people to manage a train — for slip dresses, satin shirts, bodycon cuts. Half the fabric gone; the visual impact twice as strong. The men had changed the same way: jackets off, bows loose, sleeves rolled to the forearm, drinks in hand, clustered at the floor-to-ceiling windows.

The red carpet is for the world. The dinner is for the industry. The after-party is for desire and interest themselves.

— the evening, in three tiers

Baek Si-on took a whiskey from the bar.

Bieber took the bottle.

The booth sat by the windows. Bieber dropped into it, set the bottle on the table, and started pouring.

“You know what the funniest part is?”

Baek Si-on, glass in hand, let his gaze drift idly across the party — and registered a figure heading his way.

Kendall Jenner.

She had changed into a black satin slip. Long legs, narrow waist, a collarbone line drawn with a ruler.

As one of the most expensive models on Earth, the way she walked through a room like this was itself a performance: every step knew it was being watched. Every step made sure it was worth watching.

Kendall Jenner

Black satin slip · every step worth watching · homework done in advance

Opened on his chart numbers, pivoted smoothly to her sister’s lip kits, and lost her seat to a shoulder-bump and a toast — exiting with her walk as flawless as her entrance.

“Mm.”

And rotated his head back to the ceiling.

Not a trace of awkwardness crossed Kendall’s face. As if she had only said hello in passing, she transferred eyes and body to Baek Si-on in the same motion.

Baek Si-on gave her a sideways look.

That was no longer mere complimenting.

That was a woman who had done her homework.

“You sound more curious about my business than about me.”

“I’m just curious by nature.”

Kendall switched the glass to her other hand and leaned in, a degree closer:

“You have a fanbase that size, and that many fashion resources. Why have you never done anything in beauty?”

Surprise flickered across Kendall’s face, just a little.

“My sister Kylie’s been deep in her lip-kit brand lately — it sells extremely well. She says Asia is the market she wants in on most, and she can’t find the right way through.”

A pause.

“Maybe the two of you should talk sometime?”

Baek Si-on took a sip.

“If the chance comes, it’s worth a conversation.”

Kendall had just registered that her agenda was showing, and was reaching for lighter topics, when a voice arrived from their left.

“Kendall. You’re in my seat.”

Rihanna — changed again, now in a black dress, a suspiciously colored cocktail in her right hand, glitter on her nails catching the dim light in flashes.

She did not wait for Kendall to move.

She simply wedged herself sideways into the gap between Baek Si-on and Kendall, shoulder-bumping, shoving Kendall half a body-width outward.

If you didn’t look at Kendall’s face, you’d call it two friends horseplaying.

Kendall’s face, however, said everything: somewhere behind it, all eighteen generations of Rihanna’s ancestors were being warmly greeted.

“Good evening, Rihanna.”

“Evening, darling.”

Rihanna clinked her glass against Kendall’s.

Kendall finished the last swallow, and stood.

“Baek — we’ll talk again sometime.”

Baek Si-on raised his glass an inch toward her.

Kendall left.

Her walk, on exit, remained perfect.

Rihanna watched her all the way out, then turned back to Baek Si-on.

“Keep your distance from that family.”

“When they’re whipping up a storyline, they’d drag their own dog out and film ten episodes of reality TV around it.”

Baek Si-on didn’t take the bait.

She was entitled to say that. He was not.

Rihanna didn’t need him to agree, either. She issued a personal opinion; having issued it, she was done with it. She parked the suspicious cocktail on the coffee table and folded herself into a more comfortable position against the sofa.

“…He’s sitting right there.”

“Forget it — no music talk.”

“Because the more I talk about it, the more annoyed I get.”

She took a pull of the cocktail.

“Making music is exhausting now. The streaming era: a song gets played a million times, and what lands in my hand is worth less than a crate of my perfume. Do you believe that?”

Baek Si-on was quiet for a moment.

This topic was awkward terrain for him — because he happened to be the man the streaming era had put on the crest of its wave. Of the Billboard triple crown, streaming accounted for at least sixty percent.

“The music industry is mid-transformation,” he finally said — a sentence that offended no one and took no side.

Rihanna looked at him, and laughed.

“You talk nicely. Much more interesting than that room full of nodding men.”

She set the glass down, then tipped her chin toward a corner across the party.

“See those men in the dark suits?”

Baek Si-on followed her gaze.

Three white men stood in the corner, suits razor-pressed, ties still knotted, their drinks essentially untouched. In a room marinated in alcohol and bass, they looked like they had been delivered to the wrong galaxy.

“LVMH people,” Rihanna said.

Baek Si-on drew his eyes back.

“I’m in talks with them. My own cosmetics brand.”

“Cosmetics?”

“Yes.”

She turned her head and looked at him.

“Think about how big the beauty market is now. Asia alone — skincare plus makeup — hundreds of billions of dollars a year. Still climbing.”

“I’ve endorsed so many brands. How much did I make them? Nobody can count it. But the money was theirs, the traffic was theirs, and in the end my name only ever shows up on the billboard.”

“So I’ve decided to do it myself. My own name. My own products. Money into my own pocket.”

Baek Si-on raised the whiskey and drank.

“And you?” Rihanna asked.

“What about me?”

“Your fans’ spending power is monstrous. What the Under Armour collab did in sales, you know better than anyone. And Asia is the biggest skincare-and-makeup market on the planet. You’ve never thought about building something?”

Beside the sofa, Bieber produced a beer-flavored burp.

The party’s music changed tracks.

Baek Si-on set his glass on the coffee table.

“I’ll think about it seriously.”

Rihanna shrugged.

“Suit yourself. The advice was free this time. It won’t be next time.”

And she was gone — leaving behind a coconut trail of perfume, and one question Baek Si-on did not yet have an answer to.

Past two in the morning.

New York’s streets were wider than Seoul’s, but the small-hours smell was about the same.

The van moved through Manhattan. Bieber lay half-conscious in the back, still slurring curses at Taylor.

“What’s wrong with him?”

Scooter asked, turning from the passenger seat.

“Feelings.”

“…Oh.”

Scooter had long since stopped being surprised by anything.

Baek Si-on leaned against the seatback and watched the skyline slide along the right side of the window. And the party conversation resurfaced in his head.

Kylie’s lip-kit brand sells extremely well. Asia is the market she wants in on most.

He knew Kylie Jenner’s cosmetics brand.

In his previous life’s memory, within a few years the Coty Group would acquire a fifty-one percent stake in it — a brand propped up by social-media traffic and family fame, valued at one point two billion dollars.

And Rihanna’s Fenty Beauty.

In his previous life, that brand had done five hundred million dollars in its first year on the market.

Then he thought of one more person.

Jessica Jung.

Her sunglasses brand, Blanc & Eclare.

It, too, had started as traffic turned into merchandise.

But the road had been taken wrongly. Product positioning blurry, the supply chain unreliable, until it shrank into a niche label sustained by fan devotion alone.

Three cases crossed in his head. Two successes, one failure.

What the successes shared: precise market positioning, a strong supply-chain partner, and — above all — a founder who was herself the traffic entrance.

The reason for the failure: traffic without an industry chain underneath it.

Baek Si-on closed his eyes for a beat.

Traffic: he had no shortage. Venice Best Actor, the Billboard triple crown, the TIME 100, the global sales data of the Under Armour collaboration, all sitting right there.

The sheer tonnage of the Asian market: also not lacking.

What was missing was only a properly timed entry, and an exit plan smart enough.

Build the brand, raise it three to five years, and once the valuation reached the right band, let one of the great groups come buy it.

Logically, it ran.

“Scooter.”

“Mm?”

“Do you think this logic holds? Launch an independent cosmetics brand — new-generation beauty, high quality, star-traffic-driven. Run it three to five years, then let a big group acquire it.”

Scooter lowered his phone, slowly.

He turned his head. His expression moved from no-waves to waves.

“Cosmetics?”

“Yes.”

“Meaning what, exactly?”

“The literal meaning.”

Scooter went quiet for two seconds. Then asked:

“Which girl is worth you spending tens of millions of dollars to pave a road for?”

“What?”

“You’re doing a cosmetics brand because some girl likes cosmetics. Right?”

“…No.”

“Then it’s to be your own spokesperson?”

“……”

“You’re going to front your own makeup line?!”

Baek Si-on didn’t answer that one.

Because he had just discovered that Scooter’s question wasn’t entirely unreasonable. A male artist launching a cosmetics line — fronting it himself genuinely was one of the options. And in Korea, male artists endorsing cosmetics brands was common practice.

But this was not the moment to discuss it.

“We’ll talk when we’re back.”

“No. You have to tell me right now which girl it is.”

“There is no girl.”

Scooter studied him for five full seconds, suspicion thick on his face.

Then he turned back around. Picked up his phone.

And began messaging someone frantically.

Baek Si-on leaned forward a fraction and glanced at the screen.

The recipient was Baek Eun-ah.

The first message:

Message · Scooter Braun → Baek Eun-ah

Scooter

Who’s your brother’s girlfriend in Korea?

Baek Si-on turned his gaze back to the window.

Manhattan’s lights stretched into bands across the glass, and he decided that tonight, he would answer no further questions of any kind that involved cosmetics.

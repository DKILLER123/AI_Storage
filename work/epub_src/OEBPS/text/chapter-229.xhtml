Chapter Two Hundred Twenty-Nine

The Red Bebe Set-Visit Day

The night of the door · continuing

One locked room, three ears against the door

The Red Velvet dormitory · the leader’s room

The door closed.

Son Seung-wan put her back against it and raised both hands before Bae Joo-hyun could say a word.

“Unnie, I really didn’t see anything.”

“I haven’t asked you what you saw yet.”

Son Seung-wan’s survival declaration stopped mid-air.

A very soft scraping sound came from outside the door.

Three heads had, in all probability, already pressed themselves flat against the door panel.

But Bae Joo-hyun had no attention to spare for them.

The one inside the room had already confessed.

Interrogate the one inside first.

“Talk.”

“About what?”

“What did you see in the corridor?”

Son Seung-wan fumbled.

“It was honestly nothing. You were just standing in the corridor doing a bit of a...”

She went hunting for an adjective that would not endanger her life.

“...vintage dance.”

Bae Joo-hyun’s face went cold.

Son Seung-wan corrected course immediately.

“Extremely evocative of its era.”

“......”

“Full of precious traces of early idol culture.”

The more she explained, the heavier the charge grew.

Bae Joo-hyun walked to the bed, sat, and indicated that she should continue.

Son Seung-wan had no choice but to deliver the day’s events in full.

She had come out of the recording room looking for someone.

Around the corridor’s corner, she had walked in on Bae Joo-hyun mid-undulation beside the vending machine.

Baek Si-on had been standing opposite.

When the dance ended, he had taken Bae Joo-hyun by the back collar and carried the person off into the ‘Descendants of the Sun’ project meeting room.

“That’s all?”

“That’s all.”

“And after?”

“After, I went and ate dinner.”

“You didn’t tell the others?”

Son Seung-wan straightened her spine.

“Not one word.”

That much was true.

With Park Soo-young and Kim Yerim’s appetite for gossip, half a sentence would have been enough — the living room would never have stayed on Baek Si-on’s “ideal type” alone.

Son Seung-wan had genuinely kept her secret safe.

Bae Joo-hyun’s expression eased slightly.

But the Red Velvet dorm had never been a suitable vault for long-term storage.

Holding Son Seung-wan down on the leader’s authority alone carried too much risk.

She needed a sweetener.

Money would look vulgar.

Besides, her money was still lying inside SM’s quarterly settlement sheet, not yet liberated into cash-shaped freedom.

Bae Joo-hyun shifted her sitting posture.

“Seung-wan-ah.”

Son Seung-wan went on instant alert.

Sudden gentleness from the leader usually meant the interrogation had entered a more dangerous phase.

“Ye.”

“Do you want to sing an OST?”

It took Son Seung-wan a second to catch up.

“Unnie, am I not already recording one? For ‘School 2015’.”

“I mean Kim Eun-sook-writer-nim’s new drama.”

The room went quiet.

The scraping outside stopped too.

The three of them had plainly pressed their ears in tighter.

Caution on Son Seung-wan’s face was gradually displaced by astonishment.

Kim Eun-sook.

‘Lovers in Paris’. ‘Secret Garden’. ‘A Gentleman’s Dignity’. ‘The Heirs’...

When a drama written by that woman exploded, the name of its OST vocalist crossed Korea with the plot, and then spread across Asia.

This was beyond an ordinary work offer.

Held in front of a rookie singer, it differed from an express elevator’s direct-to-penthouse button by very little.

“Unnie, how would a chance like that ever come around to me?”

“That’s why you go and fight for it.”

“You have a way in?”

Bae Joo-hyun, of course, held no power to decide who sang anything.

But she held one piece of information.

That day in the KBS meeting room, Kim Eun-sook had specified a male-female duet.

The female slot was still open.

After coming home, Bae Joo-hyun had run the list of Baek Si-on’s likely choices through her head.

His “get the dance right first, then we’ll talk about singing” — it had sounded like score-settling, but in Bae Joo-hyun’s ears it had auto-translated into a second layer of meaning:

her vocals weren’t there yet.

If even she didn’t qualify, then Sulli-sunbae would most likely be passed over too.

Sulli’s position in f(x) mirrored her own.

Acting and visual presence first; vocal completeness never the strongest weapon. Baek Si-on would not hand her that slot.

Which made the most dangerous name very obvious.

— Lee Ji-eun.

Commercial pull, stable technique, an unmistakable timbre — and more than one prior collaboration with Baek Si-on.

Bae Joo-hyun preferred not to think further down that direction.

But she thought it anyway.

If she didn’t get Son Seung-wan over there first, that OST would, ten to one, fly to Lee Ji-eun.

She bore Lee Ji-eun no personal grudge.

She simply felt that the sunbae already owned a great many good things, and this one song could afford to wait a while.

Besides — Son Seung-wan’s chart power couldn’t be compared to that name, but her timbre might not lose, and at minimum she had earned one audition.

Rookies had rookie advantages too.

Cheap. Easy to schedule. Highly cooperative. And no agency needed to hold three rounds of cross-department meetings over one harmony line.

So Bae Joo-hyun buried every fragment of private feeling in that reasoning and kept only the professional judgment a leader was allowed to say out loud.

“The female vocal hasn’t been decided.”

“Baek-sunbae’s standards are high. But you can at least go try once.”

The temptation had already written itself across Son Seung-wan’s face.

“Unnie, just showing up to ask for it... wouldn’t that be too forward?”

“We are not going to ask for resources.”

Bae Joo-hyun said it with the full force of righteousness.

“We are going on a set visit.”

Son Seung-wan’s expression turned layered, by degrees.

“Unnie. Have you wanted to visit that set for a while?”

“I am acting in the group’s interest.”

“Then why am I the only one you brought?”

“Because you sing best.”

“......”

Son Seung-wan could not rebut it.

Bae Joo-hyun had always looked after her — leader’s sense of duty, and genuine pride in a member’s results.

Through the group’s hardest stretch she had been the one soothing the younger ones, absorbing the hate comments, holding the team together; giving a resource like this to the main vocal first fit her leadership logic to the letter.

As for whether there was any other reason.

Son Seung-wan elected to respect the leader’s privacy.

The OST smelled very good.

Staying alive also mattered.

“Then... what do I need to prepare?”

The satisfied expression finally arrived on Bae Joo-hyun’s face.

“Prepare to sing.”

She raised one finger and attached the deal’s most critical supplementary clause.

“Also — forget the KBS corridor.”

Son Seung-wan nodded hard.

“I have never once in my life been in the KBS corridor.”

A suppressed snort broke through the door.

Bae Joo-hyun shot up and strode over.

Panicked footsteps exploded outside at once.

Someone hit the wall.

Someone stepped on a slipper.

And one deeply aggrieved line from Kang Seul-gi:

“Why are you two pushing me?”

Bae Joo-hyun took the door handle.

Outside had already gone still.

She didn’t open it.

Today’s killing quota was, for the time being, used up.

Rice cooker on keep-warm — one lunchbox in progress

The dormitory kitchen

Two days later.

The rice cooker clicked over to keep-warm, and the kitchen smelled of fried egg and roasted seaweed.

Kim Yerim sat at the table in her school uniform, half a slice of toast clamped in her mouth, a textbook still open in her hands.

Bae Joo-hyun pushed a cup of warm milk in front of her.

“Finish it.”

“I’m late.”

“Then stop reading.”

“Quiz today.”

“I told you to review last night. You were researching your brother-in-law with Soo-young instead.”

Found guilty, Kim Yerim picked up the milk and downed half the cup in one go.

When the front door clicked shut behind her, the house was one late-for-school reserve shorter.

Bae Joo-hyun cleared the breakfast dishes and returned to the counter.

A bamboo mat lay spread across it.

Seaweed, rice, egg, pickled radish, spinach, and ham, lined up in neat ranks.

Son Seung-wan stood by to pass her the cut ingredients.

“Unnie.”

“Mm?”

“Are you sure a Venice Best Actor eats gimbap?”

Bae Joo-hyun spread the rice evenly over the seaweed.

“Eun-ah says he can put away three bowls of rice over nothing but kimchi.”

“That’s at home.”

“A film set has meals too.”

“But other people send support trucks when they visit a set.”

Bae Joo-hyun rolled the mat tight and pressed forward with her palm.

A truck certainly had more spectacle.

She had considered it.

But it was too expensive.

True, she had just signed a six-hundred-million-won endorsement contract.

But after SM’s cut, the agent’s percentage, and taxes, the money that would actually reach her hands came to roughly sixty million won.

And that money was quarterly settlement — before it even landed in her account, the trainee-era training debts owed to the company had first claim on it.

Gritting her teeth, she could afford it. But a truck rolling onto a film set would turn the whole gesture far too loud.

Gimbap was so much simpler.

It could be called gratitude. It could equally be called concern over whether the brand’s owner was eating lunch on time.

The justification was terrifyingly complete.

Bae Joo-hyun cut the final roll.

The blade came down; the cross-section showed off vivid yellow, green, and pink.

Son Seung-wan privately felt that visiting a film set with gimbap read as slightly shabby.

So she took out her phone and searched for production support trucks.

The page loaded.

Base package: from two million won.

Beverages, banners, custom cup sleeves billed separately; rush orders carried a surcharge on top.

Son Seung-wan stared at the number on the screen and voluntarily exited the truck website.

“Unnie.”

“Mm?”

“I think the gimbap shows real sincerity.”

Bae Joo-hyun packed the cut gimbap into the resealable container.

“I think so too.”

The two of them exchanged a look.

Real sincerity.

Absolutely nothing to do with two million won.

Son Seung-wan took the lid duty.

Bae Joo-hyun pulled two bottles from the refrigerator and wedged them into the insulated bag’s side pockets.

The bag closed.

She looked everything over once.

Gimbap.

Drinks.

Son Seung-wan.

Everything a set visit required was accounted for.

That night

Work wrapped — four people staying behind, one lesson loading

An abandoned parking garage · the ‘The Three Evils’ night shoot

The lights of the abandoned parking garage could hold only the middle stretch of concrete; everything around it was thick black.

Most of the surface coating had flaked off the pillars, exposing the grey-white skeleton of the concrete underneath.

Dried oil streaks marked the floor; a few dead cars sat in the distance.

Film lights hung from aluminum stands, their beams slicing through the dust in the air.

“Ready.”

Baek Jeong-hoon’s voice came from behind the monitor.

The armorer stepped up for the final safety check, confirmed every item, and set the prop gun back into its molded slot in the working toolbox.

Baek Jeong-hoon raised a hand.

The slate clapped.

“‘The Three Evils’ — scene twelve, take six, first slate.”

Crack.

“Action.”

Jo Woo-jin stood beside the toolbox in an old jacket.

Baek Si-on walked out from behind a pillar, one smear of dark red — ownership unknown — on his cuff.

The two men stopped, the hood of a car between them.

“You came.”

“Mm.”

Jo Woo-jin crouched and lifted the lid.

Beside the pistol lay a magazine, a suppressor, and a few simply cleaned maintenance tools, all in order.

Jo Woo-jin took the gun out, weighed it in his hand, and held it across.

Baek Si-on didn’t take it at once.

He watched Jo Woo-jin first.

Then looked down at the gun.

Jo Woo-jin tilted his head.

“Scared?”

Baek Si-on took it.

“I was just thinking — hardly anything you hand me comes without trouble attached.”

“If it’s trouble, don’t take it.”

“Would you take it back?”

“No.”

“Then stop wasting breath.”

Baek Si-on received the gun.

Magazine out.

Check.

Slid back in.

He turned toward the dead car in the distance and raised his arm.

The gun in the foreground of the frame.

The camera hugged the side profile: brow and eyes claimed the entire visual center.

His index finger settled onto the trigger.

BANG—!

Muzzle flash tore the gloom open.

The sound ricocheted off the concrete walls again and again, bouncing around the hollow underground space several times before it thinned away.

Baek Jeong-hoon stared at the monitor.

“Cut. Again.”

On a first gun scene, adjustment was normal.

Props reset the position.

The toolbox closed again.

The slate fell a second time.

The same dialogue.

The same handoff.

Baek Si-on raised his arm.

BANG—!

“Cut. Again.”

The third time.

The shot still cracked on cue.

“Stop.”

Baek Si-on finally frowned.

He looked down to check the prop gun in his hand, assuming his grip or the rhythm of the raise had gone wrong.

Baek Jeong-hoon rose from behind the monitor.

“You blinked.”

He dragged the playback to the instant of the trigger.

The muzzle flash had barely appeared when Baek Si-on’s eyelids had already snapped shut on instinct.

At normal speed it was nearly uncapturable — but a close-up would magnify that reflex without mercy.

“The lead may hesitate before he kills. He may not be startled by the report of his own trigger.”

Baek Si-on studied the frozen frame.

Gun in front.

Face behind it.

That one blink had torn open the danger the character had only just built.

Baek Si-on had always solved characters with emotion, understanding, gaze, delivery, and explosion.

Those were his home grounds.

This was different.

Blinking at a gunshot was the body’s instinct.

It didn’t eat talent.

It ate training.

However fine the acting, however full the trophy shelf — the instant a shot cracked, disobedient eyelids were disobedient.

Baek Si-on set the gun back on the prop table.

“Are there any shots left tonight without the gunshot?”

Baek Jeong-hoon reached a hand toward the assistant director.

The shooting plan came over.

What remained tonight: Jo Woo-jin revealing the nightclub’s location, and wide two-shots and over-the-shoulder coverage.

Baek Si-on leaned in for a look.

“Shoot those first. I’ll make up the gun close-ups tomorrow.”

Baek Jeong-hoon sized him up for two seconds.

“Tomorrow it’s solved?”

“It’s solved.”

The camera crew began moving positions.

Lighting redressed its angles.

The production rolled into prep for the next setup.

That night, after wrap.

The crew bus carried the staff away; the actors’ vans left the garage one after another.

Baek Si-on didn’t leave.

He found the stunt coordinator, the armorer, and the gaffer, and asked for adaptation training.

The three exchanged a look.

The stunt coordinator asked first:

“Now?”

“Double overtime.” Baek Si-on said.

“Fine.”

The four of them returned to the spot they had just been shooting.

The light drill, as run

Target: a taped white cross on the far wall. Eyes locked.

A small film light, angled in from his forward flank — no warning of on or off.

First strike: full glare into the eyes — the squint came, and the focus drifted with it. “Again.”

Second: the blink reflex still fired; the focus stayed.

Third: the line of sight began to hold.

Light on. Light off. Light on. Light off — until sudden glare could no longer drag his attention, and stopped owning his eyelids.

The first layer of training started from a stable gaze.

Baek Si-on fixed his eyes on the taped white cross on the wall ahead.

The gaffer rigged a small film light at his forward flank.

No telling him when it would come on, or go off.

The light slammed on.

Glare rammed into his eyes.

Baek Si-on squinted on instinct, and his attention was hauled toward the source.

“Again.”

Light out.

Baek Si-on re-locked the white tape on the wall.

The gaffer gave no warning this time either.

The glare cut into his peripheral vision again.

The physiological blink was still there — the focus stayed put.

The third time.

The gaze gradually steadied.

The stunt coordinator stood to one side, logging every change in the response.

Baek Si-on adjusted, over and over.

Light on.

Light off.

Light on.

Light off.

After countless rounds, his eyes could hold focus inside a sudden blaze — attention no longer towing toward the source, eyelids no longer closing on reflex.

❧ ❧ ❧

The sound drill, as run

Same position. Same target. The coordinator migrating, each round, to a new spot behind him.

Slater, two meters back: “PAK!” — the eyelids moved. “Again.”

Round two: eyes held; the right shoulder climbed a finger’s width. “Shoulder moved.”

Then: wood slaps, slate cracks, hollow metal drums — from every direction, every timbre, every interval, no pattern offered.

Cure: one lap of slow jogging until the breath went ragged, gun back up, and the body finally stopped pre-tensing for a sound it could not predict. Sound was sound. The hand was the hand. The eyes were the eyes. Take them apart.

The light drill finished; the stunt coordinator changed methods.

Unwarned sound adaptation.

Same position. Same target.

Each round, the coordinator migrated to a different spot behind him — never announcing when the sound would come.

Water dripped somewhere deep in a pipe.

“PAK!”

The slate cracked two meters behind him.

Baek Si-on’s eyelids flinched.

“Again.”

The waiting was the crueler torture.

His ears began hunting every shift in the room; his shoulders stored force without permission.

“PAK—!”

The eyes held. The right shoulder climbed a little.

“Shoulder moved.”

Baek Si-on dropped his arms and shook them out.

“Continue.”

Wood slaps, slate cracks, the knock of an empty metal drum in the distance — rotating in from every direction.

At first, he braced against the sounds.

The bracing kept the muscles strung tight.

The coordinator had him set the gun down and jog a slow lap of the garage — then, breathing ragged, return to the mark and raise the gun again.

The sounds arrived without warning, again and again.

Every direction. Every timbre. Every interval.

Slowly, his body stopped pre-tightening for “the next one.”

Because he had begun to give up predicting.

No guessing when the next sound would land.

Only watching the mark ahead.

Sound was sound. The hand was the hand. The eyes were the eyes.

Take them apart.

❧ ❧ ❧

Jo Woo-jin had a night-running habit, so instead of taking the bus back to the hotel he meant to jog home from the set.

He had barely finished warming up, ready to leave, when he heard gunshots carrying from the direction of the abandoned parking garage.

The intervals were irregular.

Sometimes two in a row; sometimes one, after a long silence.

Jo Woo-jin pulled one earbud out, ran to the mouth of the underground entrance, and took the ramp down.

Baek Si-on stood beside the dead car, black short-sleeve soaked through with sweat, his arm rising again and again.

Shot.

Flash.

Down.

Check the playback.

Rest.

Back to the mark, repeat.

Jo Woo-jin leaned on the pillar by the entrance and said nothing.

He had assumed a young Best Actor of Baek Si-on’s level was frightening enough on talent alone.

The training in front of him supplied a new answer.

— Jo Woo-jin, from a concrete pillar, mid-night-run, revising his own judgment

Leaning against the concrete pillar, Jo Woo-jin suddenly felt that coming out for a night run tonight had been a touch reckless.

The lead was practicing shooting.

The man whose job was handing things over probably owed himself another two laps.

The next morning

Two hats, two masks, one insulated bag — one guard, fully alerted

Namyangju · the ‘The Three Evils’ set entrance

The next morning.

Outside the ‘The Three Evils’ set in Namyangju, a van stopped at the entrance.

The door slid open and Bae Joo-hyun came down first.

A black baseball cap pulled low, a mask covering most of her face, loose jacket over long pants — and one bulging insulated bag in her hand.

Son Seung-wan followed close behind.

Matching hat and mask, a bag on her shoulder.

To the set security guard, they looked exactly like two fully equipped sasaeng fans attempting to infiltrate a shooting location.

Three steps toward the entrance, and the pair was stopped.

“Hold on.”

The guard rose out of his booth and looked them up and down.

“Which department are you with?”

Bae Joo-hyun stopped and gave a polite bow.

“We’re here on a set visit.”

The answer bought no passage.

The guard’s wariness climbed instead.

“Visiting who?”

Son Seung-wan answered.

“Baek Si-on sunbae.”

The guard’s alertness maxed out on the spot.

The week, as intercepted

Unknown girls claiming to visit Baek Si-on: no fewer than ten groups in seven days.

Of those: eight groups — self-identified cousins. Two groups — self-identified fiancées.

The guard’s priors had been earned the hard way.

“You can’t come in here.”

Bae Joo-hyun tried again.

“We arranged it in advance with Manager Baek.”

The guard reached toward the registration desk beside him.

“Not on the list.”

The message truly had reached the producing department.

But ‘The Three Evils’ was a big production, and the location spread across several layers; the producing assistant in charge of reception hadn’t yet pushed the notice down to the outermost gate.

The guard couldn’t wave people through on a claim of “knowing Baek Eun-ah.”

The whole country knew Baek Eun-ah, presumably.

At minimum, everyone hoping to get near Baek Si-on claimed to.

❧ ❧ ❧

The explanation stalled.

Son Seung-wan nudged her leader gently with a shoulder.

“Unnie — take the mask off?”

Bae Joo-hyun hesitated a moment.

She was, after all, the ‘Music Bank’ MC now, with a fresh round of exposure behind the BodyArmor ad and ‘Oops’ — face revealed, she should be recognizable.

She pulled the mask down, tucked the hair at her cheek behind her ear, and bowed to the guard.

“Hello — I’m Irene, of Red Velvet.”

The guard studied her face for several seconds.

Bae Joo-hyun held the standard service smile.

The guard asked, uncertain:

“...What?”

The smile on Bae Joo-hyun’s face stiffened by half a degree.

Son Seung-wan hurried to supplement:

“Red Velvet.”

The guard nodded earnestly, indicating comprehension.

“Oh — the Red-eh Beh-beh?”

Son Seung-wan held her smile.

“Red. Velvet.”

The guard tried just as hard.

“Red-eh... Beh-beh?”

“......”

The dignity of a Canadian upbringing was taking heavy fire at a film-set gate.

In the end, Son Seung-wan nodded.

“Yes. The Red-eh Beh-beh.”

The guard nodded back — the two parties had, at last, reached consensus on the pronunciation question — and then added:

“Never heard of them.”

The insulated bag in Bae Joo-hyun’s hand grew noticeably heavier.

The star-feel of a No. 2 chart run and ads blanketing Seoul’s bus stops was punctured, cleanly, by one “never heard of them.”

She had no choice left but her phone.

She scrolled the album and found a photo.

A backstage group photo from a League of Legends Season 4 event the previous year.

Back then, to shrink the height gap, she had secretly risen onto her toes; the spacing in the shot sat much closer than any ordinary senior-junior photo, too.

But the version saved in her phone now held only two people at its center.

Baek Si-on.

And Bae Joo-hyun, standing beside him.

She handed the phone to the guard.

“Look.”

The guard took it and bent over the photo.

Son Seung-wan drifted in closer without meaning to.

She remembered this group photo.

Remembered it with perfect clarity.

Because the original had her in it.

And Kang Seul-gi and Park Soo-young too.

But now Baek Si-on remained in the frame, whole.

Bae Joo-hyun remained as well.

The three of them had been subjected to one precise digital purge — not one strand of hair granted a preservation quota.

The photo, as edited

Original cast: five. Remaining: two. Method: crop, seamless.

Exhibit A in the leader’s private archive — and, coincidentally, the exact moment Son Seung-wan’s survival instincts re-holstered themselves.

Son Seung-wan turned her face toward the leader, slowly.

Bae Joo-hyun felt the presence, and met it calmly.

Set against the evidence of “the corridor dance, witnessed in full,” photo-cropping no longer carried much killing power.

“Did you see something?”

Son Seung-wan’s survival system fired up at once; she raised a hand and rubbed the corner of her eye.

“The wind blew some sand in just now. My vision’s a little blurry.”

Bae Joo-hyun nodded lightly.

Good.

The OST-audition seal was holding steady.

“Ye?”

The ad was red. The song was red.

But any single member’s personal recognition was, evidently, still out there chasing.

The ramp to the shooting area ran downward.

Lid closed for now — the stunt coordinator and the armorer standing beside it.

Baek Si-on stood there.

Black hair cropped to the scalp, an old jacket on his frame, dark stains on the cuff.

If Baek Si-on personally manned the gate, even their gimbap would probably have to clear security.

“Unnie.”

“Mm?”

“You could perform ‘Like Being Hit by a Bullet’ live.”

Singing at actual gunpoint had left the realm of emotional experience behind.

That was called immersive music education.

The muzzle flash tore a white-orange gash through the gloom.

The sound ricocheted off the concrete walls, bouncing through the whole space several times before it died.

Bae Joo-hyun’s shoulders flinched, lightly.

Son Seung-wan ducked her neck and grabbed her leader’s hand.

But Baek Si-on in the frame hadn’t moved at all.

His eyes had never blinked; his sightline bore down hard on the far target.

The gunshot had failed to cut the character’s state.

Recoil lifted the muzzle; his wrist rose with it and settled, steady, back into place.

“Cut.”

❧ ❧ ❧

At the monitor, Baek Jeong-hoon rewound to just before the trigger and played the whole thing again.

Baek Si-on’s eyes, in the instant the flash appeared: motionless.

He picked up the walkie:

“Approved.”

Son Seung-wan, at the same moment, slowly let go of Bae Joo-hyun’s hand.

She knew nothing of yesterday’s three failures, nor of Baek Si-on staying in the garage to drill until three in the morning.

She had seen only: receive the gun, raise it, fire, director approves.

So she breathed:

“Sunbae’s incredible — he got it in one take.”

Bae Joo-hyun’s gaze was still on Baek Si-on.

He handed the gun to the armorer, a small curve at the corner of his mouth, and said something to Jo Woo-jin.

“Mm.”

She answered absently — and then added:

“So you’d better also get yours in one take.”

The admiration that had just bloomed in Son Seung-wan converted back into pressure on the spot.

“How is anyone guaranteed a one-take audition?”

Bae Joo-hyun pulled her attention back to the member beside her.

“Then start thinking about the key of ‘Like Being Hit by a Bullet’ now.”

Son Seung-wan: “......”

The danger level of this hush-benefit was climbing without pause.

She suddenly felt that being pulled into a room a few nights ago and smothered with a pillow might have been the gentler handling.

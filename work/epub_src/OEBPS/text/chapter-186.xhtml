Forbidden From Increasing America’s GDP With Your Life

After the opening night, the tour team working Baek Si-on stayed late, running the post-mortem around a screen of real-time social data.

The digital debrief · night one, LA · the numbers on the wall

Peak second: the mic being pulled back — “Refund him.”

Digital’s recommendation, minutes old: for tomorrow night’s second LA show, book a comedian for the interaction slot. Manager’s first instinct, out loud: “What about Kevin Hart?” Staffer, delicately: “Will he take it as an invitation or an insult?” Manager, shrugging: “Given his height, he’s probably used to it.”

Had Kevin Hart himself heard that line, backstage might have entered its boxing preliminary early. But the result was: Kevin Hart actually came. The reason was simple — the name Tom Ford Gangster had been circulating in Los Angeles circles for a season, and a comedian would beg for a front-row seat at a furnace-hot show like this.

Kevin Hart

Comedian · professional victim of his own height

1.63, officially · self-deprecation as a business model · the booster seat

America’s hardest-working small package. He came for a front-row seat, declined a public execution with impeccable timing, and walked out of the arena as the night’s second headline.

Mid-show, at the interaction segment, Baek Si-on walked to the edge of the stage and scanned the floor.

“We have a very special friend here tonight.”

Camera and follow-spot ran his gaze straight to Kevin Hart.

“Kevin.”

Kevin Hart shot to his feet, arms spread wide, smile at full wattage. The house roared. Baek Si-on handed him the microphone.

“Give us a bit?”

Kevin waved both hands, fast.

“No, no, no. I saw what you did to James Corden yesterday.”

“I refuse to be publicly executed.”

Baek Si-on kept the mic level, and asked:

“Then why did you stand up?”

Kevin spread his hands with total sincerity.

“Because standing is the only way people can see me.”

The laughter broke like a wave. The height bit was, in American comedy circles, public property — the official number read 1.63 meters, but in paparazzi frames, standing next to Dwayne Johnson, the man looked like a custom figurine that came with the Dwayne. He knew his lane perfectly; extreme self-deprecation was how he ran his empire.

Baek Si-on reclaimed the microphone and nodded, thoughtful.

“Somebody go to back — get him a booster seat.”

Kevin Hart melted down on the spot — pointing at the stage, protesting at full volume, even winding up a take-his-shoe-off-and-throw-it windup, the physical comedy dragging the room to the absolute edge of detonation. Baek Si-on stood on the stage and let the laughter recede at its own pace.

“All right.”

“Now — I want to introduce you to a friend.”

The arena quieted.

“Not a lot of people know him yet.”

“I believe that’s about to change.”

From stage left walked a tall, thin young white man.

Charlie Puth. Sitting down at the grand piano at center stage, he wiped his palm on his trouser leg — visibly. Days ago he had been a YouTube kid one fifty-thousand-dollar buyout away from having his fate signed away by a label. Now he sat on the stage of Baek Si-on’s North American tour, twenty thousand faces in front of him. The span was the kind where someone grabs your life’s progress bar and drags it straight into next season.

The piano started — first the nose-pricking, heavy-slow intro of ‘See You Again’, then a smooth slide into the gentleness of ‘One Call Away’. Charlie’s accompaniment carried Baek Si-on’s voice the whole way. One man standing on the summit; one man freshly out of the valley. On a stage, though, there is only the music.

LA, night two · the piano medley · Charlie at the keys

“It’s been a long day without you, my friend—”

“And I’ll tell you all about it when I see you again—”

— the seam —

“Call me, baby, if you need a friend—”

“I’m only one call away—”

When it ended, Baek Si-on walked to the piano.

“His name is Charlie Puth.”

Charlie rose off the bench and waved, a little stiff.

“And now —”

Baek Si-on handed him the microphone.

“You can introduce yourself with your own voice.”

He patted Charlie’s shoulder and simply handed a twenty-thousand-person stage, still hot, into the arms of a newcomer. Charlie gripped the microphone — still warm from someone else’s set — looked out at an audience that hadn’t finished burning, turned to the drummer, and snapped his fingers.

The low-end detonated. ‘Look At Me Now’ came up. It was a song about proving yourself — about everyone betting against you, and you standing here anyway.

After the show, Charlie Puth stood in the corridor in a daze. He didn’t resurface until Baek Si-on clapped his shoulder.

“Good?”

“GOOD!”

Five minutes of stage time — but twenty thousand people waving and clapping along was the best feeling he had ever had. Good enough that he finally understood why people sold slices of their souls to labels just to stand on a stage.

“I almost took off! I nearly FLEW!”

Baek Si-on let the emotion settle for a few seconds.

“Done flying? Then work arrives.”

“…What?”

Baek Si-on slid both hands into his pockets and leaned on the wall.

“I need a Middle East exclusive. For Dubai.”

Charlie blinked twice. His emotions had been at “I will write this moment into my memoir” altitude; one sentence brought him back down to his desk.

“…What genre?”

“I want the feeling of a desert slowly opening its eyes.”

Charlie: “……”

Desert. Eyes. Opening. Was this a song brief or a literature assignment?

“Deadline?”

“Two weeks at the outside. If it’s taken, you get thirty percent of the publishing.”

Charlie went quiet for a while. He was a signed artist now; writing for the boss was only natural. But. Thirty percent of the publishing!

“I think the desert can indeed slowly open its eyes. One condition.”

“Say it.”

“A plane ticket to Dubai. I’ve never touched sand. How am I supposed to write this?”

“You could watch documentaries.”

“Nobody ever wrote a good song from a documentary!”

Baek Si-on had been about to say that Beethoven wrote symphonies deaf — but what actually came out was:

“Go. Get the receipt to my sister.”

Charlie was mid-victory-motion when the addendum landed.

“Economy, though.”

“……”

Fine. The dignity of the working man had made its final concession on the subject of cabin class.

After Charlie left, Baek Si-on brought the same matter to Scooter.

“Genius idea.”

Scooter raised a thumb, slowly.

“When the Dubai crown prince hears it, he’ll probably send you a purebred Arabian horse.”

In that part of the world, a hundred and fifty thousand dollars to have someone sing two songs counted as a casual way of expressing affection — and a song custom-commissioned for your city fit their aesthetic exactly: I have money, therefore the world should be customized for me, romantically.

“Where would I keep a horse?”

Scooter: “……”

The man’s first question about any gift was always more practical than the gift.

“Forget the horse. Qatar can’t be left out either. If Dubai gets an exclusive and Doha doesn’t, over there they’ll read it as being insulted through music.”

Baek Si-on considered it. Very reasonable.

“Then find someone.”

“On it now.”

Scooter turned to go, and turned back at the door.

“Baek.”

“Mm?”

“This Middle East leg of yours — it might not be a tour anymore.”

“Then what is it?”

“A diplomatic-incident prevention program.”

“……”

I need you to make a song for me.

…Are there no producers in America?

There are. But you understand me.

He had been primed to roll his eyes — and discovered the argument was, infuriatingly, unanswerable. After this long working together, Jung Jae-joon had Baek Si-on’s expression logic fully mapped: he knew when the man genuinely had an idea, and when he was simply using one abstract sentence to manufacture insomnia for a producer.

Requirements?

For Doha. Desert, a bay, night, and golden buildings.

……

He leaned back in his chair and stared at the studio ceiling. Suddenly nostalgic for the old days, when the worst he got was vocal-slice surgery and a request to make something harder. Now it opened straight into desert, gilded coastline, and the face-saving requirements of Middle Eastern nations. An international superstar’s producer was not an easy job. It involved significantly more geopolitics than advertised.

Fine. I’ll try.

Thanks.

A scoff came down the line.

Spare me. Every time you say “thanks,” a pile of trouble is riding right behind it.

This one pays.

…Then it’s genuinely not so hard after all.

He hung up, looked at the phone screen in silence for a moment, and pocketed it. One song for Dubai. One song for Doha. Two Middle Eastern cities, still weeks from a downbeat, and the people responsible for writing, commissioning, arranging, and preventing diplomatic incidents were already losing sleep on schedule.

Baek Si-on felt very little guilt about this. He was, after all, paying well. A person may fail to understand art in this life. A person may not fail to understand publishing splits.

❧ ❧ ❧

After the second Los Angeles show, the tour truly left Baek Si-on’s temporary American home base.

First stop: San Francisco. Or precisely, the Bay Area.

The line outside the arena had formed by three in the afternoon, and it carried a distinct local flavor. They didn’t necessarily do support chants. They didn’t necessarily shout slogans. Plenty of them couldn’t pronounce his Korean name cleanly. But they wore tech-company hoodies, carried backpacks, held laptops still warm from the office, and answered Slack messages while they queued.

Baek Eun-ah watched a white guy in a search-engine-logo tee hold a BodyArmor in one hand and a Bibigo dumpling sample cup in the other, telling his friend:

“His Spotify growth curve is insane.”

“It’s not just growth. Look at the retention.”

Her eyebrows jumped. This was the first time she had watched someone discuss her cousin the way a product manager discusses retention. Even better: the Asian girl next to them nodded and added:

“His fan-conversion numbers are excellent.”

Baek Eun-ah concluded that in this city even fandom arrived pre-loaded with a Series A pitch deck. Inside, the flavor held. San Francisco didn’t scream itself hoarse from second one the way Los Angeles had. It warmed slowly — through the first songs, a lot of the room was still observing.

Then the intro of ‘Human’ began, and the stage light pressed down from blazing gold into cold white. Baek Si-on stood in it, and his voice went low. And the people who spent their days in glass offices writing code, building decks, and arguing about user growth finally set down the rational shell, lifted their heads, and sang along.

The next day, several Bay Area tech outlets ran a headline.

The Bay Area press · the morning after San Francisco

“A Korean pop star conquered Silicon Valley without speaking the language.”

Cousin’s question: “Is this headline a compliment?” Follow-up: “Complimented where, exactly?” Artist’s translation, after one glance: “It says I sold out the room without speaking human.” — The cousin has resolved to translate fewer English headlines for him in the future.

Vancouver’s climate was an entirely different thing.

If San Francisco had been a user-behavior validation study conducted by tech employees, Vancouver was an echo arriving years late. From the airport to the hotel to the arena gates, Asian faces ran the whole route — Korean students, second-generation immigrants, girls livestreaming to friends in Seoul, middle-aged parents dragged along by daughters, young people chatting in Chinese, Japanese, Vietnamese, Tagalog. Different languages, different light sticks; some of them had never come to him through Hallyu at all. But in this city, the sight of an Asian man standing at the center of a North American main stage landed differently — closer to the bone.

Seven thirty at night. The lights sank. When the intro of ‘Way Back Home’ began, a Korean girl in the front row suddenly started crying. Baek Si-on didn’t notice at first — until, before the chorus, he extended the microphone toward the audience. And that section of the crowd answered in Korean, singing.

For one instant, even the band froze. On the previous North American nights, when audiences sang along, they sang the English songs. In Vancouver, a Korean chorus pressed down over the top of the PA system for the first time.

‘Way Back Home’ · Vancouver · the chorus that came back at him in Korean

In the night sky above my hometown, the stars may rise

But in my heart, only the lamplight of home remains

I want to go back, I want to go back

To that road I walked holding my mother’s hand

— and on the crowd’s last syllable he lifted his own mic and sent the next line back to them, making it up as the chords turned —

The lamplight never went out; it waited where we left it

So tonight, from every far corner — come home

Baek Si-on stood at the front of the stage, watching the field of lit sticks and phone screens, and when the chorus finished he said, in Korean:

“Thank you for coming from so far away.”

The scream that answered nearly lifted the roof. He paused, and gave it again, in English this time:

“Thank you for finding your way here.”

The applause came in from every corner of the building. Not like Los Angeles — hot and outward. Not like San Francisco — a beat behind. It sounded like people who had lived too long in a foreign country hearing, in a strange city, a doorbell they recognized.

Backstage, Baek Eun-ah felt her eye sockets go inexplicably hot. She used to think cultural export was a word that was too big to hold. That night in Vancouver it turned concrete. Concrete as a girl crying through a Korean chorus. Concrete as a Korean father standing in the seats, awkwardly holding up the light stick his daughter had stuffed into his hand. Concrete as what someone shouted from the floor after the thank-you:

“No — thank YOU.”

By the time the tour reached New York, the whole machine was running in a state of high mechanization — airport, hotel, rehearsal, show, post-mortem, next city. Baek Si-on woke each morning and needed to check the weather app to confirm which city he was in.

The media density of New York outran every prior stop. Outside the arena, mixed in with the fans: entertainment press, fashion bloggers, music-channel camera crews, and a scrum of scalpers who had materialized from who knew where. This city never lacked celebrities. It never lacked concerts. But it possessed an almost animal nose for a phenomenon as it happened — and right now, Baek Si-on was the phenomenon.

New York’s first-night guest list: Ariana. Baek Eun-ah watched the real-time social trends and felt her scalp prickle.

“Cousin — her fan army fights hard too.”

“Mm.”

“Two of you on one stage — Scooter is going to laugh himself to sleep tonight.”

Baek Si-on glanced toward the distance, where Ariana was confirming in-ears with the vocal coach.

“Scooter has many reasons to lose sleep.”

Such as: two label artists on one stage, and the press writes the SB Projects dynasty. Such as: a certain Bieber seeing the trending list and dialing again. Such as: if tonight lands too well, every remaining city’s fans start demanding her.

Eight-forty p.m. ‘Love Yourself’ ended, and the arena lights dropped. Baek Si-on stood at center stage and pressed a hand to his ear.

“New York.”

The crowd answered like a detonation.

“Tonight’s guest could crack this roof with one note.”

Before the screen even switched, the screaming had already started — and then Ariana stepped out of the wings into the light, ponytail up high, looking for all the world like a sugar sculpture wrapped in crystal. When she opened her mouth on the first line, the invisible glass over the arena genuinely seemed to crack.

She had chosen ‘Problem’. The brass hit, and the whole venue switched from Baek Si-on’s heavy, low, cinema-flavored live to something bright, bratty, and utterly unreasonable — a pop party with the safety off. He stood beside her handling the low harmonies; she climbed the highs. One voice was a sugar-coated bomb, the other black leather — and, improbably, they fit.

After the second chorus she turned to him. He tipped his head.

“Your lung capacity should be illegal.”

She returned fire instantly:

“So should your face.”

The room froze one second — then laughter and screaming detonated together. Even Scooter, backstage, laughed out loud. Label-mates roasting each other: safe, and edits beautifully. The digital team had tonight’s viral clip pre-judged before the song ended. As she left the stage he gave her a thumbs-up; she answered with a theatrical blown kiss — nothing ambiguous in it, just two professionals who both knew they were manufacturing news for each other, meeting at the center of the ring for a clean high-five. The audience, meanwhile, was not accountable to subtext. The audience was there to scream.

❧ ❧ ❧

The show rolled on. When the final section of ‘Legend’ began, Baek Si-on walked to the very tip of the thrust stage and let his eyes run along the first row.

And stopped.

First row, right side: several familiar Korean faces. Long lenses. Name-placards. Fan-site masters — fansite noonas. Specifically: fansite masters who had flown in from Korea to New York.

His hand on the microphone stilled for one second. Then he finished the last chorus. The lights fell; applause and screaming flooded the building. He bowed to the crowd and turned off the stage — and in the backstage corridor, passing Baek Eun-ah with the water, said:

“The Korean fansite masters, first row right. Bring them to me.”

❧ ❧ ❧

When the three Korean fansite masters were escorted into the New York arena’s green room, their faces still wore the daze of the deeply unreal. They had assumed this was just another show shot from the crowd — at absolute most, seen for a blink from the stage. Nobody could have imagined that after the show a staffer would approach and ask, in very polite English, whether they could come along. All three nearly ascended on the spot.

They were still whispering in the corridor.

“Could it be photos? Together?”

“No way. It’ll be an autograph.”

“I got him and Ariana in one frame today. The composition is perfect. Do you think he saw it?”

“Ya, calm down. Don’t lead with the photos.”

“Then what do we say?”

“Say… ‘Oppa, you’ve worked hard’?”

“Too ordinary.”

“Say we flew from Korea to support you?”

“That one has to move him, right?”

The three exchanged a look and reached instant consensus: moved. He would definitely be moved. Incheon to New York — a dozen-plus hours, connections, jet lag, hotels, tickets, transit. Summed, it was enough to bankrupt a normal office worker on the spot. They had proven their love with actions.

Then the door opened.

The three of them swung into formation instantly:

“Si-on oppa, you’ve worked hard!”

“The concert was really, really amazing!”

“We came from Korea—”

“Who told you to come?”

All three voices cut off mid-syllable. The girl at the front still wore the remains of an about-to-be-praised expression, which collided head-on with a face calm to the point of temperatureless.

She hugged her camera tighter, on reflex.

“Ne?”

He looked at her.

“I asked: who told you to come?”

“We… came ourselves.”

“How many of you?”

“Three.”

“When did you land in New York?”

“Last night.”

“Where are you staying?”

The three looked at each other. One of them reported a hotel name, quietly. Baek Eun-ah checked the tablet, and her brow came down at once. The place wasn’t dangerous, exactly. It was simply not near the arena, and getting back at night meant two subway transfers — for three young women whose Korean didn’t stretch far and who were carrying cameras worth a used car, it was already enough to raise anyone’s blood pressure.

Baek Si-on said nothing. The less he said, the tighter the three of them wound. Fan-site masters who, five minutes ago, had been expecting a top-tier fan-service moment, were finally catching up to the fact that this was heading somewhere else entirely.

He pointed at the girl in front.

“Warm Light?”

She froze.

“Ne?”

“Your site name is Warm Light. Right?”

Her eyes went round.

“Oppa remembers me?”

The other two exchanged a look compounded of envy, jealousy, and your life is charmed. He did not ride that warmth anywhere.

“Last year, Incheon airport. You ran too fast and went down by the railing.”

Warm Light: “……”

The other two: “……”

Baek Eun-ah studied her tablet and felt her shoulders shake once. What caliber of public execution was this. The happiness Warm Light had just raised — my bias remembers me — had been flattened, on the spot, by the fact my bias remembers me falling down.

And he kept going.

“If you can fall over at Incheon, then New York, midnight, two subway transfers back to a hotel — do you think I should feel OK about that?”

“We’ll be careful…”

“Careful is useless. This isn’t Seoul. You don’t know the streets, you don’t know the blocks, you don’t know which areas are safe after midnight or who to call when something goes wrong.”

“One girl, one camera, a strange city, a hotel at midnight.”

“That isn’t romance.”

“That’s adding to my psychological load.”

Three heads bowed. One of them couldn’t help it, small-voiced:

“But we only wanted to support you…”

“I know. Which is why I’m still speaking to you nicely.”

“……”

It sounded as though anything short of support would have gotten them thrown out bodily.

He looked at the three of them.

“If something had actually happened to you — how would I explain it to your families?”

No one answered, because the question had no answer. Fans always believe their pilgrimage is a romance. Across cities, across countries, across time zones — as though the farther the distance, the truer the love. But Baek Si-on dismantled the thing into its parts and laid them on the table. Airport. Hotel. Subway. Midnight. Camera. Parents. Lined up like that, romance abruptly turned into a risk list.

The youngest of the three had gone red around the eyes.

“Oppa — we didn’t mean to worry you.”

“I know.”

His tone finally loosened, a degree.

“Which is why I didn’t yell at you.”

All three heads came up at once. That wasn’t yelling?

Baek Eun-ah chose her moment:

“Flights are already booked.”

The three of them froze.

“What flights?”

“Tomorrow morning. New York to Incheon.”

Baek Eun-ah turned the tablet toward them.

“Your hotel’s changed too — tonight you’re near the airport. A guard drives you there, and your luggage gets delivered to the gate in the morning.”

Three faces went completely blank. Warm Light recovered first.

“W-wait — we still have tickets. For the other shows.”

Baek Si-on: “Refund them.”

“They don’t refund…”

“Then write it off as charity for the American economy.”

“……”

Another one tried, tiny-voiced:

“But oppa — after New York there’s still Chicago…”

He looked at her. She shut up instantly.

Baek Eun-ah had considered smoothing things over — and then looked at their faces and swallowed it. He wasn’t chasing fans away. Exactly the opposite. He was lifting three girls out of a swamp of self-romanticizing. They were fans. But before they were fans, they were first three young women — who get tired, who get lost, who take the midnight subway to save a cab fare, who put themselves inside a strange city’s risks for one photograph. He did not like this kind of love. It was too expensive. Too expensive to be paid for by the fans.

“Of course —”

Baek Si-on picked up three signed tour posters from the side table, then had a staffer bring in three merch bundles. Collab hoodie. Tour cap. Signed album. One bottle of BodyArmor. One Bibigo dumpling voucher. Baek Eun-ah eyed the last item:

“Cousin — is the dumpling voucher necessary? They can eat those at home any day.”

He thought about it. Fair point.

The three of them stood holding the bundles, not knowing whether to laugh or cry. They had, technically, been scolded. They had, technically, been forcibly shipped back to their country. And the merch in their arms weighed on them like some preposterous compensation.

“Go home safe.”

The staffer came to walk them out. At the door, Warm Light couldn’t help turning back for one last small question:

“Oppa.”

“Mm?”

“Then… can we take a photo?”

He looked at her for two seconds.

“Allowed.”

Three pairs of eyes lit at once. Baek Eun-ah raised the phone. Three fansite masters, freshly disciplined to roughly the emotional temperature of schoolchildren caught passing notes, stood beside him hugging their bundles. Just before the shutter, he said:

“You may post it.”

Happiness, upgraded. And then:

“But don’t write that I invited you to New York.”

“……”

Warm Light, faintly:

“Then… how should we write it?”

He thought about it.

“Write that you were deported.”

“……”

Baek Eun-ah pressed the shutter at the exact instant three faces hung suspended between bliss and collapse. Entirely authentic. Entirely suited for posting.

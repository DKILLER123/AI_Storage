Chapter One Hundred Thirty Two

More Concrete Than the Sea of People at Incheon: the Candle Before Him

The same afternoon Just after lunch Beverly Hills · a black SUV on Rodeo Drive Old money buys futures. Baek Si-on is quietly doing arithmetic about a certain fat man he invested in.

When the car pulled away from the French restaurant in Beverly Hills, the Los Angeles afternoon sun lay at an angle across the glass towers, and palm shadows fell on the windows in sections as the black SUV crossed each intersection — like film stock being stretched and cut by an editor.

Baek Si-on sat back in the rear leather seat.

Scooter Braun sat beside him, emailing the legal team on his phone — have the Under Armour contract terms turned into formal text by tonight.

The car was quiet for a few minutes.

Then Baek Si-on spoke:

“Scooter.”

“Mm?”

Scooter’s thumb stopped on the screen.

He turned to look at Baek Si-on.

“Thank you. For getting me all of this.”

The “thank you” Baek Si-on meant was not, of course, for the way Scooter had fought across the lunch table — pushing the ten-million-three-year figure toward a sports-fashion line, a sales share, a performance clause.

That was simply Scooter doing his job.

What Baek Si-on was actually thanking him for was the structural move Scooter had made at the table — folded quietly into the terms he had pushed through.

The front-loaded contract.

The signing fee that could have made a banker weep.

Not the slow drip of three-plus million a year — but six million in cash, arriving the moment the contract was signed.

That mattered enormously to Baek Si-on right now.

He had just set up a studio.

There was tax to pay. A team to feed. Overseas runs to fund. Investments to make.

Money was not everything. But cash flow was what let a man face the world without bowing his head quite so often.

Scooter understood.

He locked his phone, put it down, and leaned back into the seat.

“Baek.”

“Yes?”

“Do you know why I like you?”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

“Because I make money.”

“That is a very important part.”

Scooter nodded.

“But not all of it.”

He smiled.

“A lot of artists, the moment they get a Billboard number one, start to feel like the whole world owes them. The manager should fight for their big contracts as a matter of course. The team should work all night as a matter of course. Every resource should just flow toward them automatically.”

“Not you.”

“You say thank you.”

Baek Si-on was silent for a second.

“I don’t say it much.”

“Which is exactly why it’s worth more.”

Scooter said.

“Last time Bieber landed a twenty-million-dollar tour contract, you know how he thanked me?”

“How?”

“He sent me a message that said ‘cool.’ Then he went off to chase girls.”

Baek Si-on:

“……”

He suddenly felt that although he and Bieber were very different people in many ways, they might be the same species on the specific matter of “reply messages using as few characters as possible.”

As the car passed along Rodeo Drive, Baek Si-on watched the watches, the jewellery, the handmade leather goods arranged in the shop windows, and asked out of nowhere:

“You’ve made so much money. What do you usually do with it?”

Scooter raised an eyebrow.

“You’re asking me about personal finance?”

“Yes.”

“Why the sudden interest?”

“Curiosity.”

Baek Si-on said.

“Buy a yacht?”

Scooter froze for a second. Then he started laughing — a low, quiet laugh, as if he had just heard the most absurd joke in the world.

“Baek. A yacht is the toy of a man who got rich overnight. It’s a bad deal.”

“Oh?”

“Real old money buys the future.”

Baek Si-on turned to look at him.

“Such as?”

“Such as Uber. Such as Pinterest. Such as… Spotify.”

“You invested in those?”

“Yes. Early angel rounds.”

“……”

Baek Si-on had not expected that this man had already, this early, put money into Uber and Pinterest and Spotify.

These Jews really were…

Scooter watched him.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That face does not say nothing.”

Baek Si-on looked out the window and said nothing.

He was thinking of Bang Si-hyuk.

The fat man he had invested in. In the future, that fat man would buy out Scooter’s company for a billion dollars.

His heart, suddenly, felt very balanced.

Jewish, is it?

Good at investing, is it?

In the future you’ll still quietly become a very well-paid employee.

Scooter could not read his thoughts. He only felt that Baek Si-on’s expression had suddenly turned extremely subtle.

“Baek.”

“Yes?”

“I keep feeling you were just thinking something not very friendly toward me.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You denied it too fast.”

“Then I’ll deny it slower?”

Scooter:

“……”

Fine.

This man had begun learning how to fight back with words.

Very bad development.

November 1 · Seoul time 6:17 a.m. Incheon International Airport · the arrivals hall If one word had to describe it: paralysis.

Fans. Media. Onlookers. And a scattering of ordinary travellers who had no idea what was happening, caught in the current, unable to move, staring around from behind their luggage.

This time the three major broadcasters had not sent mere interview crews.

This was full-channel live coverage.

KBS · morning news · live from Incheon

KBS cut its morning-news signal straight to the airport. The anchor sat in the studio, listening to the field reporter through her earpiece. In the top-left corner of the screen, a red caption:

[BREAKING] Baek Si-on arrives at Incheon International Airport · LIVE

MBC · morning news · live from Incheon

MBC went further. On the second-floor observation deck opposite the arrivals exit, they set up three cameras, shooting down across the whole hall. In the frame, the crowd churned like bubbles on a boiling surface.

SBS · news channel · aerial

SBS was the maddest of all. They had a helicopter. It circled above Incheon airport right now, the aerial shot dropping from five hundred metres:

The grey terminal. The Korean Air jet still slowing on the runway. Ground vehicles on the apron. And, outside the exit on the first floor, a black, dense tide of people slowly surging.

The SBS news channel’s aerial picture ran with the commentator’s voice:

SBS · news channel · the commentary track

“Viewers — you are looking at live pictures of Incheon International Airport. Baek Si-on — the first Korean singer to top the American Billboard Hot 100 — having completed his Lakers season-opener performance and his Billboard victory in the United States, is returning to Korea this morning on a Korean Air flight—”

In the aerial frame, the jet designated KE018 was easing toward its stand.

The commentator’s voice paused for a beat.

“We can now see that the airport has deployed a considerable security force…”

The shot pushed in.

Outside the terminal exit.

A line of figures in black uniforms was forming up fast. Riot helmets. Tactical vests. The patch on each sleeve read “Police Special Forces.”

They spaced themselves two to a group and drew a human corridor between the arrivals exit and the terminal doors — one row on the left, one on the right, facing the crowd, backs to the corridor.

In fact, the last time the Korean police had deployed security on this scale at Incheon had been when the female president returned from an overseas visit.

✿ ✿ ✿

Baek Eun-ah stood in the jet bridge and looked out once through the terminal glass. Then she turned back toward Baek Si-on, who was having his look adjusted by Park Ji-hun.

Baek Si-on looked at her.

“What is it? Lots of people outside?”

Baek Eun-ah opened her mouth.

At this airport, at this hour, the word “lots” was probably the understatement of the year.

“Oppa.”

“Yes?”

“I think… you may want to prepare yourself.”

“For what?”

Baek Eun-ah thought for a long time. She was trying to find the right comparison.

At last she said:

“MacArthur.”

Baek Si-on:

“……”

MacArthur. Coming ashore. The single most symbolically loaded “arrival” image in the country’s modern history.

Either her historical rhetoric had suddenly found a second wind, or the scene outside really had reached that level.

Baek Si-on chose to believe the latter.

“Let’s go.”

The two bodyguards went first. Kang on the left. Han on the right.

Park Ji-hun and Baek Eun-ah walked behind Baek Si-on’s shoulders.

The five of them moved down the arrivals corridor toward the exit.

The corridor was long. Footsteps echoed in the empty space.

And the further they walked, the clearer the sound from outside became.

At first it was a hum. Vague. Blurred.

Then, at the last corner of the corridor, the sound detonated to full volume.

“Aaaaaaaaah——!!!”

“Baek Si-on!!!”

“He’s coming he’s coming he’s coming!”

“Over there! Look over there!”

“Ah——he’s out!!!”

At the instant the automatic doors opened, Baek Si-on’s eyes closed by reflex.

A thousand phones and several dozen professional cameras fired their flashes at once from within two metres. The density of that white light could no longer be described as “blinding.” It was closer to someone pouring a bucket of liquid sun directly onto his retinas.

A second later, a pair of sunglasses was settled firmly onto the bridge of his nose.

Baek Si-on opened his eyes.

The dark lenses cut seventy percent of the brightness out of that sea of white.

Now he could finally see the scene clearly.

Well, then.

MacArthur had been conservative.

Inside the arrivals hall, the crowd pressed toward the exit in layers, as if caught by some invisible magnetism. At the front stood the reporters — telephoto lenses, shoulder cameras, boom mics, piled into a breathing wall. Behind them, the fans: banners, light sticks, his name, Legend lyrics. And several enormous printed screenshots of the Billboard chart. At the outermost edge, ordinary travellers who had no idea what was happening.

Baek Si-on stood outside the automatic doors.

He paused for one second.

Then he did one thing.

He took the sunglasses off.

Exposing himself again to that white storm of flashes.

And, facing the crowd straight ahead, he bowed deeply.

The instant his waist bent, the noise of the whole arrivals hall turned up as though someone had wrenched the volume knob one full rotation.

“Aaaaaaaaaah——!!!!!”

Not one section screaming.

The entire hall.

From directly in front of the exit, to the second-floor observation deck, to the corridors on either side, to the people on tiptoe by the distant check-in counters — every voice poured together and formed, beneath the steel arches of Incheon International Airport, a single sustained resonance that pressed against the eardrums.

Baek Si-on straightened.

Put the sunglasses back on.

And took his first step toward the corridor.

SBS · news channel · live pictures

The aerial camera dropped from five hundred metres. The commentator’s voice flowed above the frame.

“Viewers — Baek Si-on has now walked out of the arrivals exit. From the aerial shot we can see—”

The helicopter descended slowly. In the frame, the plaza and corridors outside the exit were a black, packed mass — like a circuit board crammed full of components. Then, through the middle of the crowd, a seam began to split. From the exit toward the terminal doors. The seam was narrow — less than two metres wide — bordered on both sides by the black wall of the police special forces.

And at the centre of that narrow seam, a small black dot was moving.

The aerial shot held on that single frame for three full seconds.

The commentator stopped talking.

He did not continue his narration.

Because the picture had already said everything that needed saying:

From five hundred metres up, a man was walking slowly down the middle of a human sea, along a narrow road that had been parted for him. On both sides, the crowd rippled with his movement like water. People jumped. People waved. People shook their banners. In the distance, more were still running toward him.

And the man walked slowly.

Because every few steps he stopped. Bowed slightly to one side. Or raised a hand. Or nodded.

Not as a performance.

Because he knew these people had arrived at two or three in the morning. Some of them had not slept the night before. Some had travelled in from other cities. Some were ordinary people who had decided on the spur of the moment after seeing the news.

They stood here. Held up their banners. Shouted his name.

All for one look at him as he passed through the corridor.

That was worth walking slowly for.

The SBS camera stayed with him the whole way — from the exit to the moment he climbed into the van.

The second-hand Kia Carnival waited in the temporary lane outside the terminal doors.

The door slid open.

Before he got in, Baek Si-on turned one last time.

Facing the whole direction of the arrivals hall.

From where he stood he could no longer see the people inside. Only the dense, shifting mass of figures behind the glass wall.

He took off the sunglasses.

Raised his right hand.

And waved once, in that direction.

Then he bowed.

One final bow.

He got in.

The door closed.

The old Carnival’s engine turned over in the grey morning of Incheon airport.

The driver put the van in gear, pulled out of the temporary lane, and merged onto the airport expressway.

In the SBS live frame, the aerial camera caught the full picture of the Carnival leaving the terminal — a slightly shabby nanny van sliding out of that black sea of people and onto the open highway.

Smaller and smaller.

Farther and farther.

Until it became a tiny point of light moving toward Seoul beneath the pale blue-grey morning sky.

The commentator finally spoke again.

“Baek Si-on-ssi’s vehicle has now left Incheon International Airport.”

He paused.

“Let us wish him—”

Another pause.

“—a safe journey.”

The same morning Just past 9:30 a.m. Jeonju · Gyeonggijeon Shrine Incense, a pig’s head, and a film crew holding their breath.

Jeonju. Gyeonggijeon Shrine.

A little past nine-thirty, the crew had already set the offering table for the production-start ritual in front of the main hall.

Incense burner.

A pig’s head.

Fruit.

Bolts of silk.

Staff murmured through the running order. Cameras and the clapperboard stood to one side. A few crew members in padded jackets trotted back and forth along the stone path.

The Sado production was holding its kickoff ceremony here today.

True, Sado was not the kind of project that depended on a fandom to push first-weekend ticket numbers. It lived on performance, script, and a director’s re-reading of a historical figure.

But once Baek Si-on’s name joined the project, the imaginative ceiling changed instantly —

A Venice Best Actor, next stop: the Crown Prince Sado. That alone was enough to get the media excited well in advance.

So even though this kickoff ceremony had not been announced publicly, reporters had still shown up outside Gyeonggijeon.

Quite a few of them.

They had not chased him from Incheon airport. They were smarter than that. They knew that at the airport there were three networks broadcasting live, a sea of fans, and police special forces clearing the road — squeezing in there would not buy them a good position anyway.

So they had simply come early and staked out the production.

Betting that Baek Si-on — even straight off a fourteen-hour flight from Los Angeles — would not miss the Sado kickoff ceremony.

Judging by the scene now, the bet had paid off.

Around ten o’clock, a black nanny van pulled into the temporary control zone.

Before the van had even fully stopped, every telephoto lens in the press area was already up.

“He’s here!”

“Baek Si-on is here!”

“This way, this way!”

The door slid open. Two bodyguards got out first and boxed a narrow safe space in front of the door, one on each side.

The moment Baek Si-on stepped out, the reporters closed in.

Gyeonggijeon · the press pack · the questions come in a volley

“Baek Si-on-ssi! What does Billboard number one feel like?”

“You came straight from the airport to Jeonju — can your body take it?”

“Human passed 120,000 pre-orders in Korea on its first day. How do you feel about that?”

“Coming back and heading straight to the Sado ceremony — does this mean you’ll officially shift your focus back to acting?”

“America is calling you Tom Ford Gangster. What do you make of that?”

“And about the NBA court controversy — now that Korean public opinion has turned, is there anything you’d like to say?”

“In Sado you play Song Kang-ho’s son — is that a lot of pressure?”

Baek Si-on absorbed the barrage for a moment before he answered.

“On the Billboard matter — thank you, everyone.”

The shutters fired in a solid wave.

“Sado is a very important work. I’ll prepare for it properly.”

Another wave of shutters.

A reporter pressed:

“And the NBA court controversy?”

Baek Si-on looked toward that direction.

“Today is the Sado kickoff ceremony.”

The reporter tried to follow up, but Baek Eun-ah stepped in immediately:

“Questions on that will be handled in one statement from the studio. Please, today — let’s focus on the film.”

Baek Si-on bowed slightly.

Then, escorted by the bodyguards, he went into the inner grounds of Gyeonggijeon.

The reporters could not follow him any further.

But it was enough.

There were pictures. There were questions. There were answers. And there was even a sentence — “Today is the Sado kickoff ceremony” — that could be cut into a headline on its own.

Ten minutes later, the first wave of dispatches was already lining up on the news desks.

Korean media · dispatch queue · the first headlines

Sado holds its kickoff ceremony at Gyeonggijeon, Jeonju — Baek Si-on’s first public appearance since the Billboard victory

Korean media · dispatch queue · the first headlines

Baek Si-on heads straight to the Sado set after returning to Korea: “I’ll prepare properly”

Korean media · dispatch queue · the first headlines

Song Kang-ho and Baek Si-on as father and son officially begin; Lee Joon-ik’s new film hot before it has even shot

Korean media · dispatch queue · the first headlines

From Billboard champion to Crown Prince Sado — Baek Si-on restarts the actor’s track

Korean media · dispatch queue · the first headlines

Song Kang-ho and Baek Si-on to play father and son — historical tragedy Sado officially begins in Jeonju today!

Inside the grounds, Director Lee Joon-ik was confirming the running order with the assistant director. When he saw Baek Si-on come in, he looked up.

No theatrical reaction. He simply smiled.

“You’re here.”

Baek Si-on walked over and bowed.

“Director.”

Lee Joon-ik looked him up and down.

“Tired?”

“I’m fine.”

“‘Fine’ means tired.”

Baek Si-on did not argue.

Song Kang-ho was nearby. When he saw Baek Si-on, he laughed.

“Has our Crown Prince returned to court from America?”

The people around laughed.

Baek Si-on smiled a little himself, and bowed properly.

“Sunbae.”

Song Kang-ho waved it off.

“Don’t be so formal.”

He paused, then added:

“Save the formality for when we start shooting. Your Father-King is very strict.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Yes.”

Lee Joon-ik watched the two of them and shook his head with a smile.

“Alright. Bow first. Talk after.”

The kickoff ceremony began.

The director, the leads, the producer, the cinematographer, the art director — each department head stepped forward in turn.

Incense.

Libation.

Bows.

The staff stood at the perimeter holding scripts and two-way radios. A few media photographers were allowed to shoot a fixed frame from a distance. In the lens: Song Kang-ho and Baek Si-on standing on the same side, white smoke rising from the incense burner, and behind them the old tiled eaves of Gyeonggijeon.

Sado was officially in production.

The moment was short.

And very quiet.

There was no mountain-shaking roar as at the airport. No arena lights. Only incense smoke, murmured wishes, and the applause of the crew.

But for the actor Baek Si-on, this was perhaps closer to “going back to work” than any screaming crowd could ever be.

The same day 7:00 p.m. Seoul · CGV Yongsan · the premiere of Green Bottle Fly A film that began in a brown-paper envelope on a coffee table. Tonight it finally meets its home audience.

Seven in the evening. Seoul.

CGV Yongsan. The premiere of Green Bottle Fly.

The film had begun with a script arriving at Baek Si-on’s door in a brown-paper envelope, placed on the coffee table. Then it had gone to Venice.

And tonight was the night it finally faced Korean audiences for real.

Baek Si-on attended.

Baek Jeong-hun attended.

Choi Jin-ri attended.

The Showbox people came too.

The screening.

The post-screening talk.

Questions from the audience.

Applause.

Photographs.

The whole programme was held on an even keel.

Baek Si-on’s answers stayed short. Baek Jeong-hun spoke more. Choi Jin-ri sat to the side, occasionally lowering her head to laugh, occasionally answering seriously when a question was about her character.

Nobody brought up the NBA incident.

At least not here.

Tonight belonged to Green Bottle Fly.

It was never a light film. When the audience came out, their faces carried that particular silence of having been hollowed out.

When the post-screening applause rose, it was not explosive. But it went on for a very long time.

As if no one knew how to set their feelings down, and could only keep clapping.

✿ ✿ ✿

After the premiere programme ended, the back corridor was a controlled chaos.

Baek Jeong-hun had been pulled away by the Showbox people. The media still had supplementary shoots to do. Staff hurried back and forth carrying posters, flower baskets, and interview boards.

Baek Si-on came out of the post-talk hall holding a paper bag.

In it were the souvenirs he had brought back from Dubai.

Camel-milk chocolate. Dates. And a small tin of saffron.

He had been about to find Choi Jin-ri and say something.

But the moment he turned around —

She was gone.

The person who had been sitting right beside him a moment ago had vanished like she had been carried off by the wind, not even a hem of her dress left behind.

Baek Si-on stood at the mouth of the corridor and looked around twice.

Nothing.

“Oppa?”

Baek Eun-ah came over holding a tablet.

“Who are you looking for?”

“Choi Jin-ri.”

“Oh — I think she was called away by a staff member just now.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

Did not think much of it.

On a premiere night, actors getting pulled aside for supplementary interviews, photographs, and content was completely normal.

Baek Si-on handed the paper bag to Baek Eun-ah.

“Give it to her manager.”

“Okay.”

Baek Eun-ah took it, walked two steps, then came back.

“Oppa.”

“Yes?”

“The Showbox distribution head wants to talk to you about something.”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

“Me?”

“Yes.”

“Not Uncle?”

“No.”

“Where?”

“The rest room in the back.”

“Let’s go.”

Baek Eun-ah led the way. The corridor lighting ran dim, and the walls were lined with Green Bottle Fly promotional posters.

Baek Si-on followed her deeper in.

“What is it about?”

“You’ll know when we get there.”

“Distribution problems?”

“Something like that.”

“Box office?”

“Also… sort of related.”

Baek Si-on did not ask again.

The answer sounded like something Baek Eun-ah had improvised on the spot.

But he could not be bothered to call her out on it.

The two of them reached the door of the innermost rest room.

Baek Eun-ah pushed it open.

Inside, it was pitch black.

Baek Si-on raised his hand, reaching for the light switch on the wall.

Baek Eun-ah caught his wrist immediately.

“Wait.”

Baek Si-on looked down at her.

“?”

Baek Eun-ah said nothing.

The next second, from inside the darkness came a very soft sound.

Click.

A lighter.

Then a small flame surfaced out of the dark.

Then a second one.

Flickering light floated up out of the blackness, swaying.

Baek Si-on saw the cake.

White cream. Candles made specially on top.

Billboard.

1.

And behind the candlelight — the face of Choi Jin-ri.

She stood in the darkness holding the cake, her eyes lit bright by the fire.

The person who had “slipped away” a moment ago was now carefully carrying the cake toward him, step by step.

In the rest room with her: Baek Jeong-hun, a few Showbox people, Park Ji-hun, and some staff.

Everyone was quiet.

They had clearly all coordinated in advance.

Baek Si-on stood in the doorway.

For a moment, he did not move.

Choi Jin-ri pressed her lips together, trying to keep her voice light.

“Congratulations.”

She offered the cake a little closer.

“Billboard number one.”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

The flame stood between the two of them.

The fire was very small.

But it lit every flicker of tension, expectation, and barely concealed triumph in the depths of her eyes with perfect clarity.

Baek Si-on stared at her for a long time.

Long enough that the wax began to run down the edge of the “1.”

Under his gaze, Choi Jin-ri could barely hold herself together. Her lashes trembled. Her eyes dropped. Then came back up.

She mumbled, very quietly:

“Why are you staring at me like that…”

A beat. Then, even more quietly:

“Blow out the candles…”

Baek Si-on stepped forward.

Bowed his head. One side of his face lit halfway by the fire.

Drew a deep breath.

Blew.

The orange light left the room.

The darkness closed back in.

And the next second, the lights came on.

Baek Eun-ah was the first to clap.

“Congratulations, Oppa!”

The staff followed.

“Congratulations!”

“Billboard number one!”

“Daebak!”

“Number one in Korea!”

Baek Jeong-hun stood to the side, clapping twice with a smile.

“Our film’s premiere day doubles as a Billboard champion’s celebration. That PR budget saved itself.”

The Showbox distribution head jumped in immediately:

“Director-nim, I’m noting that headline down.”

Everyone laughed.

Baek Si-on looked at the candles on the cake, still trailing a little white smoke.

Billboard.

1.

This picture was actually very small.

Smaller than the sea of people at Incheon. Smaller than the lights of Staples Center. Smaller than that cold, thrilling number one on the Billboard website.

But it was concrete.

Concrete enough to hold firelight.

Concrete enough to hold cream.

Concrete enough to hold a roomful of familiar faces.

And Choi Jin-ri, standing in front of him, holding the cake.

“Did you make a wish?”

Baek Si-on:

“……”

“Forgot.”

“Want me to relight them?”

“No need.”

“Why?”

“It already came true.”

Choi Jin-ri’s breath caught.

She did not know whether “it already came true” meant the Billboard number one.

Or something else.

She wanted to ask.

But she did not dare.

Because if she asked, and the answer was only the Billboard number one — then she would become the person who had “read too much into it.”

If she did not ask, then at least this sentence could stay with her for a long time.

Stay as a warm, blurred thing, open to any number of interpretations.

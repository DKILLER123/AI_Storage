First meeting of the leads — the beat the whole film turns on.

April The Han River bank — the film Sang-hun & Yeon-hui, first encounter

Day three of the shoot, two in the afternoon. The sloped street in Ansan. The leads’ first scene together.

The crew had cleared the area. Two strips of caution tape reading “film in progress” were strung at the mouth of the alley. A knot of onlookers, bags of groceries in hand, stood outside the tape gawking like it was free entertainment.

Si-on stood at the foot of the slope, looking up. The grade wasn’t steep — thirty-odd steps to the top, give or take. Both sides were old residential buildings, paint peeling, faded sheets hanging from the drying-poles.

Choi Jin-ri stood at the top, a backpack on her shoulders. The make-up artist had roughed her face with a layer of dark foundation, pressing the luminous skin down. Her hair was made messy, the bangs chopped and clinging to her forehead like it hadn’t been washed in days.

The uniform was a genuine borrowed one, the 2002 version, cuffs worn at the edges, the hem two centimetres shorter than regulation. Not for looks. Because Yeon-hui had outgrown it and her family couldn’t afford a new one, so she made do.

Baek Jeong-hun stood behind the monitor, gave the frame a look, nodded. Then he took off the headphones and called out to the two actors.

“Don’t shoot yet. Run the scene first, find the feeling.”

Si-on answered. Choi Jin-ri nodded.

The scene was simple. Sang-hun comes up the slope and spits at the halfway point, offhand. Yeon-hui comes down the slope, head down, and walks straight into the gob of spit — it hits her in the chest. She calls out to him. He turns, sees it, doesn’t apologise, and walks straight up to wipe it off with his sleeve. In Sang-hun’s logic, wiping it clean is enough — what use is an apology? Yeon-hui thinks he’s about to assault her, and slaps him on instinct. Sang-hun, slapped, swings back on instinct. One punch, and she’s knocked out cold. Beyond what he intended, but it’s too late. A tangled fate begins with that punch.

Si-on started up the slope. Not fast, shoulders hunching forward a little, back slightly curved, hands in his pockets. Choi Jin-ri started down from the top, backpack on, shoulders drawn inward, eyes on the ground, steps light — as if afraid to step on something she shouldn’t.

The two met halfway. Si-on’s throat worked and he made the gesture of spitting.

“Hey.”

Jin-ri turned, face sour as she looked at him. Si-on followed her gaze down, looking at where his “spit” had landed — Jin-ri’s chest. There should have been a wet patch there, but there wasn’t. He walked right up, raised his hand, and held it in front of her chest, waiting for the slap.

The slap didn’t come. Jin-ri’s right hand rose to shoulder height and stopped.

“…I’m sorry.”

She dropped the hand and bowed, apologising. Si-on frowned.

“What happened?”

“I… let me go again.”

The two went back to their places and ran it again. Crossing. The hand rise. Jin-ri bit her lip, right hand lifting, but it paused in mid-air half a second and sank, limp, back down. Si-on looked at those frustrated, apologetic eyes, and said nothing. He didn’t step in to play the life-coach. He stepped back a couple of paces and lifted his chin toward the monitor. That was the director’s job. He had to stay in his lane.

“Jin-ri-ssi, wait a moment.”

Baek Jeong-hun took the headphones off and hung them around his neck, then walked out from behind the monitor and, gesture and speech together, started to break down the logic underneath this scene for her. Si-on wandered to the wall at the far side of the alley. The hard part here, besides the slap, was his spit. Aim too high and you hit the actress in the face — a broadcast accident. Too low, it hits the ground and the camera can’t catch it. It had to land, precise, on the chest of the school uniform, an area smaller than a palm.

This took accuracy. So Si-on grabbed a bottle of water from the crate, held a mouthful, and fixed his eyes on a patch of peeling plaster on the wall. He filled his cheeks. Puh.

April The riverside set Baek Jeong-hun directs the scene

Over at the edge of the slope, Baek Jeong-hun talked for about five minutes. From Yeon-hui’s upbringing to her psychological defences, to why this slap had to happen: because Sang-hun wasn’t her father, wasn’t her brother. He was a stranger. A stranger’s assault triggers the last shred of her self-protective instinct.

Jin-ri listened carefully, until her eye caught something off to the side — Si-on, standing there like a pea-shooter. Her reaction wasn’t amusement — though the image was absurd. It was pressure. He was preparing in earnest, and she was holding up the shot.

Jin-ri bit her lip and forced her attention back to Baek Jeong-hun’s explanation.

“Ready?”

Jin-ri nodded.

“Ready.”

“Si-on, you?”

Si-on threw the water bottle into the crate and wiped his mouth.

“Anytime.”

“Good. Then let’s just go. All departments ready.”

The camera was set behind and to the side of Si-on. The script supervisor raised the slate. The boom operator extended the mic over the slope. Si-on went back to the bottom, Jin-ri back to the top.

“Action!”

Si-on started up the slope. Not fast, shoulders hunching forward a little, hands in his pockets. At the halfway point his throat worked. Cheeks puffed. Aim.

“Ahhh — ptoo.”

A spray of water shot out at an angle, landing precisely on the chest of Jin-ri’s uniform, a dark wet patch blooming at once.

“Ya!”

Jin-ri stopped, looked down at her chest, then lifted her head and glared at him. Si-on heard it, turned. His gaze rested two seconds on the patch on her chest — no apology, no embarrassment, just a natural movement as he walked over, raised his right hand, and used his sleeve to wipe at the wet spot.

Smack!

A sharp, clean crack of a slap burst through Baek Jeong-hun’s headphones. Not recalling the director’s theory, not considering the character’s defence mechanisms. The instant that hand pressed uncontested against her chest, the instinct of a woman being violated was bigger than her fear of violence.

Si-on’s head snapped to one side. He stopped. Slowly he turned back, eyes full of shock, then swiftly overrun by something brutal.

I wipe it for you, and you still hit me?

The next second, Si-on swung his arm with no warning, no drawing back. The wind of the punch grazed Jin-ri’s ear, lifting a few strands of hair. She could feel the suffocating pressure of that fist. The instant before it stopped, hard, three centimetres from her cheek, her mind blanked and she threw herself backward, hitting the ground hard.

“…”

“Cut!”

“Good. Prep the next shot.”

The make-up artist ran over with her kit and crouched beside Jin-ri, painting the swollen-red bruise effect on her face. Si-on stood in place, working the side of his cheek where he’d been hit. Jin-ri lay there, staring at the sky, chest heaving. He looked at her once, said nothing, and turned to walk toward the monitor.

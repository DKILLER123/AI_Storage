Chapter One Hundred Eighty Seven

The Sea King Runs an Aquarium

January 9–10 · Incheon arrivals to a Chicago highway Three suitcases and one red border · a ninety-degree bow held to the last taillight · a magazine smuggled into a handbag · the loudest phone in North America · and one contract refused in two words Incheon International Airport → a tea house street in Seoul → the road from O’Hare to the United Center A forum post lands its author in an airport bookstore, face to face with a red frame; a national little sister performs graciousness for two hours and commits one act of magazine smuggling; and a man surrounded by the entire luxury industry looks up from an interview just long enough to decline all of it — for reasons of market liquidity.

Incheon International Airport. The three fansite masters landed a little after ten in the morning.

The long-haul from New York had reduced their bones to upholstery. Warm Light led the way dragging her suitcase, her hair pressed into a strange new architecture by the airplane pillow. Beside her, one companion hugged her camera bag and waddled like a penguin that had not slept. The third cradled the signed poster against her chest with the solemnity of someone transporting a national treasure.

Nobody spoke first. The ending of the New York trip was, frankly, too ridiculous to narrate out loud. They had flown fourteen hours to a concert — and been summoned backstage, lectured for ten minutes, and deported on a ticket their own bias bought.

“I still don’t feel like it was real,” the youngest said quietly.

“Me neither.”

Warm Light glanced at her phone. Overnight, her post had climbed all the way to number one on the forum’s front page. The comments were still breeding. She stuffed the phone back in her pocket.

“Let’s just get out of here. All I want is a shower and my own bed.”

They dragged their suitcases toward the arrivals exit — and passing the airport bookstore, the youngest stopped dead.

“Wait.”

“What?”

She was pointing at a shelf inside, her expression sliding from sleepy to blank.

“…Si-on oppa?”

Warm Light followed the finger. In the international publications section — the most prominent real estate in the store, right between The Wall Street Journal and The Economist — sat the newest issue of TIME. Red border. White ground. And in the middle of it, a face they all knew.

Her first reaction was: absurd. Her second reaction: all three of them charging into the bookstore with their luggage.

The cover photo: Baek Si-on in a black coat, face turned in profile against the cold-white glow of some backstage corridor. He wasn’t smiling. He wasn’t striking a single please-look-at-me pose. And it was exactly that refusal to court the lens that made the face stick out, incongruous, among a row of international current-affairs magazines.

The three of them stood in front of the shelf, briefly without language. The youngest recovered first, hands shaking, phone up, translator app open on the cover title.

TIME · the current issue · the cover line, as rendered by a translation app in Incheon

“He Doesn’t Want to Become Anything — So He Became Everyone’s Competition.”

Filed between global capital and the world order. In the middle: one face, refusing to pose.

Warm Light read the line and felt her un-shaken jet lag take another direct hit.

This was not a fansite title. Fansites wrote: Legendary photo of Baek Si-on in New York. A human sculpture descends on North America. One glance, a guaranteed place in heaven. Tonight even the moon loses. Korean entertainment media wrote: Baek Si-on breaks another Asian record. The world conquest of Billboard’s double-crown king. The Republic’s cultural standing rises again.

But TIME had written: he doesn’t want to become anything, so he became everyone’s competition.

“Is this… a compliment?” one of them asked, uncertain.

“No idea.”

Warm Light studied the cover for a few seconds.

“But it feels expensive.”

“……”

The assessment was plain. It was also accurate. Because the position of the thing — left, global capital; right, the world order; center, his face — already said everything.

A strange vertigo came over the three of them. They had known prouder moments than this: the Vogue sellouts, the Billboard ones, the AMAs statuette, the imperial airport crowds at Incheon. But however outsized, those had all still fit inside the familiar scoreboard of K-pop stardom. This was different. It was as if someone had lifted him out of the fan-world’s scoring table entirely and dropped him into a much bigger one — with a note beside it: please re-understand him.

Warm Light picked up a copy and opened it. English, wall to wall. She could catch individual words; strung together, they stopped being language. She surrendered and opened her translator app.

The app stuttered, then produced its stiff, machine-made Korean — and the stiffness somehow made it land harder:

TIME · the opening paragraphs · via translation app, with visible effort

“When Baek Si-on reached No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100 for the second time, he did not appear at the Los Angeles celebration, and was not surrounded by record executives.”

“He was in Churchill, Manitoba, Canada — waiting for an aurora that had no obligation to appear.”

“For a man the Korean press calls a ‘national cultural asset,’ the choice looks almost willfully out of place. Which is precisely why it explains, better than any quote could, why Baek Si-on became Baek Si-on.”

The three of them looked at one another at the same time — then kept turning pages. The app kept coughing up the article in fragments:

TIME · further in · the paragraph that survived the translation

“He sings in English, yet has not become an annex of the English-speaking world.”

“He may be the first to cross over while refusing to explain himself into ‘a shape more acceptable to America.’”

“Curiously, as his results travel back across the ocean, his countrymen have fallen into a worship close to frenzy — and what they worship seems less his music than the sentence ‘a Korean can finally be looked up to by Americans.’”

“Behind that frenzy sits a long-compressed inferiority and longing, an entire region’s relationship with the Western mainstream. Baek Si-on has become the perfect vessel for filling it.”

“Is this his victory, or his predicament?”

“Wow…”

One of them breathed it out.

“I actually understood that sentence.”

“Which one?”

“‘Refused to explain himself into a shape more acceptable to America.’”

Quiet again, for a while. Because it was true. Korean media liked to say he brought glory to the nation. American media liked to call him a new phenomenon. Fans liked to say he was handsome, weird, and safe to stan. And he himself had never once cooperated with any of the narratives. He didn’t perform humility. Didn’t perform accessibility. Didn’t perform the polite Asian boy America preferred, and didn’t perform the national hero Korea ordered. He simply did every single thing to its extreme, and when people came at him with labels, he turned around and went off to do the next thing.

Suddenly, Warm Light felt the cover title make sense. He didn’t want to become anything. So everyone who tried to define him became, in the end, his competition.

Nearby, a cluster of young girls chattered past.

“I heard GD oppa’s on another magazine cover!”

“Which one?”

“Vogue Korea. The Chanel New Year issue!”

“Daebak — his fashion pull is unreal.”

“……”

The three fansite masters turned to look, on reflex. A few fans, clearly at the airport for pickup or drop-off, support banners still in hand, leafing through the New Year fashion issues. Their excitement was total and utterly normal. In the fan economy, a major-magazine cover really was a trophy worth flexing. The whole evaluation system ran on it — covers, brand titles, front rows at fashion week, airport god-photos, luxury haute couture. Fans built the league table out of these words, and used them to confirm exactly which tier their own idol occupied.

The three of them had lived in that system their whole careers. Nobody on earth understood it better. But looking at those girls now, and down at the red border in their own hands, a very delicate feeling came over them. Other fandoms were still fighting over who made a Korean-edition cover. Their person had been wedged between the Journal and the Economist, and was being used as the case study in an American argument about where Asians stood inside Western culture.

This was no longer a question of whose oppa dressed better. This was the K-pop knowledge structure being forcibly upgraded, in an airport bookstore, at ten in the morning.

Warm Light closed her copy, turned to the clerk.

“Excuse me.”

The clerk looked up.

“Checking out?”

Warm Light pointed at the shelf.

“This issue of TIME — how many copies do you have?”

The clerk checked.

“Twenty-odd on display. And a full box in the back.”

“We’ll take all of them.”

The clerk froze.

“…All of them?”

“Mm.”

Warm Light nodded.

“All.”

The other two snapped out of their daze instantly.

“We can split the carrying.”

“Bring the box out too, please.”

“Card.”

The clerk looked at the three young women — suitcases still rolling, long-haul flight still written across their faces — and experienced a brief professional crisis. She had worked this airport bookstore for years. This was her first customer to land from another continent and immediately acquire the entire print run of a news magazine.

Not far away, the other fandom’s girls had noticed the commotion.

“What are they doing buying all those English magazines?”

“No idea. Resellers?”

“I think they’re Baek Si-on fans.”

“Why would a fan buy TIME?”

“……”

Indeed. Why would a fan buy TIME? Hugging her stack of red borders toward the register, Warm Light suddenly found the whole scene so absurd that she had to laugh.

Fandom used to be scored in covers and brand titles. Somewhere between the Journal and the Economist, the scoring table got replaced — and three girls bought out a magazine’s print run to keep the receipt.

— an airport bookstore, Incheon, the morning after the deportation

❧ ❧ ❧

By the time the Producers cast’s first meeting wrapped, the sky was fully dark and the winter wind was coming down the street like a blade.

Lee Ji-eun stood on the tea house steps holding a ninety-degree bow of immaculate construction. Cha Tae-hyun sunbaenim’s car left first. Gong Hyo-jin sunbaenim’s followed it out of the alley. She held the bow until the last pair of taillights vanished around the corner, then slowly straightened up and folded herself into her own car.

The instant the door shut, the smile fell off her face like scaffolding.

“Ah — my face has set like concrete.”

She kneaded her own cheeks hard with both hands. In the driver’s seat, Jeong Han-teuk glanced over and passed her a bottle of warm water.

“You worked hard.”

She took it, drank. The warm water slid down, and only then did she feel herself come back to life out of the perfectly-operating face she’d been renting out all evening.

“I poured tea for sunbaes for two hours today.”

She slumped against the seat-back, voice ghosting.

“I don’t feel like the female second lead. I feel like a certified tea artist.”

Han-teuk started the car, offering comfort in passing:

“It’s Park Ji-eun’s writer’s room — most people can’t even buy their way in to pour tea. You did well. Didn’t Gong Hyo-jin sunbaenim compliment you on your manners on the way out?”

“Manners don’t trade for screen time on a set.”

She screwed the cap back on, eyes drifting idly forward — and stopped.

On the passenger seat lay a magazine. Red border. All-English cover. She narrowed her eyes.

“Han-teuk oppa.”

“Mm?”

“Since when do you follow international affairs?”

Han-teuk steered and watched her in the mirror.

“What?”

She pointed at the passenger seat.

“Do you actually know all the words on that thing?”

“I can’t read the words,”

Han-teuk said.

“But I know the face.”

“Face?”

She blinked — and comprehension arrived all at once. She leaned forward, snatched the magazine off the passenger seat, and pulled it back.

Inside the red border: Baek Si-on’s face. It didn’t look like a celebrity. It looked like some newly wanted man who had absolutely no intention of cooperating with his own arrest.

She stared for two seconds, then looked at the title. English. She caught a few of the words. Want. Become. Anything. Strung together, they began to hurt. She got her phone out and scanned the cover.

“He Doesn’t Want to Become Anything — So He Became Everyone’s Competition.”

Han-teuk glanced in the mirror.

“Pretty cool title, right?”

She didn’t answer. She opened the inside pages. Dense English filled the spread; her brow came down; she went at it paragraph by paragraph with the scanner. The translated Korean came out lumpy, stiff as if hauled straight out of a textbook — but she understood it. And precisely because she understood it, her chest felt tight.

A year ago, Baek Si-on had been the discharged idol who came to her carrying melodies, asking her to write words. Back then she was the sunbae. The chart queen. The Lee Ji-eun who could listen to a finished track and say, cold-faced, that the singing was middling. The distance between them had been clear: she stood up high; he had just crawled out of the mud with an unheard song in his fist.

And a year later? She was still Lee Ji-eun. Still the chart robber who went No. 1 on Melon the day she released anything. Still the name nobody in Korean music could route around. But Baek Si-on had been placed by TIME inside an essay about where Asians stand — where any Asian stands — inside Western mainstream culture.

It wasn’t that she had gotten weaker. It was that his battlefield had suddenly, quietly, become enormous. She wasn’t afraid of someone being stronger than her; she had clawed her way up through being underestimated her whole life. What she feared was that the results she was proud of would turn into something small inside his ever-larger world. If even the qualification to walk side by side began to blur — with what face, with what standing, would she keep competing with him?

Han-teuk waited, and finally couldn’t help himself:

“What does it say?”

She surfaced from somewhere far away, killed the phone screen.

“Nothing.”

“Nothing? You read it for five minutes.”

“English practice.”

Han-teuk: “……”

He wished to inform her that this excuse had roughly the credibility of I was just passing by. He did not dare.

She closed the magazine. The cover faced up again: that cool, unimpressed face. She glared at it for two seconds — and snorted.

“American taste. That’s all it is.”

“Huh?”

“The lighting is way too harsh.”

Arms folded, dripping disdain:

“They’ve shot him like a wanted poster. Zero aesthetic sense.”

The corner of Han-teuk’s mouth twitched.

“Ya — that’s TIME’s chief photographer.”

“Chief of what? Being chief lets you take ugly photos?”

“……”

Han-teuk conceded the field. Streetlights slid backward past the window, one by one. Lee Ji-eun went quiet, watching the city flow by, apparently finished with the magazine forever.

Approximately five minutes passed. Han-teuk kept his eyes on the road. From the shadow of the back seat came a small movement: Lee Ji-eun turned her head, looked once at the TIME lying on the empty seat where she had tossed it — the red border still conspicuous in the dark car. Two seconds. Then she reached out, very lightly brought it back, glanced at the front of the cabin, confirmed Han-teuk wasn’t looking — unzipped her bag, rolled the magazine up with care, and stuffed it in, zipping it shut, buried alive and officially never having existed.

— the back seat of a manager’s car, Seoul, deep winter

❧ ❧ ❧

Chicago. On the road from O’Hare to the United Center, the sky outside was a wide wash of gray-blue winter.

Baek Si-on leaned against the back seat with the newest ELLE Korea in his hands. The cover was Choi Jin-ri — beige coat, angled against a wall in some interior set, head turned just off camera. The cover line read: From idol to actress: Choi Jin-ri’s metamorphosis.

ELLE Korea · the February issue · Jin-ri’s interview, the pull-quote they set large

Q: What’s the label you most want to shed?

“I’m not trying to shed Sulli. She’s a real part of my life.”

“I’d just like it if, someday, when people think of me, the first thing isn’t a girl on hiatus — it’s someone who still gets to act.”

Set in the largest type on the page, between a full-body and a close-up. He read it twice.

Baek Si-on looked at the line for a long moment. Meanwhile, life on his side of the car was not exactly quiet — Baek Eun-ah’s phone had been a detonating device since the wheels left the airport.

“Yes, the Billboard — no, not a new album, the live numbers — the tour, yes… One moment, another call.”

“Luxury? Noted… Gucci, Milan, the show invite… Saint Laurent — when? …Just list everything.”

“Korean press? About the TIME piece — we have no official reading at this time.”

“Hello. Yes, Mr. Baek is on tour in North America. Paris fashion week?”

The assistant beside Baek Eun-ah typed into a tablet at double speed. Every call: captured, summarized, sorted, ranked by priority.

“I completely understand Dior HQ’s sincerity… Yes — Mr. Baek’s schedule is, as I said, extremely full… Of course. I’ll relay your exact words. We’ll be in touch.”

She hung up. Turned to the name list her assistant had compiled. Gucci. Armani. Dior. Saint Laurent. Prada. Tom Ford. Cartier. Jaeger-LeCoultre… and several high-jewelry houses whose names she had to check the pronunciation of twice.

Baek Eun-ah suddenly missed the days when the queue consisted of KB Bank, Hyundai Motor, and SK Telecom. At least those people didn’t put on airs while discussing money.

“Cousin.”

“Mm.”

“Do you know what these calls actually mean?”

Baek Si-on turned a page. A few pages along, a detail shot: Choi Jin-ri in a gray gown, standing before a half-mirror in a theater’s backstage. The mirror held her profile. Outside the mirror, her back. One photograph, two Choi Jin-ris. He looked at it for two full seconds before answering his cousin.

“They’re inviting me to shows.”

“On the surface.”

“And underneath?”

“Underneath, they’re done with just lending you clothes. They want to know whether you might ever wear one house and one house only.”

He finally looked up from the magazine.

“Who’s paying the most?”

“That’s the thing — nobody opens with a number.”

She turned around, serious now.

“First the show invitation. Front row, fittings, dinners, photographers, a sit-down with the designer. Then the press photographs you in their seats. Then the comment sections start guessing. Then a little ‘inside information’ leaks. And only at the very end does the thing called a global partnership discussion begin.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Very sustainable process.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

His calm about being hunted by the entire luxury industry was, frankly, unwell.

“Aren’t you excited?”

“About what?”

“These houses used to need half a year of ceremony before giving a Korean male artist a regional title. Now they’re calling one after another. Nobody’s saying it out loud, but it’s obvious. They want to sign you.”

Baek Si-on closed ELLE — the cover’s face up — set it on the empty seat beside him, and looked at it once more.

“Not signing.”

She blinked.

“Not one? Any of them?”

“Mm.”

“Reason?”

“Low pay. Too many demands.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

The man was a long way from the kid who once audited every ten-million-won penalty clause for survival. Under Armour: eight figures plus sales participation. BodyArmor: equity. Modern duty-free: dividends. The tour’s cash flow. Publishing. Against that ledger, a luxury house’s global-ambassador fee — a god-tier resource for any ordinary artist — barely cleared the bar of interesting. And signing meant a competitor-exclusivity lock: no wearing that one today, no carrying this one tomorrow, red-carpet looks filed in advance, airport paparazzi shots negotiated as deliverables, dinners and pop-ups and exhibitions and photo campaigns until death. It didn’t sound like an endorsement. It sounded like voluntarily signing up for a deluxe edition of an idol trainee contract.

Baek Eun-ah was silent for a while, then accepted the plain, effective logic.

“The fashion weeks, then?”

“Those I’ll attend. They’ve dressed me for free plenty of times. You owe faces.”

And then it landed on her. He wasn’t rejecting the fashion world. He simply refused to be bound to any one house. Because the day he signed was the day the free clothes, free seats, and free olive branches stopped coming.

“Cousin.”

“Mm?”

“I think you’re a sea king.”

“I have promised nothing to any brand,”

Baek Si-on frowned.

“Which is exactly what makes you one.”

“……”

He considered the matter.

“This is called maintaining market liquidity.”

She looked at him for a long moment, expression layered.

“You are never, ever allowed to use that sentence on anything else.”

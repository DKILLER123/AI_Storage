Chapter One Hundred Twenty

So It Turns Out I’m Poor

Late October Morning An archery range on the edge of Seoul A bullseye, a video from Jin-ri, and a film shoot he cannot call the director about.

The late-October wind had turned cool at the archery range on the edge of Seoul, and when it passed through the pines the branches made a very soft rustling. Baek Si-on was mid-practice when, on the bench behind the shooting line, his phone lit once.

KakaoTalk · Choi Jin-ri

Choi Jin-ri

They’re all imitating you~

[video]

The message arrived while he was practising. He did not reach for the phone — the string stayed nocked, and the range had his attention.

Baek Si-on stood at the shooting line. Three fingers hooked on the string. Left arm extended. The line of muscle across his back and shoulders drawn taut and smooth. His archery stance did not look like the flowing, romantic warrior of a sageuk. It looked more like a launch mechanism that had been precisely calibrated. Not beautiful. Accurate.

In truth, what was occupying his mind at that moment was not the target. It was the music video. Compared with the chorus of “greatest of all time,” “masterpiece,” “I actually cried” that the MV had drawn from North America — when Baek Si-on had sat through Bong Joon-ho’s final cut in full, what he felt was a cold sweat down his back.

He had been afraid Bong Joon-ho would get carried away. Would bury metaphors in the MV. The kind of social metaphor that Korean left-leaning filmmakers do best — invisible on the first viewing, and then, on the second, it hits you. And it turned out the reminder had only made it worse. Not reminded, Bong might never have done it. Reminded, he really went and did it.

That sequence where Baek Si-on sinks to the bottom of the pool. The shot language ran like this: holding his breath underwater, blue water all around, light refracting down from the surface, his body sinking. Eyes open. Lips pressed shut. Bubbles escaping his nose. Then he struggles. Kicks. Drives upward. Bursts through the surface.

North American viewers saw: the protagonist breaking free of the valley; it symbolises never giving up. Kobe fans saw: a tribute to Kobe’s return from injury. Athletes around the world saw: the starting point of a physical-limits challenge. But if you were Korean — if you still carried April with you, and had seen, half a month earlier, the controversy around the documentary The Diving Bell at the Busan International Film Festival — then what you saw was the 304 who never came out.

Baek Si-on did not know whether Bong Joon-ho had done it on purpose. He suspected he had. But he could not exactly phone up and ask, “Uncle Bong, is that underwater shot an allusion to the ferry?” Because Bong Joon-ho would very probably answer, “If you think it is, then it is.” And hang up.

Fortunately, LeBron James had saved him. In forty-eight hours, that hundred-and-thirteen-kilogram man launching himself out of a pool had wrenched the entire conversation around the underwater shot from “is this an allusion to the sinking?” to “the great global male-hormone challenge.” Nobody was analysing the metaphorical layer of that underwater shot anymore. Because the whole world was jumping into pools. Who had time for literary criticism?

Honestly.

Baek Si-on released the string. Whish—. The arrow flew. Thirty metres. Dead centre. The red bullseye pinned, the shaft trembling lightly behind it. The coach beside him immediately applauded.

“Wow — Mr. Baek, beautiful! That entry angle was perfect. Your anchor stability has gone up a whole level since yesterday!”

Baek Si-on ignored him. He drew a second arrow. Nocked it. Drew. Aimed.

His uncle was like that too. So was Bong Joon-ho. These left-leaning filmmakers, every last one of them, could not stop themselves from stuffing things into their work. They probably thought of it as the artist’s duty. But the people who paid the cost of “being over-interpreted” were never them. Whish. The arrow split the air. Dead centre again. The coach’s smile widened.

“Daebak! Mr. Baek, if the film ever needs to shoot a draw from the front, this state of yours is ready to use as it is!”

Baek Si-on lowered the bow and flexed his wrist. Forget it. What was done was done. The MV was out. LeBron had turned the underwater shot into an athlete challenge. The world’s attention had been successfully redirected. Chasing Bong Joon-ho about it now would achieve nothing except making himself miserable. Next time — if there was a next time.

“That’s all for today.”

He handed the bow to the coach.

“Yes, Mr. Baek. Same time tomorrow?”

“Mm.”

He changed out of his gear. Arm guard off. Finger tab off. Bodyguard Kang and bodyguard Han were waiting at the entrance to the range. This was the first week of their employment. They were quiet, and so far they looked more dependable than Park Ji-hun — at the very least, they did not stare at the wind and pronounce it “unprofessional.”

Baek Si-on got into the van. Driver Jo was behind the wheel today. The van pulled away from the range and headed for the next appointment, a court-etiquette lesson. On the road, Baek Si-on finally took out his phone. At the top of KakaoTalk sat Choi Jin-ri’s avatar.

Below the message she had sent from the range sat the video she had filmed in secret. Baek Si-on opened it.

The picture was shaky. Presumably Jin-ri had recorded it in secret on her phone. The setting was an indoor swimming pool. In the frame, Lee Kwang-soo — in his Running Man team kit — stood waist-deep in the water, took a deep breath, and crouched hard, trying to replicate the surface-breaking shot from Baek Si-on’s MV. He did not come close to launching himself up onto the pool’s edge. Instead he went over like a dehydrated beltfish, his feet going out from under him, and crashed into the water. The splash soaked Song Ji-hyo, who had been standing right beside him. Behind the lens, Choi Jin-ri was laughing helplessly.

“Hahahahahahahaha!”

The frame shook even harder. Lee Kwang-soo surfaced, aggrieved.

“Yah! This is impossible! How does Baek Si-on even get out of the water?”

Yoo Jae-suk cut in at once.

“Him? He’s a Venice Best Actor. You’re a giraffe from Seoul.”

The whole set collapsed. Watching the screen, the corner of Baek Si-on’s mouth moved, just barely. Today was Thursday. On the Running Man production cycle, the show usually recorded on Monday and Tuesday, edited Wednesday through Friday, and aired on Sunday. If today was Thursday, the footage ought to have been in the edit suite already. Which meant they had added this pool challenge on the spot — obviously because the global challenge wave LeBron had started was running too hot to ignore. Variety shows were like that. Wherever the heat was, that was where the camera pointed.

Running Man · pool challenge · the cut nobody planned

Yoo Jae-suk and Ji Suk-jin spent the rest of the shoot roasting each other over it.

Baek Si-on exited the video and went back to the chat window. He typed. Sent.

A sticker followed: a small figure lying face-down on the floor.

Baek Si-on looked at the sticker, and for a moment pictured Choi Jin-ri heaving herself upward with everything she had, only to clear the surface by about ten centimetres. He typed.

KakaoTalk · Choi Jin-ri

Baek Si-on

Normal.

Late October Same day A Running Man recording set She pokes the screen three times and still cannot poke through KakaoTalk.

At the Running Man recording site, Choi Jin-ri sat on a small stool in the rest area, glaring at the words “Stating it.” She jabbed the screen with her fingernail. Once. Twice. Three times. As if, that way, she could jab through KakaoTalk and into Baek Si-on himself.

“Honestly—”

She muttered under her breath.

“This man is going to kill me one day.”

Nearby, staff were passing towels to Lee Kwang-soo. In the distance, Yoo Jae-suk and Ji Suk-jin were still roasting each other over the completely failed “Legend Challenge.” Jin-ri looked down at the chat window. She had wanted to send something fierce back. But her fingers hovered above the keyboard for several seconds, and slowly lowered.

Arguing with that kind of person was the same as admitting that, indeed, she could not make the jump. Which she could not. But you could not admit that. She decided to change the subject.

KakaoTalk · Baek Si-on

Choi Jin-ri

I watched the MV. It was beautiful.

This time, Baek Si-on answered fast.

KakaoTalk · Choi Jin-ri

Baek Si-on

What was the final subtitle?

Choi Jin-ri blinked. Subtitle? What subtitle? When she had watched the MV, every ounce of her attention had gone to Baek Si-on’s face, his voice, the way his hair stuck to his forehead when he came out of the water, and the abdominal muscles. The line of text at the end — she had never registered it. She flipped out of KakaoTalk at once. Opened YouTube. Found the video in her likes. Legend, official MV. Play. Dragged the progress bar straight to the end. Black screen, and then a line of thin white type rose into view.

For those who refuse to quit.

To the people who refuse to give up. Choi Jin-ri stared at that line for two full seconds. Then she switched back to KakaoTalk and typed.

KakaoTalk · Baek Si-on

Choi Jin-ri

For those who refuse to quit.

Baek Si-on

Mm.

Jin-ri stared at that single “mm.” Mm. One word. What did mm mean? Was he checking whether she had really watched it? Confirming she had answered correctly? Or — was it aimed at her? For those who refuse to give up. Give up. Give up what? An idol career drowning in hate comments? A life that kept going badly? Or… him?

Her heart skipped a beat. She bit her lip. Her fingers hung above the screen, hesitated for a moment, and then, on a sudden reckless impulse, she typed one sentence.

KakaoTalk · Baek Si-on

Choi Jin-ri

I won’t give up.

Even she herself had not worked out exactly what “won’t give up” meant — career, or life, or something more specific than the words she had typed, something she was not yet ready to admit to. Jin-ri waited about half a minute. No new message appeared on the screen. She set the phone face-down on her knee. Flipped it over again. Still nothing.

Forget it. He was probably busy again. That man was always busy.

✿ ✿ ✿

It was not that Baek Si-on did not want to reply. It was that the phone rang.

Baek Si-on ← Scooter Braun · Los Angeles · incoming

“Hey, my dear Baek.”

“Don’t say it like that.”

“Hahahahahaha—”

Scooter’s laugh was full of delight.

“Fine, fine. Here’s the situation. On the twenty-sixth, you fly to Dubai and sing two songs at the private party of His Highness Sheikh Mohammed Al Maktoum.”

Baek Si-on’s eyebrow moved.

“Say that again. Sing for whom?”

“Sheikh Mohammed Al Maktoum.”

“Who is he?”

There was a second of silence on the other end. Then, in the tone of “you cannot actually be serious,” Scooter said:

“Crown Prince of Dubai. Vice President and Prime Minister of the United Arab Emirates. Head of the Al Maktoum family. One of the wealthiest ruling houses on the planet — his personal horse-racing holdings alone are valued in the tens of billions every year. At his private parties the guest list runs to Middle Eastern royals, European nobility, Hollywood A-listers and Fortune 500 chief executives.”

“…”

Scooter went on.

“He watched your S4 World Championship live, and he watched the Legend MV. He was very taken. He is hosting a party at his private estate this weekend… and all he wants is to hear you perform Legend and Bones live.”

“Scooter. You know my focus right now is preparing to go into filming—”

“One and a half million dollars.”

Baek Si-on’s words stopped dead. When he spoke again, his voice was still calm. But the subject had changed without leaving a single seam.

“…Just the two songs?”

“Yes.”

Baek Si-on looked out the window. The van was passing a stretch of tree shadow; the late-October light fell through the gaps in the leaves and broke on the glass into strips of moving brightness. One and a half million dollars. Two songs. Ten minutes. A figure too absurd to be a performance fee. It was more like a statement in the Middle-Eastern dialect of “I am rich, so the world turns at my taste.”

He remembered the early commercial-gig days. Show after show. Singing and running. Changing clothes in the car. Eating cold rice backstage. And now he sat in a van on the way to a court-etiquette lesson while an American agent told him Dubai’s ruling house had priced two songs at one and a half million. The world really did move fast.

“When’s the flight?”

“They’ll send a private jet to pick you up in Seoul. Out on the morning of the twenty-sixth, about seven hours in the air. Land, rest, soundcheck, perform that night. Then straight on to Los Angeles. Arrive the twenty-seventh. Lakers home opener the twenty-eighth.”

“Mm.”

“Is that mm a yes, or are you chewing over some strange Korean morality question again?”

“It’s a yes.”

“Good.”

Scooter audibly exhaled.

“Baek, trust me, don’t overthink this money. There are people in this world who spend a million and a half dollars just to make their weekend a little more pleasant. If you don’t take it, someone else will.”

“I know.”

“Then get some rest.”

Something occurred to Scooter, and he added:

“When we meet on the twenty-seventh I’ll brief you properly on the commercial offers around Legend. The NBA, the sports brands, the game licensing, the film-trailer people, the endorsements… a lot of things are lining up.”

“Mm.”

“And I’ll have finance wire the Dubai advance over shortly.”

“All right.”

“See you on the twenty-seventh.”

“Bye.”

The call ended.

Baek Si-on set the phone back on his knee and the van drove on. About twenty minutes later, the phone buzzed — a bank credit alert.

Kookmin Bank · credit alert

international wire · USD · S.W Studio corporate account

Credit: USD 600,000

Fifty percent of the Dubai fee, paid in advance — before the first note of the first song.

He glanced at it, then opened the banking app out of habit and checked his total balance. Roughly ₩1.5 billion. That figure included the settlement of Way Back Home’s streaming royalties for August that had landed a few days earlier — over ₩200 million on its own. He frowned. It sounded like a great deal of money. But measured against the density of his schedule over these months, it felt… small.

He remembered earning plenty. The signing fees from KB Bank, Hyundai Motor and SK Telecom together had come to ₩2.5 billion. The fee for Sado had been ₩600 million. Add a month of commercial gigs, the S4 World Championship show… money had kept flowing in on the income side. So why was the balance only ₩1.5 billion? Oh. Right. Bang the fatty. Big Hit Entertainment. The ₩3 billion investment. Add everyday expenses, recording-studio costs, wardrobe and styling, overseas travel, legal fees, MV production, the salaries for Eun-ah and Park Ji-hun, the newly hired driver and bodyguards. ₩1.5 billion. No, it really was not much.

Thinking of Bang Si-hyuk, the seven boys surfaced in his mind of their own accord — the ones who had bent into ninety-degree bows, sweating, in that waiting room. He wondered how much exposure today’s global broadcast had bought them. It should have. In a broadcast watched by tens of millions, their faces had appeared. Even a few seconds counted. Being seen was the beginning. He hoped they would blow up soon. The sooner they blew up, the sooner Big Hit became valuable. And the sooner Big Hit became valuable, the less time he would have to spend sighing at his own account balance.

The afternoon’s court-etiquette lesson was as grinding as ever. Kneeling. Sitting upright. Walking. The chafed inner thighs from the riding practice still ached faintly, and yet Baek Si-on held a completely expressionless face through all of it.

At nine in the evening, in Yeonnam-dong, Baek Si-on opened the front door, changed into slippers and walked into the living room. Baek Eun-ah was sitting cross-legged on the rug in front of the coffee table, laptop open before her, the cap of a ballpoint pen clamped between her teeth, her brow furrowed tightly enough to kill flies. Hearing the door, she looked up at once and waved him over like a rescue beacon.

“Oppa! Come here!”

Baek Si-on crossed the room and sat on the sofa.

“What?”

Eun-ah swung the laptop around to face him and pointed at the dense spreadsheet of figures on the screen.

“Eun-ju unnie and I finished calculating your income today, and I found out one thing.”

“What?”

“If you don’t set up the personal studio, and you file everything under the top bracket of individual income tax… for these three months alone, you would owe ₩1.6 billion in tax.”

Baek Si-on: “…”.

He quietly recalled the account balance he had checked at noon. ₩1.5 billion. In other words, every won he had saved would not be enough to settle that bill with the National Tax Service. Three months of running himself into the ground, and at the end of it he would owe the tax office ₩100 million. That was more exciting than a robbery.

Every won he had saved was not enough to settle the individual bill. The studio is the difference between owing the tax office a fortune and owing it nothing.

Looking at Baek Si-on’s rarely-seen frozen expression, Eun-ah finally took the dented ballpoint out of her mouth and shifted tone.

“The good news, though, is that today we sent the change-of-payee notices to every brand partner.”

She opened another file.

“Under the Corporate Income Tax Act, profit under ₩20 billion is taxed at only twenty percent. So as long as the money runs through the studio’s corporate account, we legally save close to ₩800 million in tax.”

₩800 million. That was no small number. Baek Si-on’s expression eased a little. Eun-ah kept scrolling, pointing to an estimate row at the bottom of the sheet.

“Also, Legend’s overseas copyright fees and download royalties will start landing sometime in the first quarter of next year. If that overseas income went straight into a personal account, it would be taxed twice. But now that the studio exists as the entity, we can keep that money cleanly on the books through offshore structuring.”

Baek Si-on listened to the whole explanation and nodded. He looked at the spreadsheets in front of her, then at the ballpoint pen whose body she had nearly bitten through. A few months ago, she had sat in the back of a taxi, head low, apologising because the LOEN split could not be improved. Now she could walk him, item by item, through individual income tax, corporate entities, overseas copyright revenue, offshore structuring and payee-path changes for the brands. She really had grown up.

Baek Si-on lifted a hand, meaning to ruffle her hair. Halfway there he remembered Los Angeles and that handful of oil, and lowered it again, naturally.

“Well done.”

At that, Eun-ah’s eyes lit up.

“Obviously~”

The tail of her voice rose. She tried to look as though it were nothing, but the corners of her mouth would not cooperate.

Baek Si-on stood up from the sofa, headed for the shower. He had taken two steps.

“Oppa.”

Eun-ah called him back. He turned.

“Mm?”

“Director Hwang’s edit is finished. The first cut of the Love Yourself MV. When are you going to watch it?”

Baek Si-on checked the time. 9:17 in the evening. Not too late.

“After my shower. Put it on the television.”

“Okay.”

He headed for the bathroom. Midway, he stopped again.

“Eun-ah.”

“Mm?”

“Don’t force yourself to grind through tax matters on your own.”

Eun-ah blinked. Baek Si-on said:

“Let Seo Eun-ju and the accountants do it. Your job is to understand the conclusions and judge the risk. Don’t turn yourself into the tax office.”

Eun-ah blinked again.

“Oh.”

Baek Si-on added:

“And stop biting the pen. It’s toxic.”

Eun-ah looked down at the ballpoint she had nearly destroyed.

“…Got it.”

The bathroom door closed. Soon the sound of running water began. Eun-ah moved the ballpoint to the far side of the table, picked up her phone, and air-played the video file to the big television in the living room.

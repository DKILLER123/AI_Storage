Chapter Two Hundred Forty

JYP’s Dignity and Ji-eun’s Bloodless Conquest

Mid-July

One crown changes heads · twice

The Billboard Hot 100 · then Pyeongchang

Mid-July. North American pop finally produced a name capable of moving ‘See You Again’ off the summit.

OMI — a Jamaican singer whose global mainstream recognition had, until now, been limited.

‘Cheerleader’.

For most artists, keeping one song at the top of the Billboard chart for a single month would be career-glorifying enough to make the memoirs. ‘See You Again’ had sat up there for nearly three months — film heat, radio play, streaming, memorial services, graduations, countless private edits and farewell videos. The song had long since spread beyond a ‘Furious 7’ soundtrack cut and become a farewell anthem in the most general sense. So when ‘Cheerleader’ completed the changing of the guard, the Western press had ample cause to praise OMI, and the title “strongest rookie of 2015” appeared almost immediately.

Praising a rookie is standard media practice. But the label that signed OMI, in its hurry to cash the hype, chose the laziest possible shortcut for the publicity campaign: forced bundling — climb by stepping on someone else. OMI’s greatest weakness at present was that the public knew ‘Cheerleader’ without truly knowing the man who sang it. If recognition couldn’t be accumulated slowly, find a reference point the whole world already knew. Print “OMI defeats Baek Si-on” ten times in a row, and readers would gradually file the two names onto the same comparison table. As for the gap between a twelve-week champion and a one-week champion, between three chart-topping singles and one resurrected old song — the headline wasn’t responsible for explaining that. The headline was only responsible for the click.

And so the fronts of every entertainment site and music forum filled with near-identical wire copy:

❧ ❧ ❧

Baek Si-on responded to none of the hit pieces; he was busy running ‘Sado’ promotion. A chart battle was, to him, one row of numbers in a back-end data sheet, and he had no time to fight a war of words over one row. But the silence only made OMI’s team bolder — until Justin Bieber’s ‘What Do You Mean?’ was released worldwide.

A light tropical-house groove unfolding out of a tick-tocking clock timbre. Relaxed bass. Clean electronics. Bieber’s much-restrained singing no longer chased the overly sweet fireworks of his teenage register, pressing against the melody in a looser, easier way. For the first time, the public could see it plainly: the fringe-haired kid who once sang ‘Baby’ had grown up. ‘What Do You Mean?’ swept North American radio and streaming on its first day, and the MV’s play count climbed on an exponential curve.

North American gossip accounts began exhuming the previous two years of Bieber’s record at scale — the Miami DUI drag race, the eggs thrown at a neighbor’s house, the tour lateness and the walked-off shows — and the wire copy tried to build one narrative line:

The pieces did make an impact. They carried one fatal flaw: they were too old. Most listeners had long known what Bieber had done, and people willing to enjoy a song do not stop playing it upon reading about the egg incident for the seventh time. What actually decided the result was still the song — the tick-tock timbre, the light-footed rhythm, one chorus that refuses to leave after a single hearing. In an era where streaming holds more and more of the chart’s weight, a listener can curse Bieber and replay the track in the same breath. Moral condemnation failed to defeat the ear.

Chart-update day arrived.

The Hot 100 · the update

No. 1 — Justin Bieber, ‘What Do You Mean?’

No. 2 — OMI, ‘Cheerleader’

The crown changed heads. OMI’s marketing narrative collapsed entirely that week.

❧ ❧ ❧

To the North American industry, this No. 1 was an ordinary changing of the guard. To Bieber himself, the meaning reached the sky. From the moment ‘Baby’ made him globally famous, Justin Bieber had never once held a Billboard Hot 100 chart-topper — his peak result forever stuck at No. 2 (‘Boyfriend’). ‘What Do You Mean?’ rewrote that record: the first chart-topping single of his life.

Los Angeles. When the number came in, Bieber lost every ounce of composure he owned. He had felt territorial dread when Baek Si-on’s ‘Legend’ went planetary; he had soured into jealousy after casually handing ‘Love Yourself’ over and watching the other man turn it into a mega-hit. In this moment, all of it washed out clean. He finally understood what Scooter’s line — Baek Si-on grows the whole pie — had meant. Back then, Bieber had only seen the media handing fresh attention to Baek Si-on, only seen the company splitting resources with an Asian newcomer who had barged into the North American market. Now Baek Si-on had used one song to wash two years of mud off him and bodily lift him onto the throne he had chased his entire career.

In a state of pure mania, Bieber published a long post to Twitter and Instagram simultaneously.

The post, as posted

Thank you to my fans! Thank you, Scooter! But today, the person I want to thank most is my brother over in Korea — Baek Si-on!

You gave me this incredible song! You got me the first No. 1 of my life!

You are a true genius!

Brother, I love you man!!!

Attached: a photo of the two of them from the Met Gala red carpet, and Baek Si-on’s account tag. The essay of brotherhood and unrestrained worship collected millions of likes within the hour; Bieber fans, Baek Si-on fans, and passersby packed the same comment section — and everyone, on both continents, waited for one reply.

❧ ❧ ❧

Two hours later, somewhere on the ‘Sado’ roadshow circuit, Baek Si-on finally came online. He reposted the essay with a single line of his own.

The reply, as posted

My orientation is perfectly straight. Either way — congratulations.

The internet laughed itself sick.

The same weeks · full sun

One outdoor stage bleaching white · one producer staring at a phone

Pyeongchang · the Yeongdong Expressway Music Festival rehearsal

Pyeongchang. At the Yeongdong Expressway Music Festival rehearsal ground, the sun had bleached the open-air stage white. The steel-framed main stage lay across the middle of the lot, staff streaming back and forth along the lifts and the extension runway; the low-frequency shudder of the sound test rolled outward through the ground until even the water bottles in the rest tent trembled faintly.

The dance team had just finished one pass of the blocking when Yoo Jae-suk sat down straight onto the floor — one hand propped behind him, the other tugging his collar for air, chest heaving hard.

“You’re sure this is only a music festival?”

Park Jin-young stood beside him, toweling the sweat off his forehead. He was breathing hard too — just nowhere near as wrecked.

“On stage, you must never let them see you working. What the audience has to feel is enjoyment.”

Yoo Jae-suk looked up.

“I’m enjoying myself right now.”

“Enjoying what?”

“Enjoying sitting.”

Park Jin-young had no answer for that. When the choreography staff ran over to adjust the next formation, Yoo Jae-suk seized his window and lay down flat on the ground, demonstrating with his whole body the concept of refusing labor.

Park Jin-young did not join him. He unscrewed a water bottle, drank twice, and picked up the phone waiting beside the speaker. The North American music news was still occupied by a single story: ‘What Do You Mean?’ at the top of the Hot 100 — Justin Bieber’s first career crown. Under the news sat Bieber’s manic thank-you essay, and Baek Si-on’s heartbreakingly cold one-line reply, and a comment section that had already remixed the brotherhood into dozens of versions. Park Jin-young read the whole report top to bottom, and his chest slowly let the breath go.

Yoo Jae-suk, lying nearby, had sharp ears.

“What’s wrong?”

Park Jin-young shook his head.

“Nothing.”

“Your face says otherwise.”

Yoo Jae-suk propped himself up on his elbows.

“Trouble with the new girl group?”

“No.”

“Then it’s seeing somebody else write a good song.”

Park Jin-young turned his face toward him.

“Since when do you understand producers this well, hyung?”

“Since working with you.”

Yoo Jae-suk lay back down.

“You songwriter types — the moment somebody else succeeds, your face is a parent whose kid dropped one point on an exam. Ji-eun, for instance.”

Park Jin-young didn’t deny it. The champion story on that phone was, admittedly, enough to sour any Korean producer’s mood.

The America ledger, as he carried it

He was the most America-obsessed man in Korean entertainment: to crack that market he had stationed Wonder Girls in the U.S. long-term — radio, events, handing out flyers on the street with his own hands.

The tuition bought JYP its title as the first Korean agency to truly step into the North American mainstream — and cost Wonder Girls their throne at home while Girls’ Generation slipped in through the open door.

That tuition is still cited as the cautionary tale. And now Baek Si-on doesn’t even need to stand on an American stage — one song written for somebody else carries the Billboard crown home.

He laid the phone face-down on his knee. The sigh was not jealousy. A matter shelved for months had just become considerably thornier.

❧ ❧ ❧

Back in March, Bae Suzy had called him privately. When the nation’s first love deploys aegyo, the resistance of the entire JYP building drops. Suzy said she deeply admired Baek Si-on’s talent and hoped to collaborate on a song. Park Jin-young had thought the idea genuinely good: the nation’s first love plus an international star, a cross-company joint between JYP and Baek Si-on’s personal label — topic threshold guaranteed, streaming results very likely fine. So he wrote the draft himself: a light R&B love song on a jazz foundation, working title ‘Dream’.

The ‘Dream’ timeline, as shelved

March — Bae Suzy’s private call; the admiration; the request. Park Jin-young writes the draft himself.

Spring — the arrangement unfinished, the company launches the girl-group survival project; trainees, stage evaluations, and a debut lineup swallow the calendar. ‘Dream’ and its invitation get shelved — the plan was to wait until ‘SIXTEEN’ ran its course.

Summer — and summer’s Baek Si-on is not March’s. ‘See You Again’ rules the Hot 100 for twelve weeks. ‘Jumpshot’ storms the top five. ‘What Do You Mean?’ takes the crown for Bieber. Sending the ‘Dream’ demo now admits exactly one reading by the public: the song wasn’t even recorded yet, and the bandwagon label was already on its head.

Park Jin-young could accept criticism. What he could not accept was his own work being narrated as opportunistic bandwagon-riding. Not sending wasted the song. Sending wounded his pride. A producer of the very first rank, stuck on an email he had not sent.

He sighed again — and a clear voice arrived beside him.

“PD-nim, is something troubling you?”

Lee Ji-eun stood by the folding chair, cap brim low over her forehead, loose rehearsal clothes — and on the brim, embroidered, the Under Armour mark. More precisely: the Baek Si-on × Under Armour collaborative personal-brand model. Park Jin-young paid the cap’s branding no mind at all. But the moment he saw Lee Ji-eun, the door that had been jammed in his head for months abruptly acquired a possibly useful key. The whole industry knew Lee Ji-eun and Baek Si-on worked closely — MVs together, each other’s songs covered, rumors simmering on and off. More importantly, Lee Ji-eun was herself a creator.

“Ji-eun-ah, sit.”

He patted the spot beside him. She sat. Yoo Jae-suk, hearing two musicians about to discuss craft, considerately moved himself a distance away — his music-participation quota for the day already spent on dancing.

Park Jin-young told the whole ‘Dream’ saga: Bae Suzy wanted the collaboration; he had written the draft himself; company business had stalled the plan; and now that Baek Si-on’s international numbers had exploded again, sending the invitation now might well be read by the media as bandwagon-riding. The account was detailed, sincere, withholding nothing — one step short of playing the ‘Dream’ demo right there on the spot.

Lee Ji-eun held a perfect listening posture throughout. Smile at the corners. Nod frequency calibrated. The standard empathic interjections issued at proper intervals: “I see...” “Is that so...” “You’ve thought of everything, PD-nim.”

Inside, the air-raid siren had sounded at maximum level.

Suzy?

A duet?

So that cup of Milan tea had been steeping all this time — and it was still warm?

Lee Ji-eun sneered inwardly. They were all women carrying a “nation’s” title — whatever that nine-tailed fox was plotting, each of them understood the others perfectly. All this admiration of talent, all this musical exchange — stripped of politeness, it meant using a duet as the pretext to lock two people inside one sealed room. “Sunbae, this breath could take a different phrase.” — “Right, come closer and listen for me.” — back and forth a few times, and the scheduled chemistry would occur precisely on time.

She knew the playbook far too well. She had invented it.

Absolutely not. Choi Jin-ri, that oblivious marshmallow, wasn’t settled yet, and Bae Joo-hyun was closing distance through a cosmetics brand — introduce Bae Suzy into a studio now, and the contest upgraded from a three-way standoff into the industry’s summer hiring season. One position. An ever-lengthening applicant list. Having vetoed the plan internally, her face grew only more sincerely understanding.

“Your concerns are completely right.”

Park Jin-young stopped mid-lament.

“Really?”

Lee Ji-eun continued:

“You’re the founder of JYP. One of the godfathers of Korean popular music.”

Her tone had the gravity of someone reading aloud from the industry’s constitution.

“‘Dream’ is your own flesh and blood. If you send it over now, what will his American-side management think of it?”

“It would be an insult to your talent!”

Park Jin-young nodded along, deeply comforted.

“Right? That’s exactly what I kept thinking.”

Step one — the pride scaffold — complete. Lee Ji-eun switched to the professional register.

“And honestly, I’m not sure ‘Dream’ fits where Baek Si-on’s music is right now.”

“He’s been doing basketball songs, a world-championship theme, tropical house — his film is a crime story.”

“His whole public image at the moment runs sharp and international.”

“PD-nim’s ‘Dream’ is sweet, retro, a jazz love song.”

“Handing this song to the current Baek Si-on...”

She searched out one perfectly shaped comparison.

“It’s like putting the Terminator in a pink sundress.”

“A forced collaboration would wreck the texture this song already has.”

Park Jin-young stroked his chin. It really was a problem. Bae Suzy owned formidable recognition inside Korea, but Baek Si-on’s global volume now towered; pair them, and the promotion would tilt lopsided fast. ‘Dream’ was written for two people on equal footing, ambiguity in balance — forced together, it could easily become an international star descending to patronize a Korean singer. The relationship would be a rung shorter before a single note was sung.

Seeing him weigh it in earnest, Lee Ji-eun finally delivered the alternative.

“If PD-nim tailored this for Suzy, then the male vocal should serve her.”

“Any suggestions?”

“SM’s EXO — Byun Baek-hyun.”

Park Jin-young blinked once. Lee Ji-eun kept dismantling:

“JYP’s nation’s first love, paired with SM’s main vocal — that’s an equal marriage between two of the Big Three!”

“Say THAT out loud — the sheer prestige of it!”

A light went off in Park Jin-young’s head. Of course. Byun Baek-hyun’s timbre genuinely suited light jazz better. And JYP extending the olive branch to SM read as strength joining strength — an industry tale, not one whisper of bandwagon suspicion. Face: saved. Song: properly voiced.

“Byun Baek-hyun...”

His hand was already reaching for the phone, the SM planning office next — halfway there, it stopped.

“But I did promise Suzy I’d try reaching out to Baek Si-on...”

Lee Ji-eun’s answer arrived so naturally it might have been rehearsed for a month.

“Trying contact isn’t the same as guaranteeing a collaboration.”

“If the change of partner comes from the work itself, and the result is good enough — she’ll understand.”

Park Jin-young nodded. The logic had closed into a loop. And it came with a bonus: a topic-worthy collaboration between two major agencies. He rose and walked toward the side of the stage, calling his company team to prepare the complete ‘Dream’ sample and the collaboration proposal. Before the call connected, he turned back:

“Ji-eun — thank you.”

She smiled, meek as a model pupil:

“Just glad I could help, PD-nim.”

He walked off with the phone at his ear. Lee Ji-eun leaned back into the folding chair and stretched, long and full — the cap brim tipping up slightly, revealing a face fully unwound, smiling.

Far away in Seoul, Bae Suzy would never imagine it: half a year of planning — admire the sunbae’s talent, leverage a duet into a connection — rewritten in the space of one song, on a hot Pyeongchang afternoon, by the head underneath an Under Armour collab cap.

Chapter Two Hundred Twenty-Eight

The Six-Billion-Won Lunch

On the day of its ceremonial kickoff, ‘The Three Evils’ occupied the entertainment pages of every major portal.

The heat of that coverage attracted far more than Baek Si-on’s fans, Korean film audiences, and media looking to debate the political material.

It also attracted a species with far sharper instincts for heat.

The brand sponsors.

A “table of immortals” — the Venice Best Actor, a grand-slam Best Actress, and Chungmuro’s lions of character acting — backed by CJ E&M’s enormous investment: the industry’s consensus was that this was 2016’s most anticipated Korean film.

No runner-up.

So on the day after kickoff, the outskirts of the set were already ringed with the cars of brand representatives.

Business people in pressed suits carried proposals and product catalogs, queuing to get a meeting with the producing department.

Ordinary audiences cared who killed whom.

Brand departments cared what phone the killer used before the murder, and which shoes he ran in.

Crime films have commercial value too.

Given a bright enough lens, a sponsored product can sit right beside the corpse.

Though best not to make it food.

Bad for the appetite.

❧ ❧ ❧

The day after kickoff

Crew ready — slate undropped, two men mid-argument

Namyangju · the hospital exterior set

The most senior of the visitors was Hyundai Motor’s vice chairman, Chung Eui-sun.

Nobody actually read the vice chairman’s trip as a simple vehicle-placement inspection.

Confirming the film’s car fleet required one checklist from the marketing department.

Chung Eui-sun had come in person; he plainly wanted something else.

Entering the production’s periphery with an escort, he found the atmosphere eerie to the point of alarm.

The hospital exterior was fully dressed.

Two cameras held the primary positions, a dolly track ran down the steps toward the parking area, the lighting crew had the reflectors set, and the extras were in costume.

PAs stood along the walls, the cinematographer waited at the camera, the producer stood with arms folded, and Jo Woo-jin sat on the edge of the steps in costume.

An ambulance idled at the side entrance, its doors breathing open and shut.

Everything was ready.

The slate would not fall.

Because the director and the lead were arguing.

Chung Eui-sun asked a producing staffer beside him.

“What’s going on?”

The staffer turned, recognized the face, and his spine snapped straight.

“Chairman-nim, hello!”

The bow came first.

Then, voice lowered:

“The director and Actor Baek have... a difference of opinion about how to play today’s scene.”

The staffer recounted, briefly, what they had been about to shoot.

❧ ❧ ❧

Today’s scene was the male lead’s first after learning the diagnosis.

The character Baek Si-on played had just walked out of the hospital.

The doctor had told him: late-stage cancer, not much time left.

At this point the lead had not yet set out on the road of ridding the three evils — he was only a fugitive suddenly handed a date.

A man who had never prized his own life much — and who still went briefly blank when death was laid on a counter before him in the form of an examination report.

The planned shot went like this:

The lead walks out of the hospital’s main doors.

The city keeps running around him — an ambulance passes, a family hurries a patient inside, the convenience store does business, the signals cycle on their fixed rhythm.

No one knows this man has just been read his countdown.

He stops at the hospital doors, takes a cigarette from his pocket.

Lights it.

Draws.

The smoke veils part of his face.

He watches the street, trying to understand the fact of his own dying.

The chase and dialogue that followed the scene were uncontested — Baek Si-on and Jo Woo-jin had already walked the route.

The only thing the uncle and nephew were fighting over was that opening cigarette.

❧ ❧ ❧

Baek Jeong-hoon was himself a smoker.

Writing the script, he had designed the cigarette, naturally, as the lead’s first action after the diagnosis.

In his creative logic the action did two jobs.

The cigarette, as defended

One: the pause. Fresh off a death sentence, the character cannot explain himself in dialogue — the cigarette gives the performance a natural rest.

The audience may read it as fear, numbness, self-mockery, defiance, acceptance of death, or just an old habit firing on instinct.

Two: the metaphor. A burning cigarette — a life about to go out.

Therefore Baek Jeong-hoon held his line: “This cigarette gets lit.”

And Baek Si-on’s objection was not merely “smoking is bad for your health.”

He now carried an idol’s burden.

He knew his on-screen behavior would be dismantled and reassembled by fandoms on loop, and that its influence would escape the context of the story and radiate outward.

Younger fans would bind his smoking to “maturity,” “pain,” “manliness,” “anti-hero aura” — and then imitate it, in a race.

Baek Si-on had no intention of shooting a free image advertisement for the tobacco industry.

“Smoking is already this industry’s most common emotional all-purpose glue.”

“A character doesn’t know what to do — light a cigarette. A director doesn’t know how to shoot silence — light a cigarette. A writer can’t stretch the pause between two lines — light a cigarette, again...”

Baek Jeong-hoon’s voice climbed too.

“Common doesn’t mean ineffective. Walking is common. Eating is common. Should none of that be filmed either? The point was never whether he smokes — it’s HOW he smokes.”

Baek Si-on didn’t retreat.

“I can act lost. I don’t need a cigarette to act it for me.”

“Are you teaching me how to direct now?”

“You gave me the script four days ago.”

“Four days not enough for you?”

“Which is why I’m standing here arguing with you.”

❧ ❧ ❧

The departments, for their part, had their own partisans.

The camps, as divided

The cinematographer: pro-director. Backlit smoke would genuinely look beautiful — aperture and camera positions already designed; without the smoke, the frame’s layers must be rebuilt from zero.

The producing department: pro-lead. One glimpse of this buzz cut with a cigarette in a trailer, and tobacco brands would feast on three months of free heat — with the complaints, the youth-protection controversy, and the PR statements all billed to production.

Jo Woo-jin, the scene’s other actor: playable either way — and publicly committing to nothing.

Take the director’s side, risk the lead. Take the lead’s side, risk the director. And the two of them were uncle and nephew — they could finish arguing and go home to budae-jjigae like nothing happened. An outsider who picked a lane tonight might find the family reconciled by morning and his name on the other side’s ledger.

❧ ❧ ❧

High heels crossed the ground of the exterior set.

Baek Eun-ah pushed through from the back of the crowd, folders in her arms.

She had come to the set today with two items to report:

LAVUE’s trademark registration was now moving through its process — and the outcome of the SM negotiations over Bae Joo-hyun’s three-year endorsement.

But a few equipment racks in, she sensed the mood was wrong.

One glance toward the monitors.

Baek Eun-ah sped up.

Those two arguing at home was one thing.

Extending the family meeting to the full crew and the sponsors was, arguably, a complaint that the Baek household’s artistic atmosphere wasn’t circulating widely enough.

“What are you two doing?”

The uncle and nephew turned simultaneously.

“Making a movie.”

Baek Si-on added immediately:

“Stopping him from shooting a bad frame.”

Baek Jeong-hoon glared at him at once:

“WHO is shooting bad frames?”

Baek Eun-ah closed her eyes briefly.

Five minutes later to this argument, she judged, and it would have escalated into whether the Venice jury truly understood cinema.

“Dad.”

Baek Jeong-hoon was still mid-fume.

“What?”

Baek Eun-ah gestured in a slow circle that took in the whole stopped set.

“The lot bills by the hour.”

“Equipment rental, extra fees, vehicle dispatch, and the overtime that every squeezed-back scene earns afterward.”

“The time you two spend fighting over one cigarette is burning budget.”

That sentence worked on Baek Jeong-hoon best of anything so far.

On matters of art he would not yield one inch.

But as director and project lead, he could not pretend shutdown costs didn’t exist.

‘The Three Evils’’ cast was expensive to begin with.

Coordinating their dates was harder still.

Lose one scene today, and what slipped might not be half a day — it might be an entire date block belonging to Yoo Ah-in, Kim Hye-soo, or someone else.

Baek Eun-ah turned to Baek Si-on.

“Cousin — do you see how many people came today?”

Baek Si-on swept his eyes along the direction she indicated.

The brand representatives stood under the canopy in their pressed suits, proposals and product catalogs in hand.

“They didn’t hear your complete artistic discussion just now.”

“What they will see is: day two of a film’s production, the lead and the director arguing in front of the whole crew, the shoot stopped, the staff standing around waiting.”

Baek Eun-ah pressed the blade in:

“And yesterday, in front of the press, you said the kickoff belonged to the film.”

“Today, the loudest thing on this set is you and the director fighting.”

The set went quiet.

Baek Jeong-hoon had been struck in “budget and lost hours.”

Baek Si-on had been struck in “public image and losing composure in front of brand representatives.”

Baek Eun-ah didn’t seize the moment to keep moralizing; she asked one thing only:

“So — can each of you give one step?”

The uncle and nephew looked at each other.

❧ ❧ ❧

The cinematographer pulled focus.

Jo Woo-jin was placed at his preset mark, far behind Baek Si-on, to sharpen gradually from blur as the lens racked.

Baek Si-on stood behind the hospital doors with the examination report in his hand.

The slate operator stepped in front of the lens.

“‘The Three Evils’ — scene seven, take three, second slate.”

The crisp crack carried across the set.

“Action.”

The hospital’s automatic doors opened.

Baek Si-on walked out into the light.

Sunlight came down hard, head-on.

He stopped on the steps; the wind lifted one corner of the report.

The street was loud.

An ambulance passed in front; a mother and daughter hurried past him into the hospital.

Nobody paid attention to the man standing at the doors.

Baek Si-on looked down.

The words on the report fell into view.

Late-stage cancer.

He folded the paper and slid it into his coat’s inner pocket.

Then took out the cigarette pack, shook one loose, and set it between his lips.

The lighter slipped against his fingertip.

He regripped it.

Click.

No flame.

A second time.

The flame caught.

Baek Si-on’s eyes hung low, his attention resting on that small wavering fire.

Nothing visibly sad in his face.

The meaning of dying had not yet entered the body.

The doctor’s words, the results, the traffic on the street — all of it behind something that could not be breached.

The flame drifted toward the cigarette.

In the monitor, Jo Woo-jin’s figure sharpened slowly out of the long lens’s blur.

Baek Si-on’s attention crossed the flame and locked onto that distant face.

The lighter still burning.

His shoulders had already tightened first.

Focus pulled off the flame and Baek Si-on’s face and settled on Jo Woo-jin.

The two men recognized each other across the hospital-door crowd.

Jo Woo-jin was just raising a hand —

Baek Si-on was already turning.

The flame still lit.

He made it down the steps before he closed the lighter.

The cigarette at his lips dropped to the ground, bounced once, rolled aside.

Jo Woo-jin stood frozen.

“Hey!”

Baek Si-on charged into the parking area.

Jo Woo-jin bolted after him.

A taxi had barely stopped; the rear passenger was still pushing the door open when Baek Si-on slammed it back shut with one hand.

The driver leaned out and yelled something; he didn’t turn his head.

Jo Woo-jin rounded the tail of a car and lost the lane to an ambulance.

The chase tore into an alley.

Their breathing grew heavier and heavier.

Jo Woo-jin caught the wall and shouted ahead:

“I already quit the detective squad!”

Baek Si-on turned, braced on his knees.

“You couldn’t have said so earlier?”

“You never asked!”

Baek Si-on straightened and stared at him.

Jo Woo-jin spread his hands wide, demonstrating harmlessness.

“Don’t believe you.”

Baek Si-on turned and kept running.

Jo Woo-jin’s hands were still in the air.

“Aish...”

He gritted his teeth and chased.

“Good. Cut!”

Baek Jeong-hoon’s voice came over the walkie.

He reviewed the playback from every position, start to finish.

The cigarette stretch above all.

The unlit cigarette was, genuinely, better for the scene than the original design.

It kept the pause of death intact — and exposed the man’s absurd will to live that much earlier.

Baek Jeong-hoon picked up the walkie.

“Approved.”

Footsteps resumed across the set.

The camera crew moved to check the next position; the PAs walked the extras off the hospital doors.

Baek Si-on came back from the alley direction.

“Well?”

Baek Jeong-hoon pulled off one side of his headphones.

“Mediocre.”

Baek Si-on glanced into the monitor.

“‘Mediocre,’ and it passed in one take?”

“Saves film.”

“We shoot digital.”

“……”

Baek Jeong-hoon put the headphones back on.

“Set the next scene.”

He elected to end the conversation with the director’s remaining power.

❧ ❧ ❧

The lunch notice came over the walkies.

Lighting shut down part of the rig first.

Extras drifted toward the rest tents.

Props began checking in the documents, the cigarette packs, and the lighters used on the hospital exterior.

The support truck drove in from the parking area too — the moment the warmers hit the ground, the smell of hot food drifted down the row of tents.

Baek Jeong-hoon took off his headphones and had just stood to go claim a lunchbox when the producer caught his arm.

“Director — the brand people outside are waiting.”

“Let them wait.”

“They’ve waited all morning.”

“I also shot all morning.”

The producer kept smiling, and didn’t let go.

“There’s a restaurant booked nearby. We eat, we talk placements.”

Baek Jeong-hoon swept his eyes across the sponsor area.

A dozen-odd suited brand representatives all smiled their polite smiles at once.

The formation didn’t look like lunch. It looked like preparations to take the entire film apart, shot by shot, and auction it.

Baek Jeong-hoon’s brow came down.

“I shoot this afternoon.”

“You’ll be back before lunch ends — guaranteed.”

“Not going.”

The producer lowered his voice.

“Director — art can skip a meal. The budget can’t. They’re willing to pay.”

“Why didn’t you lead with THAT?”

Baek Jeong-hoon clamped the script under his arm and walked toward the brand representatives under his own power.

An artist’s principles are precious.

The budget is more concrete.

❧ ❧ ❧

In the temporary wardrobe room, Baek Si-on peeled off the costume coat.

The black tank top beneath had soaked through one layer with sweat.

An assistant handed him a dry towel; he wiped his neck in passing and pulled on a fresh tank.

Baek Eun-ah waited at the door, folders in her arms.

Her materials had been carried since morning; the covers were warm from her palms.

And then a set assistant walked up escorting Chung Eui-sun.

Baek Eun-ah glanced down at the folders.

Getting a work report onto the cousin’s calendar today was roughly as hard as wresting a meeting slot from a chaebol.

“Any plans for lunch?”

Baek Si-on, changed, tipped his head toward Baek Eun-ah’s direction.

“There were.”

Baek Eun-ah stood there in silence, hugging the folders.

Chung Eui-sun took the opening.

“Join us, then.”

Baek Si-on didn’t refuse.

Baek Eun-ah could only hug the folders tighter.

Her report was postponed again.

❧ ❧ ❧

Same day · noon

Reservation-only — one table, three people, one folder still unopened

‘Mokhyang’ · a hanok hanjeongsik house at the foot of the Namyangju hills, facing the Bukhan River

The destination was ‘Mokhyang’.

A reservation-only hanjeongsik restaurant hidden at the foot of the hills in Namyangju.

The building was a renovated traditional hanok — tiled roof, wooden pillars and stone steps kept in their original state.

A stone path led in; a few maples stood outside the low wall.

Through the wooden gate, a shallow pool sat at the courtyard’s center, a few fallen leaves drifting on the water.

The private room faced the Bukhan River.

When the paper doors slid open, the window held a stretch of slow water.

The far hills pressed low.

Noon sun landed on the river, and the wind broke it into a skin of fine scales.

The three of them took off their shoes and sat.

The hanjeongsik spread itself course by course.

The table, as laid

Gujeolpan — the nine-section divided dish, first to land.

Seasoned greens, cold and neat.

Grilled fish. Hanwoo beef. Soy-marinated crab.

The banchan rotating in behind, one small plate at a time.

The low table filled steadily — negotiation food, the kind that takes its time.

Chung Eui-sun drank a sip of tea and brought the talk to business.

“Aslan’s launch was last year. Hyundai owes you a formal thank-you.”

Baek Si-on lifted a piece of grilled fish.

“The endorsement fee was already paid.”

“Money and results are two different ledgers.”

Chung Eui-sun didn’t dodge Aslan’s current state.

The ad song ‘Way Down We Go’ had genuinely delivered the model launch numbers far beyond forecast.

Day-one orders broke four thousand units, and “the Baek Si-on effect” was briefly a proper noun in the financial press.

But as the endorsement heat cooled, the model’s own problems surfaced.

The positioning was awkward.

Upward, it couldn’t reach the true luxury marques; downward, it cost far too much more than an ordinary Hyundai.

To put it plainly: Baek Si-on had brought the customers into the showroom.

Aslan had failed to keep enough of them at the payment desk.

“So Hyundai is preparing to give up on Aslan?”

“Not discontinuing it yet.”

Chung Eui-sun lifted the teapot and refilled Baek Si-on’s cup himself.

“But we won’t keep leaning on star power to paper over a product-positioning problem.”

That frankness cleared a great deal of ceremony off the table.

Baek Si-on’s original contract had been a one-year base term plus a one-year mutual option.

The second year required both parties’ consent.

Once the option window opened, Baek Si-on’s refusal alone would bar Hyundai from continuing to use his likeness, his ad materials, his music, and his endorser identity.

The contract’s true negotiation node wouldn’t trigger until autumn — but the auto industry’s preparation cycles are long.

Ad shoots, collateral design, media buys, motor shows, overseas coordination — all of it has to be booked early.

Starting talks now was, honestly, not early at all.

“I want to show you something not yet public.”

Chung Eui-sun took a dark folder from his secretary and handed it across.

Baek Si-on received it.

A logo was printed at the top of the first page.

Genesis.

“We’re pushing internally to spin Genesis out from a model line into a standalone luxury brand.”

Baek Si-on opened the file.

The first page carried the new brand-mark design.

Beneath the draft: the initial positioning for the global markets.

The playbook, as borrowed

Hyundai’s overseas beachhead had been built on value-for-money: cheap, durable, family cars. Those tags sold millions of units — and became the ceiling hardest to dismantle the moment the company aimed upward.

Consumers could believe Hyundai builds a decent car. Believing it enough to pay Mercedes, BMW, or Lexus money over the same “N” badge was another matter entirely.

Toyota had already answered this exact question, with Lexus. Genesis intended to walk that road. And the new brand needed one face that could connect consumers in Asia, North America, and Europe at once. Korea currently had exactly one man who qualified.

Baek Si-on didn’t reach for the money.

He turned past the market plan and the product timetable and confirmed the scope first.

“Which model am I endorsing?”

“The Genesis brand entire. Models will rotate. The brand identity won’t.”

That was why Chung Eui-sun had come to the set in person.

An ordinary endorsement requires shooting advertisements.

A founding-brand endorsement embeds one person’s public temperament into the value definition a brand is born with.

“Terms?”

“Three years. Two billion won a year.”

Baek Eun-ah suddenly found the beef in her mouth flavorless.

She had spent days running around a three-year, six-hundred-million contract for Bae Joo-hyun.

Her cousin sat down to one lunch, and the figure came out with an extra zero.

Chung Eui-sun laid out the scope.

The Genesis offer, as tabled

Term: three years — two billion won per year.

Title: Genesis global brand ambassador.

Annual fixed ad shoot. Brand launch events. Korea and North America key-market campaigns.

A set number of motor-show or brand appearances per year.

Likeness and ad-material usage rights, for the term.

Not included: new songs written for ads, large commercial films, special overseas events, appearances beyond the agreed count.

Those bill separately. Plus: an annual sales reward of 0.1% of actual factory-settlement revenue across Korea and North America — no fixed cap.

Hyundai had tasted the Aslan project and knew better than to stuff Baek Si-on’s creative value into a standard endorsement fee.

Baek Eun-ah, beside them, had already set her chopsticks down.

If Genesis moved real volume in Korea and North America, 0.1% sounded small — multiplied by the total, it could exceed the base fee.

The incentive, as bound

Logic inherited from the Hyundai department-store duty-free binding.

The better the brand sells, the more he earns — and the more reason he has to plug his international resources in.

Alignment by design: his upside and the brand’s volume, wired together.

Baek Si-on set down his teacup.

“If the production models meet the standard in that file — I have no objection.”

The sentence left a condition standing.

He supported the domestic industry; supporting it with his eyes closed was a different thing.

Chung Eui-sun accepted it.

“Before any signature, you test-drive first. If the product disappoints, no contract.”

“Then I have no objection.”

Baek Si-on slid the file back to the center of the table.

Principle agreed, verbally.

The detailing would go to Hyundai Motor’s legal, Seo Eun-ju, Baek Eun-ah, and the two brand departments.

Chung Eui-sun raised his teacup.

“Then — welcome to Genesis.”

Baek Si-on lifted his own cup to it.

❧ ❧ ❧

Lunch ended.

Chung Eui-sun arranged a car to return them to the set.

The car ran south along the Bukhan River road; the water outside kept getting cut into broken silver by the trees.

Baek Eun-ah finally opened the folders she had hugged all morning.

“Can I talk now?”

A faint grievance rode in her voice.

Baek Si-on settled into the seat.

“Talk.”

Baek Eun-ah turned the first page.

LAVUE’s trademark registration had completed its process; filings were submitted across every category, and the contract manufacturer had begun batch production.

Next page.

SM had formally accepted Bae Joo-hyun’s personal endorsement.

For a girl group not yet one year past debut, the terms were generous enough — SM had no reason to refuse its artist a clearly visible individual ascent lane.

Especially with the ‘Descendants of the Sun’ cameo and the full Red Velvet consolation performance attached behind the contract.

Baek Si-on heard it out and nodded.

Now that Bae Joo-hyun was his endorser, the resources had to keep pace.

He couldn’t have her raving only in the KBS corridor.

“Does she have a solo magazine cover right now?”

“No.” Baek Eun-ah shook her head.

“Contact ‘CeCi’. Arrange her a solo cover.”

“Main edition?”

“Mm.”

With Baek Si-on’s current fashion capital, forcing Bae Joo-hyun onto a top-tier glossy was not actually impossible.

But it would do her little good.

Thin résumé, over-leveled cover — that only made her a target.

‘CeCi’ sat at the young-fashion, mid-tier position; its readership overlapped the beauty-buying public almost one to one.

Its main-edition cover had weight — without the rigid seniority order of the top glossies.

Exactly high enough for her to rise on tiptoe.

Without forcing her onto stilts.

Baek Eun-ah noted it in her memo, then asked:

“And if ‘CeCi’ says no?”

“Find whichever one says yes.”

Blunt to the point of rude.

But Baek Eun-ah knew ‘CeCi’ almost certainly wouldn’t refuse.

A magazine doesn’t only sell people who have already succeeded.

People about to succeed give the reader a stronger sense of taking part.

Baek Si-on went on:

“Then make a trip to LG Electronics.”

“To discuss what? A renewal?”

“The ad screens.”

“Which ones?”

“New York, Times Square. Ask them when that screen has an opening — the gloss launch needs it, consecutive, at least one full week.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

She swept back over everything she had just written down.

The contract wasn’t even formally stamped, and the support package already ran from Seoul all the way to New York.

This density of resources could no longer be explained by “find a rookie, get her cheap.”

This was not cheap. Not any part of it.

Baek Eun-ah looked up.

“Cousin — aren’t you being awfully good to Joohyun-unnie?”

Baek Si-on’s gaze rested out the window; a truck drove past and briefly blocked the river.

“She’s the brand’s first face.”

Bae Joo-hyun rises, LAVUE earns.

LAVUE explodes, and Bae Joo-hyun rises with it.

The two interests point the same way.

The more concentrated the investment, the harder the first wave hits.

The rollout, as ordered

‘CeCi’: one solo main-edition cover for Bae Joo-hyun — or whichever magazine says yes.

LG Electronics: the Times Square screen, booked around the gloss launch — consecutive, minimum one week.

Everything above, moving before the contract’s ink dries.

Seoul to New York, one endorsement deep.

Baek Eun-ah studied his profile for a few seconds and didn’t press further.

But she plainly didn’t fully believe it was only that, either.

Especially when, a few days ago in that van, she had heard Joohyun-unnie circling, sideways, around the state of her cousin’s abs.

These two.

One pouring out resources with both hands.

One digging for abs with both ears.

Each of them, separately, could be explained.

Put together, the explanation started drifting distinctly improper.

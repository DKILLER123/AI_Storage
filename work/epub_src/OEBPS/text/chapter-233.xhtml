The Boss Is Out, So the Company Sold Out

Late June

Launch countdown — every box ticked

Seongsu-dong · the LAVUE office

Late June.

Seongsu-dong.

The LAVUE office no longer looked like the empty room of a month before.

Packaging samples, product insert cards, media mailing boxes, and a row of numbered transparent storage cabinets now lined the walls.

The whiteboard had moved on from “brand logo design” to a launch countdown dense enough to read like a stock ticker.

Every item had its check.

The whiteboard, as ticked

COSMAX: first run of fifty thousand units produced, random inspection passed.

Film-forming speed, color deviation, scent, seal integrity: all clear.

CJ Korea Express: warehousing and product codes complete.

Brand site and social accounts: live.

Packaging, commercial master, first media plan: approvals closed.

Baek Si-on was still shooting ‘The Three Evils’; Baek Eun-ah ran external business, and Seo Eun-ju handled legal compliance.

The daily operation of the Seongsu office fell entirely on four young women who had been hired less than two months ago.

A makeshift crew was still a makeshift crew.

Except this patch of grass had started growing revenue.

“Commercials really should just be handed to great beauties.”

“On our mouths it’s called lip gloss.”

She pointed at the screen.

“On hers, it’s called a reason to buy.”

Nobody argued.

On COSMAX’s test paper, the two glosses and on Bae Joo-hyun’s lips were, frankly, two different products.

Only when the color landed on her face did the picture in a consumer’s head find its footing.

What they bought was a lip gloss.

It was also the imagined space of “if I put it on, I might get close to that kind of pretty.”

That is what an endorser is for.

Choi Na-young held down the play key.

“Run it again?”

The product girl said:

“Watching it three times earns no bonus. Moving product earns the bonus.”

That sentence outperformed any management system on earth.

Four chairs scattered at once.

The office refilled with keyboard clatter and phone calls.

❧ ❧ ❧

The media plan had been re-cut once, a few days earlier.

Baek Si-on had genuinely considered using the LG Electronics relationship to put the commercial up in New York’s Times Square.

A Korean makeup brand not yet officially on sale, its endorser on the biggest screen in the world — the press value wrote itself.

But when Baek Eun-ah carried the budget report into the ‘The Three Evils’ set, he withdrew the idea.

The media plan, as re-cut

Times Square: no European or American sales channel exists. A hundred thousand viewers on Fifth Avenue still have nowhere convenient to place an order — the spend buys a few “K-pop idol lands in Times Square” write-ups. Spectacle.

The same money, kept in Korea: bus stops in the key Seoul districts, subway light boxes, portal precision pushes, chat-platform ads, CJ cinema pre-roll, and LG’s in-store display walls.

The former has face. The latter has orders. He chose orders.

Launch day · ten minutes to noon

One countdown clock · four monitors · zero composure

The LAVUE office · the war room

Ten minutes to noon.

Choi Na-young sat in front of her computer — the site’s live dashboard on the right of her screen, the CJ logistics inventory system on the left; payments, server monitoring, and the brand accounts spread across two other monitors.

The first pre-sale stock was loaded: ten thousand two-color sets, twenty thousand units total.

Single retail: nineteen thousand won a tube. The two-tube set: thirty-five thousand eight hundred won.

The pre-sale existed to test: price acceptance, preference split between the two shades, and the set’s conversion rate.

The glasses girl read the rules through one last time.

“Order submitted locks stock.”

“Payment unfinished in ten minutes, stock auto-releases.”

“Two sets maximum per account.”

Choi Na-young stared at the system clock.

The countdown hit zero.

Product status flipped from “coming soon” to “buy now” on its own.

The pre-sale opened.

The first wave, as it sold

Access curve: vertical. Server load: climbing fast. Stock: falling.

10,000 → 9,300 → 7,800 — every refresh, new numbers.

The market girl barely had her phone up to record the curve when the back end dropped under 5,000.

3,400 → 1,020 → three digits.

The product page grayed over. One popup: SOLD OUT. Elapsed time: the clock hadn’t even left the opening minute.

Four people stared at the sold-out page in a daze that defied classification.

They had prepared to test price acceptance, shade preference, and set conversion.

The consumers had declined to leave them anything to observe.

They had simply handed in the exam.

The glasses girl adjusted her frames.

“We sell lip gloss, right?”

The product girl nodded.

“We do.”

“You’re sure this isn’t the boss’s concert tickets?”

“The price point says no.”

“Fair. His tickets aren’t this cheap.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Once they’d calmed down, the four pulled the traffic-source analysis, and the answer surfaced fast.

Choi Na-young’s startup-Vlog strategy, proposed back at the company’s founding, had updated steadily for a month.

The “what it’s like to work at Baek Si-on’s company” series had accumulated dozens of videos, every one of them pulling in his fans by the crowd.

Before LAVUE had sold a single unit, the account already held a following no nascent brand sees on normal terms.

When the pre-sale link went out, the fans arrived carrying one very simple idea.

His first real business. Of course they’d support it.

They had braced for “a top star’s brand must be expensive” — with cars, banks, luxury houses, and global deals on his resumé, a makeup brand wearing his name was easy to imagine at six figures of won.

Then the product page said thirty-five thousand eight hundred won for the set.

The purchase logic collapsed into a single word: buy.

The four crowded around Choi Na-young’s monitor for an emergency five-minute meeting.

The most common complaint in the comments: people stuck at login, address, and payment.

Meaning even a one-minute sellout hadn’t touched the ceiling of demand.

Choi Na-young proposed the restock.

“Put up another ten thousand sets.”

The product girl ran inventory on the spot.

The restock math, as run

Remaining stock: thirty thousand units.

Ten thousand more sets consumes twenty thousand.

The last ten thousand must also cover press samples, single-tube retail tests, breakage replacements, and after-sales reserve.

Margin: razor thin — and COSMAX’s second production run has to accelerate, now.

The glasses girl asked:

“Release it?”

The market girl turned her screen toward them — the calls for a restock still climbing.

“If we don’t dare put up ten thousand sets, what kind of brand do we think we’re running?”

Four votes, unanimous.

The restock notice went out.

The notice, as posted

Due to the first pre-sale wave selling out instantly, an additional 10,000 sets will be released in ten minutes.

The feeds picked it up and ran. Most buyers logged in early and pre-filled address and payment, waiting on nothing but the refresh.

Ten minutes later, the second wave opened.

Page refreshed.

Sold out.

“Did the site crash?”

“No.”

“Inventory API broken?”

“Normal.”

“Payment channel?”

“Normal.”

Every set had gone into locked status — users had been camped on the payment page and needed only to submit.

The system never even showed the four of them a curve this time; the second wave was over before it rendered.

And of course, sold out only meant locked. Payment confirmations kept running; a few timed-out orders re-released and were snatched back instantly by the waiting queue. The unpaid count shrank steadily to a number that could be ignored.

The glasses girl put the two rounds side by side on the screen.

“We sold forty thousand tubes of lip gloss today?”

Nobody answered.

Three months ago these four had been worrying about spring hiring in Korea.

Now they sat in a Seongsu-dong office where a barely-born brand had moved forty thousand units through two selling windows shorter than a commercial’s intro.

The final pre-sale gross: seven hundred sixteen million won.

Even after the OEM cost, logistics, taxes, the returns still to come, payment fees, and the launch spend — a first-day number the four graduates could not digest.

The euphoria lasted until the market-research girl asked one question.

“The people buying — are they here for the product, or for the boss?”

The cheering stopped.

The answer didn’t actually need discussing.

The first wave’s buyers skewed overwhelmingly Baek Si-on fans; the traffic source sat right there. The Vlog audience was, at bottom, his audience, glad to pay for his first company. And the price was low enough to require no consumer decision at all — thirty-five thousand eight hundred won, a Gangnam coffee-and-dessert set, zero psychological cost to support an idol.

Which was, to be clear, a good thing.

Every new brand dreams of a cold-start pool that can sell the place out on day one. LAVUE didn’t need six months of market education or trust built from zero — his name had already paved the road.

But if every future drop ran on fan support alone, LAVUE would curdle into star merchandise with better packaging — light sticks swapped for plastic tubes.

Choi Na-young looked up at the other three.

“The fans bought us our first report card.”

“Whether the next one sells out gets decided by people who actually wear makeup.”

Three nods, and back to work.

One combed the beauty-blogger list. One verified media addresses. One split the press samples.

The glasses girl dragged both rounds’ back-end screenshots into the next startup diary episode.

The title was already written.

So Beauty · 180K subscribers · new upload

“Fan tax or forty thousand sold for a reason? LAVUE’s two-shade gloss, no-filter tested.”

Cover: half a bare lip, half the layered duo — the split-screen everyone was about to argue over.

The first arrivals were, of course, Baek Si-on’s fans — seven of the first ten comments wore support-banner phrasing.

Then the current changed.

Ordinary users’ reposts and saves began accelerating ten minutes after upload, because the video surfaced something beyond star power: a genuinely practical fix.

Korean society’s demands on appearance management run high, and in that environment lip color is not decoration — it is the anchor of the whole face’s color.

LAVUE’s most obvious selling point needed no makeup bag, no ten minutes lost in a bathroom, no years of blending technique. Two tubes. Two steps. Fifteen seconds. Touch-up time, cut from five minutes to fifteen.

To women sprinting for the morning subway, redoing lips between classes, or finishing a dinner date — that is not a marketing line. That is a working solution.

That night

One call past midnight · one family question unresolved

Namyangju · the nanny van, heading home

Namyangju’s night slid backward past the van windows.

Baek Si-on leaned into the airline seat, the next day’s call sheet open on his knees, mountain silhouettes blurred to black shapes outside while convenience-store and gas-station lights swept the glass and fell away behind.

He turned the call sheet’s second page.

The schedule was running absurdly ahead of plan.

‘The Three Evils’ carried real weight: the club violence block pushed through multiple levels of space; the politician assassination needed mass extras and exterior coordination; the church massacre was the film’s densest passage, visually and emotionally. Shot traditionally, the first two alone could drag a production for three months.

But the virtual-scene pre-visualization system Baek Jeong-hoon had commissioned from Bluehole had eaten the most time-hungry work in preproduction. On a normal shoot, the killer isn’t an actor doing one more take — it’s several hundred people standing on set while the director hunts the right blocking: lighting waits on camera, extras wait on blocking, the DP waits on framing, stunts wait on fight routes — and every instance of “wait” bleeds wages, equipment, and location rent.

‘The Three Evils’ still argued over a single cigarette on any given day.

But the cameras no longer got lost.

Result: a wrap projected for late July was now likely by mid-July — and if the church block went clean, possibly every one of his shots done in early July, leaving him what he had never once had: a complete runway to prepare his Hollywood debut.

He closed the call sheet, and was about to start dividing the second half of the year, when the phone rang.

Baek Eun-ah.

Baek Si-on → Baek Eun-ah · voice · the van, past midnight

“Talk.”

“Can’t you ever open with ‘have you eaten?’”

“Have you eaten?”

“I’ve eaten.”

“Then talk.”

“......”

A very small inhale came down the line — his cousin plainly found this concern to be of assembly-line quality, and had no intention of tutoring a midnight cousin in warmth.

“Pre-sale settlement is in. Two rounds, twenty thousand sets, forty thousand units. Timed-out and abnormal orders re-released; final valid gross: seven hundred sixteen million won.”

“Returns?”

“Very low so far. Most of it hasn’t even shipped — the real return rate waits until delivery. But the official account is currently unwatchable.”

“What happened?” Baek Si-on asked.

“The consumers are ordering us to go to the factory and screw on bottle caps.”

“Did they go?”

“Of course not.”

More acid in her voice now.

“Those four are project staff, not COSMAX assembly line workers. The comment section currently believes that if Choi Na-young would just stop editing her startup diary, the saved hours would spontaneously manufacture twenty thousand lip glosses.”

“Good suggestion.”

“Good how?”

“Next episode: film it at the factory.”

“......”

Baek Eun-ah suspected that, at the bone, her cousin and the comment section were the same species of capitalist.

“One more thing.”

“What?”

“Director Lee Joon-ik called this afternoon.”

Baek Si-on’s fingers stopped at the edge of the call sheet.

“‘Sado’?”

“Mm. Postproduction is done. Distribution is scheduling internal screenings and the publicity war — he wants you back in Seoul next week. First he needs to know how much promotion you can commit to.”

‘Sado’ was not a film aimed only at the Korean box office. What Lee Joon-ik and the distributor were actually sighting was Korea’s submission for the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film — and “the submission” has never been achieved by printing words on a poster. Korea may send one film a year. ‘Sado’ first had to beat every other domestic candidate at the Korean Film Council’s review — and a jury weighs market proof as well as quality. Domestic box office isn’t a hard metric, but a film its own country won’t watch is hard to sell to juries, overseas distributors, and awards publicists as a traveler in more foreign climates. Promotion was therefore part of the war: Song Kang-ho held the trust of “the Nation’s Song,” and Baek Si-on’s assignment was marching the film out of the period-piece ghetto and into the young audience. With both leads absent, no rice-chest poster could carry a whole campaign.

Baek Si-on understood the weight, and did not hide behind the Hollywood project.

“How many events does Director Lee want me for?”

Paper slid across a desk somewhere on the line.

“The distributor’s first table said twenty-seven.”

“...They plan to have me live in a cinema?”

She failed to hold back a laugh.

“I asked something similar. The distributor said they could book the promotional hotel near the theater.”

“Very thoughtful.”

“So I cut it for you.”

“To what?”

“Twelve. Six in Seoul, three in Gyeonggi, two in Busan, one in Daegu.”

Twelve was still a lot. It was also, against twenty-seven, sane.

Baek Si-on opened the call sheet again. ‘Sado’ needed its release and its submission. ‘The Three Evils’ was burning Lee Mi-kyung’s money. ‘La La Land’ and the Hollywood debut would decide whether he actually entered the North American system. And the tour contract’s venues and performance clauses were not going to evaporate out of respect for cinema.

“Understood. Hold time for the press conference and the first-week promotion tour too. Variety, we’ll see.”

Fast pen strokes on her end. Then, carefully:

“Want me to coordinate the dates with Abba?”

“I’ll talk to him myself.”

“You’re sure?”

Baek Si-on was, of course, not sure. Baek Jeong-hoon would not smile and release his lead because ‘Sado’ had Oscar ambitions. He would clutch his shooting plan and ask the simplest, most unanswerable question in the world:

— And what happens to my movie?

“And if the talks blow up?”

“Then I send Mom in.”

“......”

Baek Eun-ah had discovered something about this family: no matter how the conflict was dressed — as film art, as Oscar plans, as international scheduling — the final resolution always routed through the same place.

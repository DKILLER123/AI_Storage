Chapter Two Hundred Fifty

The Bottom Line of Art Is the Insurance Company

They had practiced until two in the morning.

Baek Si-on collapsed into bed the moment he got back to his room, and Emma was probably much the same.

One night could not close the gap between them and professional dancers.

What actually changed was the mindset.

The warehouse — the next morning

No social crisis for the director to clean up this time

The next morning, the two met at the warehouse entrance.

Emma greeted him first.

Baek Si-on nodded in reply.

There was none of the first day’s social crisis that had needed the director to step in and clean up.

Two people who had been deceived by the same director, having confirmed that both of them were far from good enough, had instead gained a plain, honest comrades-in-arms feeling.

After the greeting, each went to his own training area.

……

He had been shoved into the piano room the moment he arrived yesterday, so Baek Si-on had only a limited view of the warehouse as a whole.

Arriving early today, he finally saw clearly what Damien had converted this space into.

The warehouse had been divided into several zones.

On one side were the dance-rehearsal area and the piano room, which he had seen yesterday.

The other side went entirely beyond expectation.

The camera crew was running a technical pre-visualization on a “simulated street” built from tape and cardboard boxes.

The shot, broken down to every beat — before a single real frame

Where the camera enters.

Which character it passes.

Where it turns.

Which downbeat has the actor step to which mark.

Every detail was first built into a rough one-to-one version out of cardboard boxes, tape, and manpower.

Before the official start of shooting, the camera crew would run the entire route dozens of times.

When they finally reached the real set, all they would have left to solve was the weather, the light, the actors’ condition, and whatever went wrong on the spot.

As for where the camera should go — that question had long since been settled.

Baek Si-on stood beside a café exterior wall built from cardboard boxes, watching the cinematographer complete another move.

Baek Jeong-hoon had done similar pre-visualization when shooting Green Fly.

Damien’s method was more traditional:

Cardboard boxes, tape, manpower.

Baek Jeong-hoon’s relied more on technology:

Virtual engines, digital pre-visualization.

The tools seemed separated by a whole technological era, but the two methods arrived at the same place by different roads: both existed to press the waste of time and budget to the minimum once real shooting began.

Baek Si-on stood watching for two minutes, then went back to the piano.

……

As the high-intensity project preparation rolled out in full, Baek Si-on entered once more the state of extreme compression he knew best.

The day, compressed to the bone

Morning — piano. The jazz melodies Sebastian played most, over and over, until the fingering was carved into muscle memory. His teacher had moved from “correcting his hand shape” to “demanding tone and emotion.”

Afternoon — tap and partner-dance rehearsal.

Evening — the list of reference films Damien sent.

Past midnight — sliced into fragments by messages from Seoul.

The reference-film list — the grammar of the Golden Age musical

Singin’ in the Rain

Funny Face

The Band Wagon

The Umbrellas of Cherbourg

Fred Astaire’s footwork was so relaxed it seemed to write on the floor.

Gene Kelly’s physical power was like a slingshot that could fire at any moment.

The two styles were utterly different, but their common point was clear: their bodies looked effortless.

Baek Si-on knew how much effort “effortless” took to achieve.

The small hours were cut into fragments by messages sent from Seoul.

Between Los Angeles and Seoul lay the entire Pacific and most of a day’s time difference.

Baek Si-on’s deep night in California was exactly Choi Jin-ri’s evening in Seoul.

Video calls became the couple’s only fixed point of contact.

Choi Jin-ri witnessed the whole process, from “just off the plane, in good spirits” to “the dark circles under his eyes gradually becoming the norm.”

She had said “take care of yourself,” said “can’t you sleep earlier,” said “I’ll hang up, go rest.”

Baek Si-on agreed to every one, and kept not one.

In the end Choi Jin-ri gave up trying to persuade him.

She could not change the way Baek Si-on threw himself at things, but she could be the reason he paused for at least twenty minutes every day to talk to a living person.

Once his sleep was compressed to four or five hours, a subtle change came over Baek Si-on as a whole.

His reactions slowed.

His movements grew slower.

Every so often he would space out.

His expression gained a kind of non-aggressive weariness.

The first time Damien caught him in this state during a rehearsal break, he stood in the doorway and observed quietly for a while.

Then he walked over to the production designer and said one thing:

“The way he looks right now — he already resembles Sebastian quite a lot.”

The production designer fell silent.

Sebastian was down-and-out; Baek Si-on was being squeezed dry by the director and the training plan working hand in hand.

The causes were different, but the finished effect might be the same.

Hollywood’s styling department discovered for the first time that sleep deprivation could actually save them part of the makeup budget.

It was just rather hard on the actor.

One day at noon.

The warehouse’s temporary canteen was full of crew.

The meals were matched to the actors’ training needs.

Grilled chicken breast.

Rice.

Vegetables.

And a nutritional drink whose flavor came very close to wall paint.

Baek Si-on was halfway through his meal when Baek Eun-ah carried her own plate over and sat across from him.

She chatted normally about the schedule for a couple of lines, then lowered her voice and said:

“The Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism just called.”

Baek Si-on’s chewing slowed.

“President Park visits China in early September, to attend the parade marking the seventieth anniversary of victory in the War of Resistance.”

“They’re inviting you to join the president’s delegation to China.”

In 2015, China–Korea economic and trade relations were in the last stretch of their honeymoon.

South Korea’s export dependence on China was extremely high, and President Park, braving the diplomatic pressure of the South China Sea issue, attending a Chinese military parade as the leader of a member of the Western camp, was itself an extremely delicate piece of political balancing.

The Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism wanted the delegation to include a cultural symbol soft enough and international enough.

Baek Si-on was the best fit.

So long as he attended,

this delicate diplomatic operation could be packaged into the soft narrative of “Korea extending cultural-cooperation goodwill to China.”

Baek Si-on understood the logic.

He respected the political significance of President Park’s visit to China, and acknowledged the importance of China–Korea trade to the Korean economy.

But he declined.

The reason was exactly the same as when he had earlier refused to take a political side at the Busan International Film Festival, and politely turned down the Seoul City Hall plaza concert.

As an entertainer, he had no obligation to serve personally as a political prop, nor should he.

More critically, he knew this honeymoon would not last.

Once the camera plan landed a year from now, China–Korea relations would deteriorate rapidly.

The handshakes and smiles of now would all be repriced by public opinion by then.

Baek Si-on said to Baek Eun-ah:

“Reply to them — I’m shooting a film in America, my schedule conflicts.”

Baek Eun-ah noted it down and went to reply.

The four words “schedule conflict” were both face-saving and true.

He really was shooting a film.

As for whether he would go if he had no schedule — that was a question that did not need answering.

The last week of August.

Korean Film Council — updated admissions data

Sado — cumulative admissions break nine million

One million left to the Korean film “ten-million club”

Distributor Showbox already negotiating with the three major theater chains to extend the theatrical run

With the summer season’s heat naturally fading, and Sado’s current word of mouth and occupancy, a two-to-three-week extension would make the ten-million breakthrough only a matter of time.

Baek Si-on received the data Baek Eun-ah forwarded while practicing piano.

With the hand that had just played a wrong note, he replied with a single message: noted.

Then he kept practicing.

Showbox’s publicity team waited a good while, received no second sentence, and in the end could only write “lead actor emotionally stable” into the non-public work log.

Los Angeles — the 105/110 freeway interchange ramp

The first weekend of September — the first official shoot day

The first weekend of September.

Los Angeles.

The interchange ramp where the 105 and 110 freeways met.

The La La Land crew, four months in preparation, finally reached its first official shoot day.

The initial strategy Lionsgate had set for the film was to compete for the main competition at Cannes the following year, then lock in a summer release.

The timeline no longer allowed any further delay.

The scene Damien had pre-visualized hundreds of times in the warehouse with cardboard boxes and tape now had to be moved into the real world.

The very first scene to shoot was the most famous sequence in the whole film.

The freeway ensemble dance.

For this ramp, Damien had applied for a long time before obtaining a two-day weekend road-closure permit.

Two days.

One real freeway ramp.

Dozens of cars stuck in traffic.

Over a hundred ensemble dancers.

And the unshaded California sun.

An ordinary dialogue scene could be broken into wide shots, medium shots, close-ups, over-the-shoulder shots, and reaction shots.

Each time the camera position changed, the lighting, blocking, and focus could be readjusted.

The freeway gave the crew no such leisure, and the sun changed angle every moment.

The ensemble dancers were scattered among dozens of cars, jumping down from doors, roofs, and hoods, then moving forward along the traffic.

Shot the traditional chopped-up way, every camera-position change would require all the actors and vehicles to be reset.

By the time the scene was restored, the sunlight would already have changed.

Two days would not be nearly enough.

So Damien chose the long take.

Cut as little as possible, let the camera flow along with the ensemble, and compress the entire opening dance into one continuous, fluid sequence of shots.

All the technical pre-visualization by those cinematographers and dolly grips in the warehouse had been for this moment.

Yet despite countless rehearsals, once they actually shot on the road, problem after problem appeared.

Day one — everything that went wrong

Wind, engine noise, and distant road noise mixed together — the ensemble dancers could not hear the beat.

Someone jumping from a car roof landed off-target, nearly hitting the Steadicam.

One car door jammed, trapping a dancer in the seat, able only to watch the whole troupe dance away through the window.

After two in the afternoon, the shifting sun badly overexposed the whole frame — the actors’ expressions, the cars’ colors, and the road detail all swallowed by white light.

Day one wrapped.

Damien sat behind the monitor and scrolled from the first take to the last.

Not one complete, usable shot.

Over a hundred actors, dozens of cars, a full day of road-closure cost — and in the end, one hard drive full of failed footage.

Damien did not lose his temper either.

He picked up a pen and marked every point where a failure had occurred.

……

The second day.

Starting at five-thirty in the morning.

Damien broke down every problem from the previous day one by one, adjusting the ensemble’s jump-off time window and the camera’s starting position.

The softest sunlight window was between eight and ten in the morning.

Two hours.

The whole crew shot several takes back to back within those hundred and twenty minutes.

One of them finally ran all the way through.

The ensemble’s beat was accurate, the camera movement fluid, and the sunlight happened to fall from the side of the lens, forming a soft warm side-light without shining directly into the lens.

The actors’ jumps from car roofs to road surface were all clean and crisp.

Damien called out from behind the monitor.

“Cut.”

He walked to the front of the monitor and dragged the playback from beginning to end.

“That’s a wrap.”

This freeway ensemble-dance shot later became the most iconic opening of the entire La La Land.

A big-scale long-take sequence like this — for another director to pass even one take would count as fast if it took half a month.

Damien finished it in a day and a half.

So fast that Baek Si-on for a moment suspected the young director had some kind of time-compression device installed in his head.

……

Once the ensemble dance was done, Baek Si-on appeared right after.

According to the story, after the ensemble dance ends, traffic returns to normal and the cars creep forward.

Mia, played by Emma, sits in her own car and receives an audition invitation on her phone.

She looks down at her phone, not noticing that the traffic has already started moving.

Sebastian, played by Baek Si-on, drives a 1982 Buick Riviera convertible, stuck behind her.

A convertible means no roof to block the sun.

The ensemble dance was shot for two hours; Baek Si-on waited in that car for two hours.

At first he still wore a hat.

The styling team, worried his hair would be crushed, took it away later.

The crew had given him a sun umbrella.

Once the camera began adjusting positions, the umbrella was withdrawn too.

Honk—!

Honk—!

Honk—!

Emma glimpsed in her rearview mirror the driver behind her laying on the horn like a maniac.

Baek Si-on sat in the convertible, arm resting on the wheel, the impatience on his face pure and unalloyed.

Method acting still requires an actor to dig up painful memories, whereas the emotion the California sun provided was free, stable, and reached straight to the skin.

Damien silently gave a thumbs-up behind the monitor — this physiological irritation was more convincing than any method actor’s brewing.

Seeing the car ahead still not moving, Baek Si-on turned the wheel and swung around past her.

The two cars ran parallel for a short distance.

Emma rolled down her window and gave this rude man the middle finger.

Baek Si-on’s lips moved, making no sound.

But Emma read from his mouth that it was definitely not “have a nice day.”

“Cut!”

Damien walked out from behind the monitor, his face carrying a rare, genuine satisfaction.

“This one’s good.”

The crew began releasing the cars from their shooting state.

Emma got out of the car and walked over beside Baek Si-on.

“What was it you just said?”

“The Korean national art.”

“The national art? … K-pop?”

“… Yeah.”

And so Sebastian and Mia’s first meeting was officially completed, in one honk of a horn, one middle finger, and one silent curse.

……

The horn and the middle finger on the freeway were only Mia and Sebastian’s first impression on each other.

Afterward, on screen, the two kept running into each other.

Sebastian goes to a party, playing in a band the eighties pop songs he has no desire at all to play.

Mia recognizes the man who cursed at her in the traffic jam, deliberately walks up to the request booth, and asks him to play a song enough to kill the dignity of jazz.

The band’s set ends.

Sebastian squeezes out of the crowd; Mia pretends not to hear his greeting.

A few days later, they meet again at a café near the set.

Further on: the party breaking up, the parking lot, the car that can’t be found — two people who are plainly aware of each other yet insist on using caustic dialogue to prove they feel nothing.

These scenes were completed one after another in different areas of Los Angeles.

The locations on the shooting schedule changed every day.

Cafés.

Streets.

Party houses.

Studio backlots.

After wrapping, Baek Si-on still had to keep rehearsing dance and playing piano, and sometimes find time to handle the transpacific emails for the S5 Worlds theme song and the new album.

The work density packed into two weeks nearly caught up with the combined intensity of the Green Fly and Sado crews.

The only difference was that Korean films mainly tortured his mind, whereas La La Land had no intention of sparing his ankles and fingers either.

Cathy’s Corner — Griffith Park, mid-September

A corner platform on the Hollywood Hills, facing the LA skyline

Mid-September.

A mountain road inside Griffith Park — Cathy’s Corner.

This was a corner platform on the Hollywood Hills, facing straight toward the Los Angeles city skyline.

In clear weather, by day one could see the downtown high-rises, the crisscrossing roads, and the distant ridgeline.

At dusk, the scenery entered another state.

The sun sank toward the horizon.

The sky turned from gold to orange-red first, then faded to purple and deep pink.

The city had not fully lit up yet; the last layer of natural light hung on the skyline, dyeing the buildings, trees, and human silhouettes a soft ring of color.

Cinematographers called this brief window of light the “magic hour,” lasting less than half an hour each day.

Once it vanished, the sky darkened quickly into Los Angeles’s ordinary urban night view.

Damien chose this location to shoot Mia and Sebastian’s dusk duet, betting on that half hour.

The crew arrived on site at dawn.

Baek Si-on and Emma rehearsed from the morning.

From blocking, to lyrics, to the railing moves, to where to change shoes, to the tap section — every point had to be confirmed again and again.

In the afternoon, wind blew over from the other side of the slope.

Emma sat on a folding chair, looking up to check the sun.

“What if there are clouds today?”

Damien stared at the weather app.

“We keep waiting tomorrow.”

“What if it’s still cloudy tomorrow?”

“The day after.”

Baek Si-on worked his already-aching ankles.

“What if there aren’t any for a whole week?”

Damien answered:

“Then it means Los Angeles doesn’t want this film to happen.”

This director, who normally begrudged even buying a few extra cardboard boxes, showed astonishing patience in the face of the sunset.

The lighting nature provided charged no rent, and had a temper harder to negotiate with than a Hollywood cinematographer’s.

……

Dusk.

The sun finally began to sink.

The sky turned from bright white gradually to warm color.

Damien stood behind the monitor, glanced at his watch, then looked up again to confirm the color of the skyline.

The golden band of light was moving downward; orange-red began seeping in from the edges.

“Ready.”

The crew, who had waited all day, sprang into action.

“Action!”

Baek Si-on hooked his suit jacket over one hand, laid the other on the roadside iron railing, circled the railing once, and began to sing along with the music.

“A Lovely Night” — La La Land (2016)

Now playing — the dusk duet at Cathy’s Corner

The plot of “A Lovely Night” takes place at the stage where Mia and Sebastian are attracted to each other but neither is willing to admit it.

The two stand before the most romantic sunset in all of Los Angeles, yet what they sing runs contrary to romance.

Sebastian admits first: the night really is beautiful.

Mia immediately points out: the view is lovely, but the person beside her is wrong.

Sebastian expresses agreement.

Both insist they feel nothing for the other, and lament with a straight face: what a shame that such a wonderful night is wasted on someone they feel no attraction to at all.

The lyrics sound like an argument.

The dance steps, however, are flirting.

The feelings the characters hide behind their stubborn mouths are laid bare directly by the melody and the body’s movement.

Singing into the middle section, the two changed into tap shoes.

Baek Si-on and Emma completed an alternating change of position on the stone bench beside the mountain road.

He hopped onto the stone bench and tapped out two beats with his feet.

Emma lifted her face.

The scene before her suddenly overlapped with another night in memory.

The Venice Film Festival closing banquet.

That Asian young man who had just won the Volpi Cup, standing among the ham, the desserts, and the overturned wine glasses, singing.

Abstract, flamboyant — utterly unlike the way the restrained, taciturn Baek Si-on would ordinarily show himself at a Venice closing banquet.

At this moment, the stone bench at Cathy’s Corner and that dining table suddenly overlapped.

The corner of Emma’s mouth curved up.

This smile exceeded the bounds of the choreography and the character design.

It belonged to Emma herself.

According to the storyboard, she should not smile here; Mia was supposed to keep the “I feel nothing for you” expression throughout this song and dance.

To smile was to admit attraction ahead of schedule.

Baek Si-on noticed the anomaly immediately.

By his past experience, this take had already shown a clear deviation, and the safest handling was to stop the movement and avoid wasting the stamina that followed.

But weeks of continuous training had already given his body an inertia.

The music was still going.

Right foot down, left foot following, shoulders and waist turning with the rhythm.

The movement he had meant to rein in was carried along and extended, turning into a slightly smug bit of improvisation from Sebastian.

As if, discovering he had made Mia laugh, he deliberately showed off a little more of that obnoxious charm.

Damien did not call cut behind the monitor; the camera kept moving along its planned route.

Emma, realizing the shoot was still running, immediately caught Baek Si-on’s improvised reaction.

The smile on her face quickly reined in, but did not entirely vanish.

As if, having been amused by the other, she then remembered she was trying hard to dislike this man, and could only hide the feeling with a turned head and a light hmph.

……

The two returned from the stone bench to the mountain road.

The tap entered its second set of rhythms.

Entering the second eight-count, Emma’s right foot landed unsteadily, and her body gave a slight stumble.

She had rehearsed too long during the day; by dusk, the accumulated fatigue in her ankles began to show in the movement.

She immediately adjusted her center of gravity, using the next side step to hide the stagger inside the dance.

Baek Si-on kept partnering her, already assuming in his heart that this take would be reshot.

But Damien still did not call cut.

All the way until the song ended.

Sebastian and Mia stood at the edge of the slope, the magic-hour light dyeing their silhouettes an orange-purple.

“Cut.”

Afterward, Damien gave no comment.

He had the two return to the starting position and went on to shoot a second take.

The color of the sky was still within the window.

A third take.

A fourth.

A fifth.

Between each take, only enough time was left to check the equipment and let the actors catch their breath.

Because the sun gave them no leisure to discuss the performance.

The magic-hour light faded gradually from purple-pink to dark; by the time the fifth take was shot, the sun had fully set.

The city lights came on, the color drained from the sky, and it became Los Angeles’s ordinary deep blue and orange bands of light.

……

After wrapping, Baek Si-on and Emma huddled beside the monitor to watch the playback.

Baek Si-on personally preferred the third take.

In that one, his and Emma’s tap beats aligned perfectly, the expression control was on point, and technically there was no obvious flaw to find.

Emma agreed.

The third take was the most stable she had been all the way through.

Damien, sitting behind the monitor, instead replayed the first take three times.

The very one with the smile and the stumble.

Baek Si-on and Emma exchanged a puzzled look.

Damien dragged the first take’s progress bar to the point of the smile.

“When you smiled here, the Mia in the lens had already given up pretending.”

He pointed at the corner of Emma’s mouth in the frame, just reined in and then unable to help curving up again, and said:

“Her mouth is saying ‘I feel nothing for you,’ but her face can’t hide it.”

“And the stumble afterward, too.”

He dragged the progress bar to the second eight-count of the tap.

“When she comes down off the stone bench, her feet lose control — just like her heart.”

Emma was silent for a moment.

“You’ve explained my mistakes very romantically.”

“That’s the meaning of a director’s existence.”

“And what if I just fall down?”

“We’ll see whether Baek can catch you. If he catches you, we keep it; if he doesn’t, we file an insurance claim.”

Baek Si-on: “…”

❝

Hollywood’s final bottom line for artistic creation, it turned out, was underwritten by the insurance company.

— “Catch her and we keep it; miss and we file a claim”

In truth, neither Mia nor Sebastian was a professional dancer.

One was an actress who failed audition after audition; the other a poor pianist who insisted on playing obscure jazz.

The tap they danced was never supposed to be perfect.

If every step were precisely in place and every beat clean and crisp, it would instead look like two professional dancers performing, rather than two ordinary people dancing clumsily in the Los Angeles night.

The third take was more “correct.”

The first take was more “real.”

Damien’s judgment in choosing the first take was more accurate than either Baek Si-on’s or Emma’s.

……

Back at the hotel.

Baek Si-on finished his shower, still digesting the feelings from today’s shoot.

His phone rang.

He thought it was Choi Jin-ri, but it turned out to be from Hyundai Motor.

Hyundai Motor → Baek Si-on · the Genesis launch, two things to confirm

The Genesis brand would officially launch this November as an independent luxury brand, with its first flagship model to follow soon after.

“There are two things we’d like to confirm.”

One — the ad-song’s progress. Baek Si-on had earlier proposed adapting “Starboy” into the Genesis brand song; Hyundai wanted to know whether the finished product could be delivered by mid-October.

Two — the brand short film. Genesis is positioned as a high-end line independent of the Hyundai parent brand; if the short film showed only Baek Si-on driving alone, the message would come closer to a successful man’s tool.

But if a woman of equal texture appeared in the frame, sitting in the passenger seat or standing at the other end of the story, the brand expression would change.

The car would no longer be only career and status; it would enter life, companionship, and relationship.

The premium from selling a lifestyle was obviously higher than from selling a car.

“Is there a specific candidate for the female lead?”

“Not finally decided yet.”

The Hyundai side’s ideal candidate needed high enough public recognition, while covering the young female consumer group.

Her image could not be too youthful.

Her fashion expressiveness could not be weak.

More importantly, standing beside Baek Si-on, she could not be entirely swallowed up.

There was no shortage of female entertainers in the Korean entertainment world who met the conditions, such as Jung Soojung, Bae Suzy, and Lee Sung-kyung.

But someone who could truly hold her balance beside Baek Si-on in a commercial short film was not as easy to find as one might imagine.

“So for the specific candidate, we’d be glad to hear your recommendation.”

“Let me think about it.”

He hung up.

……

After setting down the phone, he leaned back in his chair, and a few faces flashed through his mind.

Some were beautiful, some were close to him, and some had already formed a good on-camera rapport with him.

But none of this was urgent.

Genesis would not officially launch the new car until December — first finish the film in front of him, then think about the rest.

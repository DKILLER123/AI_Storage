Chapter Seventeen

The Seoul Marathon: Buying Tens of Millions in Lottery

June Hongdae convenience store Buying the first lottery ticket

A convenience store in the Hongdae commercial district. Si-on pushed the door of the first one, walked to the lottery counter, and in one flowing motion filled out two slips — a two-leg parlay, the Netherlands over Spain at 4.5, and Costa Rica over Uruguay at 8.0.

He set ten thousand won in cash on the counter. The clerk ran the slips through the machine with practiced hands and printed a thermal ticket, handing it over. He took it, then turned to Baek Eun-ah standing in the doorway with a face like a stunned fish, pulled another ten thousand in cash and a matching slip from his pocket, and pressed them into her hand.

“What are you doing?”

“Pay for it. Or the penalty’s yours to clear.”

“…”

Eun-ah ground her teeth and, like a reluctant accomplice in a robbery, trudged to the counter and handed over the money and the slip. The clerk looked at her, then at Si-on by the door, his expression going odd. But he did his job, printed the ticket, and passed it back. She took it — and hadn't even glanced at it before Si-on yanked it out of her hand.

“Move. Next one.”

Eun-ah: “…?”

She hadn't registered it yet, and she was already being hauled out the door.

In Korea, legal sports betting is hemmed in by extremely strict law to prevent gambling addiction: a per-person, per-single-purchase cap of ten thousand won.

Si-on didn't make trouble for the clerk. He made trouble only for his own feet and his cousin's.

Store two. Store three. Store four.

Eun-ah started to sense something was off.

“Cousin, are we buying this much?”

“Mm.”

Store seven. Store eight. Eun-ah's feet started to ache. She'd worn a strappy sandal with a little heel today, because it looked nice. It didn't look nice anymore.

Store ten. Fifteen. Twenty. Twenty-five.

Eun-ah cracked.

“Cousin, my feet hurt—”

“Bear with it. Almost there.”

“Almost where?”

“Twenty-five more.”

Eun-ah: “… You've lost it.”

Fifty stores, two tickets each, a hundred tickets, ten thousand won apiece. Her cousin had gone genuinely insane.

By store thirty, her heels had finally betrayed her. A blister had rubbed raw on the back of her foot, each step a stab of pain. She crouched on the curb, pulled off her shoe, and watched the growing wad of tickets in Si-on's pocket, deciding he'd used his head for target practice in the army.

Si-on glanced down at the raw blister. He said nothing, turned, and walked back into the store behind them. Came out a minute later with a box of band-aids. He tore one open, crouched, and pressed it gently onto the blister.

“Go home.”

“What about you?”

Eun-ah rubbed her calf.

“I keep going.”

She tried to argue, but he took her by the arm and folded her straight into a taxi that had just stopped at the curb, shutting the door for good measure. The taxi pulled out. Eun-ah turned and looked back through the rear window at that flower-print shirt standing under the neon, growing smaller, and her nose stung.

June Day Hongdae Seventy tickets and a bad feeling

By the time he'd bought ticket number seventy, the sky was properly dark. He'd powered through from the Hongdae district all the way to Hapjeong on nothing but his own two feet, the soles of his feet going numb, his throat dry.

This was too slow. He pulled out his phone, scrolled his contacts, and dialed the number of the one “friend” he'd ever actually wanted to save.

“Come out. I'm buying you dinner.”

Half an hour later, Han-teuk stood at the door of a GS25 in Hapjeong, staring at the ten thousand won and the betting slip that had been forced into his hands, and could have wept. He'd known there was no such thing as a free dinner. If he'd known the cost of this meal was walking the streets of Seoul on a madman's errand until his legs broke, he'd have carried IU's bags all day instead.

But he was already here.

At ticket one hundred, the two of them collapsed into the plastic chairs outside the store, gulping water from a bottle. Han-teuk watched Si-on slide the ten million won worth of tickets, one by one, into a waterproof documents pouch, and finally couldn't hold it in.

“Mr. Baek, have you lost your mind? The Netherlands beating Spain, fine — but why the hell would Costa Rica beat Uruguay? Can you even read a match?”

“No. But I've got a feeling.”

“What's a feeling worth?”

Han-teuk rolled his eyes.

“At current odds — three hundred sixty million.”

“…”

Han-teuk didn't believe it. If the world turned on feelings, there wouldn't be a spot left on Han River bridge. But when he looked at Si-on's eyes — not feverish, but terrifyingly calm — something in his chest dropped. He hesitated. Then he shot to his feet and dragged Si-on toward the store across the street.

“Come on, buy me the same two. I'll trust you once.”

June SM Entertainment, seventh floor The f(x) practice room

Apgujeong. The SM Entertainment building. A practice room.

The subwoofer bass was looping the hard-hitting electronic beat of Red Light, the title track of f(x)'s upcoming comeback. The choreography was intricate, the staging thick enough to choke on.

“Stop!”

The choreographer clapped his hands and cut the music.

“Ten-minute break. Sulli — that turn was half a beat behind. Run it twice alone in a bit.”

“Yes, teacher.”

Choi Jin-ri panted out the reply and walked to the corner, sliding down the wall till she was sitting on the floor. Tired. Every muscle in her body protested. But oddly, compared to that earlier smothering dread, this was just physical exhaustion now.

She reached for her phone in the bag by the wall. The screen lit up. No new messages. She stared at it a second, let her thumb drift through her contacts and stop on the thread labeled “Baek Si-on.”

The last message was still the “good work” they'd sent each other on wrap day. She bit her lip, typed.

She looked at the line, the corner of her mouth twitching. Why did this man always talk like that?

She'd sent fifty thousand won of her own. A few hundred million? She didn't understand football, didn't understand lottery, and had no idea why Si-on had suddenly gone out to buy so many tickets. But she did know one thing.

This man never did anything without a reason.

No sooner had she sent it than a weight dropped onto her right shoulder.

“Who are you texting? You're grinning like that…”

Chapter One Hundred Forty Four

Gold Beneath a Man’s Knees (Physically)

The same evening · Los Angeles, local time A limo, a highway, and one unscheduled banking stop Los Angeles · the road from LAX toward SB Projects The AMAs run-of-show is open on one knee, and the other knee has just said stop the car.

The car headed for SB Projects.

Scooter Braun sat alongside, eyes down on the AMAs run-of-show, confirming the evening’s rehearsal time with Baek Eun-ah.

Passing a bank, Baek Si-on suddenly spoke.

“Pull over for a second.”

The driver checked the rear-view.

“Here?”

“That bank, up ahead.”

Baek Eun-ah looked up.

“Oppa? What is it?”

“Withdraw some cash.”

Scooter’s head came up.

“What do you need cash for?”

“To use.”

“To use for what?”

“Spending.”

“……”

Extremely reasonable.

So reasonable there was no possible follow-up question.

The car stopped at the curb. Baek Si-on pulled on a cap and got out.

Baek Eun-ah instinctively moved to follow; he raised a hand and stopped her.

“The bodyguard and I are enough.”

She watched his back recede, not entirely reassured.

Scooter, for his part, was not especially tense. He glanced at the bank’s sign.

“Relax, Sweetheart. He’s not going to fight Dwight Howard inside a bank.”

“……”

Thanks.

She did not feel comforted.

Inside, the bank was nearly empty. Baek Si-on sat down at the counter and took off the cap.

The teller was a white woman in her thirties. She looked at him once, and placed him.

“Mr. Baek?”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“I’d like to withdraw twenty thousand dollars in cash.”

The teller snapped into professional gear. Verify identity. Sign. Confirm the account.

Baek Si-on sat waiting at the counter. In the lobby, a small electronic board was scrolling foreign exchange, precious metals and fund products.

the lobby board · fx · precious metals · funds

GOLD — today’s quote ≈ $1,200 / troy ounce · roughly $38.5 a gram.

FX RATES — scrolling, as always.

FUND PRODUCTS — scrolling, as always.

He gives it one casual glance. It is the most expensive glance of the bank’s week.

“How much is gold right now?”

The teller checked the screen.

“Today’s quote is about twelve hundred dollars an ounce. Call it thirty-eight dollars fifty a gram.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

Genuinely cheap.

About as cheap as bitcoin at three hundred-some dollars a coin — the same slightly absurd cheapness that makes you want to look twice.

The teller handed over the cash. Baek Si-on did not get up.

“Do you carry gold bars?”

The teller blinked.

“Gold bars?”

“Mm.”

“Bars need an appointment — but we do stock American Gold Eagle coins, one ounce each.”

“In stock?”

“Yes.”

“I’ll take a hundred ounces.”

“……”

Her hands stopped moving.

“You mean — one hundred coins?”

“Mm.”

She re-verified his account permissions. Then she called the manager over.

“Mr. Baek — you’re certain you’d like to purchase one hundred one-ounce gold coins?”

“Certain.”

“At today’s spot price, plus the three percent mint premium, the total comes to —”

“Take it from the account.”

The manager’s expression went professional and warm in the same instant.

“One moment, sir.”

What followed was a mildly cumbersome but extremely efficient sequence. Identity confirmed. Account authorized. Precious-metals transaction confirmed. Risk disclosure. Signature. Signature again.

At last the manager walked back from the vault, carrying a silver metal case.

He opened it.

Inside, one hundred gold coins lay in perfectly ordered ranks, each one sealed in its own clear plastic capsule. The obverse carried the raised figure of Liberty; the reverse, a bald eagle with its wings spread.

Baek Si-on looked at it.

“Good.”

The manager closed the case.

“Sir — would you like us to have security walk you out?”

“No need.”

Kang, the bodyguard, stepped forward from behind him and took the silver case in one hand.

The manager looked at Kang’s shoulders. Then at Baek Si-on.

“Understood.”

He handed over a receipt. Baek Si-on signed the last page, turned, and walked out toward the door.

Back in the car, the first thing Baek Eun-ah saw was the case in Kang’s grip.

“Oppa.”

“Mm?”

“How much cash did you take out?”

“Twenty thousand.”

“Then why does it need a case?”

“Bought some gold coins.”

The car went silent instantly.

Scooter looked up from the run-of-show.

“Gold coins?”

Kang opened the case at exactly the right moment, and gold lit up the interior of the car.

“……”

“……”

“Oppa — are you investing in gold?”

“Mm.”

Baek Eun-ah exhaled in relief.

“Oh. That’s fine, then.”

Scooter visibly relaxed too.

Gold. Good. Very traditional. Very ancient. Very human civilization. It sounded a great deal more dependable than internet magic coins. He had been picturing a spontaneous bitcoin shopping spree; now that he had seen actual coinage, he felt the world return to a range he could understand.

Baek Si-on had no plans to explain.

The gold was, of course, an investment.

It was not the main purpose.

The main purpose was to make Scooter and Eun-ah stop talking. The moment those two had heard the word “bitcoin,” they had reacted as if he were about to throw the entire S.W Studio cash flow into a Las Vegas slot machine. So he had bought gold first — visible, touchable, a stack that looked like a palace treasury when you set it out.

As for the bitcoin.

Later that night, when there was a quiet moment, he would go all in — quietly.

The same night A lobby that already knows, and a rehearsal room that is about to learn Los Angeles · SB Projects · the lobby, then the second-floor rehearsal room Eight dancers, one choreographer, one chair — and one hundred ounces in a silver case by the door.

The car reached SB Projects.

The moment they came through the lobby, the front desk and several staff members looked up at once.

SB Projects · the lobby · the bonus weather system arrives

“Baek!”

“King is here!”

“Legend!”

The core project team — the ones assigned to him — brighten in a way that is visible from across the room. The last time Legend topped Billboard, he paid each of them a ten-thousand-dollar bonus. In capitalist society, that kind of memory outlasts love.

The atmosphere was warmer than last time. SB Projects’ collective affection for Baek Si-on was extremely simple at its root. This was not an artist. This was a bonus weather system. When he showed up, there was overtime pay in the air.

“Thanks for the work, everyone.”

Baek Si-on answered each greeting in turn. He was not the type for warm small talk — but every person who greeted him got a pause, a nod, a word back.

For the people of SB Projects, that was plenty. This legend’s emotional wiring had always been energy-efficient. Low battery. Very long battery life.

Scooter walked beside him, watching the staff reactions, and said quietly:

“You’re very popular in my company these days.”

Baek Si-on glanced at him.

“Because of the bonuses?”

“Because you make money.”

A pause.

“Of course, the bonuses also matter.”

Baek Eun-ah trailed behind with her tablet, nodding silently.

Very honest. Very American.

The rehearsal room was on the second floor. The door opened onto eight backup dancers and a choreographer, already waiting. The floor gleamed, one whole wall of mirrors, speakers and crates of water bottles in the corner.

The choreographer was a white man in his thirties in black athletic wear. He came forward and offered his hand.

“Baek — nice to meet you.”

“Nice to meet you.”

Scooter did the introductions:

“He’s the on-the-ground execution choreographer for the AMAs stage. Tabitha’s framework is locked — he’ll drill the details with you.”

“Thanks for taking this on.”

“Are you kidding? Being in the room on this stage with Tabitha’s framework is gold-leaf on the whole team’s résumé.”

“Then let’s start.”

“OK.”

First run. Baek Si-on sat down on the chair. The intro rolled; the dancers unfolded at his sides. He barely moved — which for any other singer would be laziness, and for the King stage is the entire point. The steadier he is, the more the movement around him means.

First verse ended. Second verse: he rose from the chair, the eight dancers split into two rows and went down on one knee, he walked the corridor between them, they rose and fanned into the wedge behind his back — and the chorus broke open, and only then did the movement truly start.

The choreographer clapped.

“Good. Very good.”

Scooter, beside the mirror, nodded too.

“That’s the feel.”

Second run, vocals on. The dancers began locking to his tempo; footsteps and drums merged; and when the chorus landed, the eight of them sharpened into that wedge behind him. In the mirror — black-clad dancers, one chair, the man at the centre, and a carefully engineered air of surrender — the stage had a skeleton now.

After two runs, Baek Si-on did not continue. He looked at Baek Eun-ah.

“Eun-ah.”

“Mm?”

“Go bring the coin case up.”

“……”

She had already guessed what he intended. A man who buys one hundred gold coins out of nowhere does not do it just to flash them at Scooter and her in a car. It hurt. It genuinely hurt — one hundred ounces, purchased an hour ago, the case not even warm yet. But she also knew that the things he decided to do usually had a logic to them. Infuriating logic, sometimes.

“Okay.”

She went. Scooter looked at Baek Si-on.

“What are you doing?”

“Handing something out.”

“Handing out — gold coins?”

“Mm.”

“……”

Scooter began, quietly, to regret finding gold so reassuring in the car. Traditional, yes. But tradition is powerless against a man who spends it like coffee money.

Soon Baek Eun-ah returned hugging the silver case, Kang behind her to set it down in the middle of the floor. Baek Si-on opened it, and the gold flashed under the white rehearsal lights.

All eight dancers went silent at once.

It was a very real kind of silence. Nobody stays casually indifferent in front of a case of gold.

He took out sixteen coins, in pairs, and walked the line of dancers, handing them over one by one.

The first dancer, a Black man, froze.

“Uh — for me?”

“Mm.”

The second, a Latina dancer, turned the sealed coin over, looked at Liberty on the front, then at him.

“Is this real gold?”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“An ounce each.”

A low wave of awe ran the length of the room.

Scooter stood to one side with a hand over his face. Wonderful. Very Baek Si-on. Other artists buy their dancers coffee after rehearsal. This one hands out gold. This had passed generosity somewhere back — this was an ancient reward system, installed in a mirror-walled room in Los Angeles.

the rehearsal room · the treasury opens

Dancers on the AMAs stage8
Gold coins per dancer2 × 1 oz American Gold Eagle
Total distributed16 troy ounces

When the coins were gone, Baek Si-on stood in front of them.

“In the far-off East, there is an old saying.”

The choreographer’s eyes flicked to Scooter on instinct.

Scooter spread his hands. Don’t look at me. I have no idea which school of Eastern philosophy this is.

“It goes: beneath a man’s knees, there is gold.”

The dancers glanced at one another. Strange phrase, for an American ear. But there were coins in their hands, and they listened carefully.

“It means a person’s dignity, and the act of kneeling, is worth more than gold. A knee bends only for heaven, for earth, for one’s ruler, one’s parents, one’s teacher.”

“……”

By the door, Baek Eun-ah felt the scene tip into pure surrealism. Her oppa — lecturing a room full of Americans on the gold beneath a man’s knees. As cultural exports went, this one was being delivered with force.

He was still speaking.

“You are professional dancers, working for a salary. But for this stage, you are going to go down on one knee in front of me.”

“I know it’s only choreography. I know it isn’t real fealty. But I don’t like owing anyone their dignity.”

“So I’m giving you the gold in your knees back.”

The rehearsal room went completely quiet.

Eight dancers stood there holding coins, and for a while nobody said anything at all. They had all met generous artists before. Tippers. Buyers of dinner. Givers of sneakers. But never anyone who had stood in a rehearsal room with real gold coins and solemnly explained why he would not accept their kneeling for free.

In that instant, the stage move changed meaning. It stopped being a piece of surrender choreography. It became labour — acknowledged, respected, taken seriously.

The Black dancer came back to life first. He looked down at the two coins in his hand, then up at Baek Si-on.

“Man… ”

He smiled and put out his fist.

“Respect.”

Baek Si-on reached out and tapped it.

The others gathered round.

“Thank you, Baek.”

“Best rehearsal of my life.”

“I will now kneel gladly — like a knight swearing fealty.”

“……”

Scooter’s eye twitched. Wonderful. These men were no longer backup dancers. They were reserve members of the golden armoured knights of Baek Si-on.

And the room’s temperature genuinely did change from that moment. Before, everyone had been professional — show up on time, work for the pay, hit the marks, know where to sink the weight, know whose job it was to make the man in the chair look enormous. That was professionalism. Now there was something else in it.

Third run. The intro rolled. He sat. The dancers unfolded — and the same eight bodies were visibly heavier now. Not showy effort: every landing, every turn, every knee that went down carried real weight. Second verse — the two rows knelt, he walked through — and this time the mirror did not show stage design. It showed a rite.

Scooter, beside the mirror, had been ready with a joke. He ended up just stroking his chin.

The rehearsal ran on another two hours. At the end, the eight dancers came over for fist bumps, unprompted. The Black dancer patted his own chest first.

“AMAs day — we’re going to make you look like the actual King.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Thanks for the work.”

The man laughed.

“Took your gold — of course you get your money’s worth.”

From the back, Baek Eun-ah muttered:

“It had better be worth sixteen ounces.”

Scooter heard it and failed to hold in a laugh.

The Four Seasons, Beverly Hills. Ten forty at night.

Fresh from the shower, wrapped in a bathrobe, Baek Si-on sat on the sofa with his phone and looked over his personal balance.

10:40 p.m. · the personal ledger, surveyed in a bathrobe

The Under Armour money had landed. Six million gross had become three hundred and fifty thousand dollars sitting in his account after taxes and splits. The CJ and LG endorsement fees, for now, all sat in the S.W Studio corporate account — and that money did not move. The studio had taxes to pay, salaries to run, projects to keep alive, and the compliance checklist Seo Eun-ju had drawn up, which was headache-inducing at a glance.

He looked at the string of numbers and let out a slow breath.

Money. When you have little of it, it matters enormously. Past a certain quantity, it is a string of digits.

He opened the browser.

Typed in Coinbase’s address. Registered. Verified his identity. Linked a card. And then the risk-disclosure page came up.

coinbase.com · account just activated

“Cryptocurrency is a highly volatile asset. You may lose its entire value in a short period.”

“Please confirm you understand the risk above.”

YES / NO

He presses YES. The verification email lands; he opens it; confirms; returns. The account comes alive.

BTC / USD — $317.80

A small green arrow twitches upward. +0.4%. A genuine rally — or the platform’s UI, welcoming a freshly registered potential buyer it has just detected.

Baek Si-on looked at that number.

Cheap to the point of making a person sigh.

He clicked buy. Entered an amount: five million dollars. Confirmed. The system threw the risk warning up a second time; he skimmed it and pressed confirm. The order began filling in batches, and a few minutes later his account held a new string of digits.

Roughly fifteen thousand seven hundred bitcoins.

Baek Si-on stared at the screen for a few seconds, and said one line, quietly:

“See you around.”

November 23 · morning Awards day, and the corridor is already a battlefield Los Angeles · the Four Seasons, Beverly Hills · the eighth-floor corridor Two racks of black velvet, four assistants pretending not to exist, and one very bewildered housekeeper.

November 23. Los Angeles. The Four Seasons, Beverly Hills.

Through the elevator lobby came a small fashion task force. At its head, the North American public-relations director of Tom Ford: Marc. A deep-grey suit, a tie knitted to a razor-thin blade, shoes polished to the point of reflecting human vanity. Behind him, two assistants pushed a rank of garment racks, every piece shrouded in black velvet, like a squad of secret weapons about to be sent up a hill.

He rode the elevator to Baek Si-on’s floor with no expression at all — but his whole body spelled out one sentence: today, we put Tom Ford Gangster on the AMAs.

To Marc, this had never been an ordinary brand booking. It was fate. It was stamped by the internet. It was the god-given moment an Asian man in bespoke tailoring barged into an NBA street-culture narrative — and a brand seizes that, or it forfeits the right to complain about history.

The elevator opened. Marc took one step out.

And stopped.

At the far end of the corridor, another fashion task force was just emerging from the service elevator. Two assistants. A rank of racks. The same black velvet shrouds. The only difference was the man at the front: the North American public-relations director of Gucci, Vincent.

The two directors stared down the corridor at each other for three full seconds.

Marc turned his head and dropped one low sentence of French to his assistant. The assistant froze for half a second, understood, and accelerated. Marc started pushing too. The rack wheels rolled a low rumble into the Four Seasons’ thick carpet.

Gucci was not slow either. Two ranks of haute couture accelerated in parallel.

A housekeeper, leaning on her cart, stood to one side with pure bewilderment written across her face. She had not planned to witness American-couture bumper cars on her shift.

The Gucci rack was about to win the corner into Baek Si-on’s suite — when Marc’s hand shot out, grabbed his own rack’s crossbar, and heaved it sideways.

Clack.

His wheels locked dead against the rear axle of the Gucci rack. The Gucci assistant nearly went over forward with the momentum.

Marc planted himself behind his rack, arms on his hips, and raised one hand to point at Vincent’s nose.

“Get your junk cart out of my sight line.”

Vincent smiled.

“This is the Four Seasons. Not anybody’s private wardrobe.”

Marc came forward a step.

“You leeches bled our boss dry back then, and now you come for his muse? Go back to Florence!”

The Gucci assistant’s face changed. Vincent, on the other hand, was very steady. He looked down and adjusted his cuff.

“One: Mr. Ford walked out of Gucci unannounced, packed off who knows how many sketches, and never said thank you. Two: Mr. Baek has signed no exclusivity with Tom Ford. Three: two days of the internet calling him Tom Ford Gangster does not mean he is engaged to you.”

“So please, Marc. Get your junk cart out of my sight line.”

The muscle along Marc’s jaw twitched. He said nothing more — swung past his own rack, and shoved the Gucci rack outright.

Vincent’s assistant lunged to catch it, but the whole thing was already sliding away down the corridor. Vincent grabbed the frame with fast hands. A corner of black velvet tore loose — revealing a deep-green velvet suit underneath.

Marc’s eyes swept over it. The expression of a man served a pot of badly overcooked pasta.

“That’s it?”

“Your sack-cutting on Mr. Baek’s body would be a crime.”

Vincent came over fast.

“What did you just say?!”

“I said every Gucci suit is a sack!”

Vincent’s mouth opened. Rage briefly outran his English vocabulary. He switched to Italian.

“Tu, figlio di——”

Then he abandoned language altogether and went for the suits on Marc’s rack with both hands.

Marc blocked. Vincent pulled. Marc shoved back. Vincent drove in with his shoulder. The two racks shrieked against each other down the length of the corridor, and a corner of black velvet tore away entirely — the cuff of a silver-grey double-breasted Tom Ford suit slid into view.

Marc saw his own suit being tugged and his eyes went wide.

“Let go!”

“You let go first!”

“You damn Gucci!”

“You damn Tom Ford!”

The four assistants gaped from the walls. Nobody dared step in. This was a brawl between two North American public-relations directors. Whoever touched it first, resigned first. The safest play was to crouch by the baseboard and pretend not to exist.

The noise finally carried into the suite.

Baek Eun-ah pulled the door open and leaned half of herself out. First she saw the two racks jackknifed across the middle of the corridor. Then she saw Marc and Vincent elbowing each other in the ribs — French profanity in one mouth, Italian counterattack in the other, with a few phrases of English mixed in purely so the American assistants would know, definitively, that they were being insulted too.

The velvet shrouds were hanging crooked. The silver-grey Tom Ford sleeve showed at one rack; the deep-green Gucci velvet hung half-exposed from the other.

Baek Eun-ah watched for three seconds.

She had been about to say something. Something like — please stop fighting, both of you! Or: this is a hotel corridor, not the backstage of Milan Fashion Week!

She gave up quickly, because Marc had just shouldered Vincent into the wall, and Vincent had answered by seizing the Tom Ford crossbar with one hand and loosing a burst of Italian that could have cracked the stained glass in a chapel.

Baek Eun-ah went silent.

So, it turned out: the real Gangster was not in the arena. The real Gangster was in a Four Seasons corridor.

She withdrew, very calmly, and shut the door gently.

“Aish…… ”

Baek Si-on had been on the sofa with the AMAs run-of-show. He looked up.

“What’s wrong?”

Baek Eun-ah stood by the door with a complicated expression.

“There’s a fight happening outside.”

He set the papers down.

“Who and who?”

“Tom Ford and Gucci.”

“……”

The answer sounded like two brands finishing a long process of personification by finally arriving at physical violence.

He got up and went to the door. She stepped aside. He pulled it open.

Dead centre of the corridor, the war had entered its second phase — which is to say, each director had now stripped one or two pieces entirely off the other’s rack. The four assistants crouched along the walls. Intervening was overstepping; not intervening was negligence. The optimal strategy was to do nothing, and re-hang everything, piece by piece, once it was over.

Baek Si-on watched for two seconds. Then he, too, quietly withdrew and closed the door.

“Oppa — what do we do?”

He walked back to the sofa, sat down, and picked the run-of-show back up.

“Let them fight it out first.”

“……”

Very good.

Tonight’s red-carpet look would be decided by European haute couture, by single combat.

Chapter Two Hundred Three

Today Is Good for a Steal

An afternoon, early April · Yeonnam-dong · the studio that time forgot A lungful of nicotine, old coffee stains, and unidentified paper mold · three ashtrays like volcano craters · a director who betrayed indie ideals by buying an apartment · a district of shoe factories that will become the most expensive block in Seoul · a script two villains longer than the bragging at the wrap party · one phone call about zombies that ends in a nuclear weapon walking over by itself Yeonnam-dong → a window seat overlooking nothing yet → Pangyo, tomorrow

The Yeonnam-dong studio was exactly as remembered. When Baek Si-on pushed the door open, a gas blended of nicotine, aged coffee stains, and the mold of unidentified paper came at his face head-on.

“Uncle, are you cultivating immortality in here?”

He frowned and fanned a hand in front of his nose, twice, on reflex. Useless. Smoke of this concentration was not a fanning problem. It was a fire department problem.

Baek Jeong-hoon sat on the leather sofa from the Green Bottle Fly era, three ashtrays stacked on the coffee table in front of him, each mounded like a small volcano, one nearly burnt-to-the-filter cigarette in his fingers, a stack of papers in the other hand.

Baek Si-on walked in, opened a window first, then the air conditioner, then surveyed the room.

The studio, as found — identical to last year

Green Bottle Fly storyboards — still pinned to the wall.

Several crates of unopened bottled water — corner, unopened since purchase.

Film-theory books — one layer of dust. Beside them, an electric heater, unplugged.

“Green Bottle Fly’s profit share is banked, the Lion of the Future is collected. Why haven’t you moved studios?”

Baek Jeong-hoon stubbed out the cigarette.

“Spent it.”

Baek Si-on raised an eyebrow.

“Spent it? You went to Macau?”

The uncle glared.

“Bought an apartment.”

“An apartment?”

Baek Si-on genuinely hadn’t expected that. In his experience, directors of this species put earnings into the next film, into alcohol, or into loans for friends from some unnamed production. Buying property was, for an independent film director, very nearly a betrayal of the ideal.

“Where?”

“New build over in Seongsu-dong. Not far from here either — once I move, the studio can move into Eun-ah’s place. Saves a month’s rent.”

Baek Si-on did not file an appeal on Baek Eun-ah’s behalf. He was thinking about Seongsu-dong.

Seongsu-dong in 2015 was mostly old shoe factories, car-repair workshops, and red-brick warehouses. The streets weren’t narrow, but they were nothing to be proud of either.

But Baek Si-on knew that in two, three years, this district would become one of the most expensive trend-merchant blocks in Seoul. Blue Bottle, Dior pop-ups, independent galleries and select shops would sprout from the gaps between the old factories like mushrooms after rain. Housing prices would multiply several times over. Land prices, more. Even SM Entertainment would, in the end, move its headquarters into the area.

The uncle had bought for the quiet — and had stepped, entirely by accident, onto the single most certain appreciation zone in Seoul’s next five years.

Baek Si-on looked at that face — a face that in no way suggested a man who had just executed a brilliant investment — and silently awarded it a like. Then, in his head, he moved the item “sweep Seongsu-dong land” three slots up the priority list.

“Uncle, your eye was good this time.”

Baek Jeong-hoon didn’t follow.

“What eye? The whole block is broken factories. I just wanted quiet.”

“Quiet is good. Quiet places generally stop being quiet.”

The uncle frowned at him.

“Why do you sound like a fortune-teller?”

Baek Si-on smiled and didn’t explain. He planned to call Baek Eun-ah the moment he left. But first — the script.

❧ ❧ ❧

Baek Jeong-hoon tossed the stack of pages in front of him.

“Read.”

He lit a fresh cigarette.

“The nonsense you bragged at the wrap party — I built it for you.”

Baek Si-on picked it up.

The cover, as printed — white paper, black type

Tentative title — ‘The Villains’.

Alternate — ‘The Man Ranked Third’.

Written & directed: Baek Jeong-hoon.

Baek Si-on turned to page one. The synopsis.

The national wanted list · special report · as broadcast

Photo one — unknown to him.

Photo two — seen before.

Photo three — his own face. NUMBER THREE.

He stares at that “third” for a long time; then the television goes dark. He is not afraid of dying. He simply cannot accept ranking third even at crime. Verdict: before he dies, the two above him come down.

Baek Si-on nodded at the end of it. The core premise matched his wrap-party pitch; the uncle had made the character’s starting point more specific.

❝

Koreans understand ranking too well. Exams rank you. The army ranks you. Debut ranks you. Ratings rank you — and past death, even cemetery plots come in order. A man who can rank only third on the wanted list is this country’s deepest anxiety, pushed to its logical extreme.

— the premise, as read; the nation, diagnosed in one page

He kept turning. In the version Baek Si-on had originally sketched, the opening had the male lead crashing a mob boss’s birthday banquet and killing him there. The uncle had changed it. The script now read:

The opening, as rewritten — target one

The man: gangster, fifteen years ago — protection money in the Cheongnyangni red-light district, blood on his hands, tattoos on his skin, a résumé with no line that could see daylight. Today: a suit, a street in a Seoul district, a microphone and a banner. THE BANNER — “PROTECT THE COMMUNITY. PROTECT THE FUTURE.”

The staging: assistants, a secretary, the campaign team, dozens of placard-waving supporters. A beautiful speech — a school for the neighborhood, unlicensed structures cleared, winter heating for the elderly. A voice that carries, sincere posture, a shine of tears in the eyes. Unchecked: a textbook grassroots politician, “from the bottom to the National Assembly.”

Then the male lead walks, slowly, from the outermost edge of the crowd to the very front. The gun out. Raised. Three shots, in front of every supporter, every camera, the whole campaign team. The politician falls beneath his own banner — the words spattered with blood: a very ironic painting.

Baek Si-on looked up.

“Uncle, this change is properly deranged.”

Baek Jeong-hoon leaned back into the sofa, voice level:

“Gangsters slashing with knives is the last century’s play.”

He exhaled smoke.

❝

Today’s gangsters raise their hands and vote in the National Assembly. Kill a man with a knife in an alley and the news runs three days. Shoot a man in a suit who is gathering votes in the street, and this country aches for a year.

— Baek Jeong-hoon, directing thesis, ash lengthening

Baek Si-on kept reading. The second evil. Number two on the wanted list. At the wrap party this one had been a blur — “the incarnation of violence and vice.” The uncle had made him concrete. In the script, the second evil is the operator of a top nightclub called “EDEN.”

EDEN, as written — Gangnam core, lights, music, alcohol, celebrities

From the street: indistinguishable from any high-end members’ club. The queue — models, trainees, second-tier idols, young people clawing toward the upper class. The VIP rooms — chaebol heirs, entertainment executives, the occasional passing Assemblyman.

Come for one drink and leave, and you have seen the most elegant face of Seoul nightlife.

The script writes the other face. Pills, supplied. Companionship, supplied. Hidden cameras in the booths, recording what politicians and chaebols cannot afford to see daylight — leverage, traded. Black money washed through the bar tab and fictional consumption until it runs clean. A full-service line for officials who need to “relax”: pickup to drop-off, zero records anywhere — except in his videos.

He is not merely a criminal. He is a key that opens a great many people’s secrets. That is why nobody touches him — not the police, not the prosecutors, not the press. Nobody knows whose face is on his drives.

Baek Si-on’s expression shifted as he finished. This setting, this logic, this “politico-business-vice chain behind a club owner” — why did it look more and more like that Big Bang fellow?

He raised his head and gave his uncle a very particular look.

“Uncle.”

“Mm?”

“Did you go do field research at places like this?”

Baek Jeong-hoon’s expression went instantly grave. He took the cigarette from his mouth and said, with full solemnity:

“Is your uncle that kind of person?”

Baek Si-on blinked.

“Isn’t he?”

The uncle snatched up the empty cigarette box from the table and raised it as if to throw. Baek Si-on sat in his chair and watched, and didn’t dodge. The box hovered in the air for two seconds, then came down, defeated; the uncle coughed.

“I heard it from people while drinking. In the industry.”

“Oh.”

“I didn’t go.”

“Mm.”

“Really didn’t.”

“I said mm.”

“Your mm had a wrong tone in it.”

Baek Si-on just looked at him. The uncle glared for three seconds, lost the standoff first, and muttered:

“Places like that do exist in Gangnam. Everybody knows. I dramatized it, that’s all.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Well dramatized.”

“Of course.”

The director’s confidence reinstalled itself.

“And I wrote it one level deeper. Not just the pills and the vice — the point is the videos in that man’s hands. He’s not a criminal, he’s a key. That’s why no one dares move. Nobody knows whose face is on those drives.”

Baek Si-on set that section down and read on. Next page. The first evil. The true number one on the wanted list. The source of all of it —

The Madonna, as written — the first evil

A woman. Publicly: a vast church and a charity foundation. Sunday worship, three thousand attendees. The annual charity gala, tens of billions of won raised. Her name in every major outlet’s public-interest pages, photographed with orphans, warm smile, dignified posture.

Her believers are not the desperate poor — the poor cannot pay dues. Her true congregation: chaebol wives, prosecutors’ wives, the family of conglomerate inner circles, and the core aides of the ruling party.

All of them defer to her, because she can “reach God.” She predicts election results. She decides which conglomerate a national fund flows to. She has, on occasion, sat in a certain Blue House office and reviewed an un-delivered policy speech on behalf of the man in the chair.

Baek Si-on closed the script.

“Good.”

The uncle looked at him.

“Just good?”

“Very good.”

Baek Jeong-hoon sniffed, dissatisfied with the information density of that review. But Baek Si-on had no intention of unfurling feelings over this topic. Whose prototype the character carried, both of them knew in their bones — it had all been said, thoroughly, the night of the wrap party. No need to re-confirm, one more time, whom exactly they were cursing.

He put the script down.

“This role — who do you want for it?”

The uncle stubbed the cigarette out and sank into the sofa.

“Kim Hye-soo.”

Baek Si-on considered.

“Not Jeon Do-yeon sunbaenim?”

The shake of the head came fast — something long considered and long rejected.

“Jeon Do-yeon is too literary. She plays a role like this, the audience decides she has reasons — that the era pushed her there, that she’s a victim too, in some sense.”

The eliminations continued:

“Lee Young-ae, no either. Too upright. She sits down and she reads as a protected chaebol wife — not as the woman who owns the chaebol wives. And — she almost certainly wouldn’t dare take it.”

A film of this material, from script to signed contract, doesn’t cross an acting threshold. It crosses a nerve threshold. Some actors have the skill and hold positions too high to risk it.

“So it’s Kim Hye-soo sunbaenim or nobody?”

“Yes.”

Baek Si-on turned over Kim Hye-soo’s screen history. The casino queen of Tazza. The coach of Take Off. That quality — fearsome without raising her voice, her smile scarier than other people’s anger. True enough: if Kim Hye-soo played a sham god holding the Blue House, the audience wouldn’t doubt it for a second. They might not blink even if she turned up in a documentary.

❝

From script to signature, a film like this crosses no acting threshold. It crosses a nerve threshold — and some careers sit too high to gamble them.

— the casting arithmetic, as settled between two ashtrays

❧ ❧ ❧

Casting talk parked itself there. The uncle’s expression shifted into “director beginning to face reality.”

“Si-on-ya.”

“Mm?”

“There’s one stretch I genuinely don’t know how to shoot.”

He flipped the script to a page in the middle and put his finger on a scene description. “The second evil — the massacre in the nightclub.” Baek Si-on glanced over it. The script read:

The nightclub sequence, as page-dumped

The male lead enters the club alone. Lights, music, alcohol, crowd. One hundred plus extras distributed across the dance floor, the booths, the bar, the corridors, the toilets, the kitchen.

He moves from the ground floor to the second, from the dance floor into the VIP room, passing through at least seven distinct spaces — each with different lighting conditions, different obstacles, different crowd behavior. Some scream and run. Some drop and cover. Some fight back. Some are trampled.

Through all of it, the male lead carries out the slaughter calmly, systematically.

And in production terms: one hundred walking routes designed — who runs where, who falls when, who collides with whom, all pre-staged. The lead’s path mapped — entry, reload points, turns, which camera tracks which. Strobe lighting, unstable frequency, one mistimed setup and the whole take dies. Plus recoil simulation, shell ejection direction, blood-pack trigger timing…

Baek Si-on looked up.

“The scene is excellent.”

The uncle massaged his temples, thoroughly irritated.

“Excellent, yes. But I don’t know how to shoot it. Back on Green Bottle Fly, you chased a man down a street and I followed with one handheld camera and we were done. This is a hundred people moving inside a closed box at the same time. If I handheld it again, the audience leaves the cinema and registers at the vertigo clinic.”

Baek Si-on understood the shape of the problem. Baek Jeong-hoon was an indie director: street violence, family tragedy, two people clinging and crying by the Han River — top-tier. Large-scale, multi-thread, high-density action — no experience. Not an ability problem. An industrial-experience problem. Korean directors, mostly, either leaned on the action director to carry it or let the edit hide it. The ones who could decompose a complex action sequence into a Hollywood-grade industrial flow timed to the second were vanishingly rare.

Baek Si-on thought, then said:

“Hollywood shoots these with previsualization now. You build the entire set in 3D first — walls, pillars, the bar, the stairs, all to real proportions. Then every character’s path, every extra’s escape route, every camera move, the lighting rhythm — all of it goes in and runs as a simulation. On the day, everyone walks the positions the simulation already proved.”

The uncle’s eyes lit up. Then went out again.

“I know it works — Director Bong used it on Snowpiercer. But that thing is billed by the second. Ask Dexter Studios — the Along with the Gods effects people — what they charge, and one minute of CG previz is a quote that will give me a heart attack on the spot. And our material is politically sensitive. The CG houses are afraid of it too — if the Blue House makes one phone call telling them not to take the job…”

He didn’t finish. He didn’t need to. Baek Si-on heard all of it: the problem wasn’t just money and scheduling. It was political risk. Korean culture industries had never once run in a vacuum; a film that aimed at the Blue House could land on an unpublished “watch list” at the script stage.

Baek Si-on thought, then took out his phone.

“I’ll call and ask.”

“Who?”

Baek Si-on didn’t answer. He was already dialing.

Yeon Sang-ho ↔ Baek Si-on · voice · the previz question · three rings

“Boss Baek?”

Yeon Sang-ho’s voice sounded freshly escaped from a set; somewhere behind him, people were shouting about something.

“Director Yeon — sorry to bother you.”

“No bother. What is it?”

No pleasantries.

“The zombie crowd scenes inside the train carriages in ‘Train to Busan’ — how did you previsualize those? Did you use Dexter?”

A laugh came down the line.

“Dexter? We didn’t have that budget.”

“Then how did you solve it?”

“I hired a game company.”

“A game company?”

“Yes. Game engines now — Unreal, for example — do scene modeling and real-time rendering extremely fast. You find a team that knows the engine, they build your set inside it, your characters run on off-the-shelf game skeletons, and blocking, lighting — all of it simulates in real time.”

A pause.

“The character models look a bit like blocks, nothing like what a film VFX house makes. But for blocking and camera-move precision it’s completely sufficient. And the most important part—”

“The price?”

“Less than a tenth of Dexter’s quote.”

Beside the phone, Baek Jeong-hoon’s ears stood straight up. He couldn’t make out the words — but “one tenth” carried perfectly.

“Any company you’d recommend?”

Yeon Sang-ho thought, rummaging in memory.

“Pangyo has plenty of game studios. The big ones — Nexon, NCsoft — won’t take this kind of work; they’re busy running their own games. But some of the mid-size studios take outsourcing. You could try Bluehole… I hear they’re struggling. Nothing big after ‘TERA’ to carry them — they’re looking for investment and taking outside projects to keep the lights on.”

The line held. Baek Si-on said nothing at all.

“Hello? Boss Baek? Still there?”

“Here. Please send me their contact, Director.”

“Will do — the second I hang up.”

“Thank you.”

“For what — between the two of us.”

The call ended.

Bluehole. Multi-player on a single screen. One enormous map. A physics engine. Real-time collision at scale. String those keywords together and push two years forward — a hundred people drop onto one map, and only one of them walks away. Every internet café, every livestream, every esports arena on the planet, occupied by one game. PUBG.

And its developer was the small company Yeon Sang-ho had just described as “solid fundamentals, rough times.”

Baek Si-on was holding forty million dollars and had been circling the question of where to put it. The targets he’d shortlisted were all decent — but none of them was the nuclear weapon, the one that doubles in two or three years and builds the stake for buying an NBA franchise. And now the nuclear weapon had walked over on its own legs. Wearing the costume of “helping uncle previsualize a movie.”

The projection, as assembled from six keywords

Multi-player, one screen — yes.

One enormous map — yes.

Physics engine, real-time collision — yes, exactly their trade.

Two years forward: one hundred drop in; one walks off. The cafés, the streams, the arenas — full.

Name of the weapon: PUBG. Current status of its maker: taking previz outsourcing to make payroll. The world does occasionally run on karma.

The uncle had sat bolt upright on the sofa, eyes locked on Baek Si-on’s face with the expression of a patient waiting outside the lab for his results.

“What did they say?”

“There’s a game company called Bluehole. Made ‘TERA’. Engine and scene tech are solid.”

“A game company can do this?”

“Same engine, different rendering. Film-grade is offline rendering; game engines render in real time — slightly worse picture, but you can change anything at any moment. We’re making previsualization, not final effects. It’s enough.”

The uncle nodded. The technical detail washed over him entirely — but “a tenth of the price” and “it’s enough” were two phrases he understood perfectly.

“Then will you run this for me? If I go knocking myself, they may not even open the door.”

“Mm.”

Baek Si-on stood and walked to the window, looking out at Yeonnam-dong in the afternoon. The sun was good. An old man walked a dog on the corner. The sign of the café across the street swayed gently in the wind. Very quiet. Very ordinary. Nobody knew what was currently happening inside his head.

“Si-on-ya.”

The uncle called from behind him.

“What?”

Baek Jeong-hoon leaned into the sofa, cigarette raised:

“That face you just made — that wasn’t a face thinking about movies.”

Baek Si-on turned.

“Uncle, do you think the game industry will be bigger than film one day?”

The uncle started — then snorted, disdainful.

“Film is art. Games are toys. How is that even a comparison?”

Baek Si-on smiled.

“True.”

He pushed the door open and walked out. Baek Jeong-hoon watched the back of him vanish around the stairwell corner, and could not shake the feeling that the kid’s smile, just now, had been off. As if he was laughing at him.

Or as if he was laughing at the whole world.

Didn’t Bring a Wet Wipe Today

December 19 · 8:17 a.m. until nightfall One phone that would not stop shaking · a boardroom surrenders · retail learns fear · one cat, one doorway, one cheek Seoul · a bedroom, a YG meeting room, a Gangnam storefront, Yongsan’s electronics floor, one cat house, one apartment corridor A meme escapes containment and enters the economy; five brands discover what they are actually selling; a man interviews cats like roommates; and a wet wipe, for the first time in recorded history, is forgotten on purpose.

December 19. Eight-seventeen in the morning.

Kim Min-jun was woken by his phone.

Not exactly woken. It was closer to being woken by a suspicion that Seoul was having a small earthquake.

The phone was under his pillow, buzzing nonstop, one side of his face gone numb from it. He fumbled for it half-asleep, got stabbed in the eye by screen brightness before his lids opened, and stared at the notification shade.

Full. Stacked. Crammed.

YouTube. Instagram. KakaoTalk. Plus three forum DM badges from communities he barely remembered joining.

Kim Min-jun stared at the screen for two seconds.

Then sat bolt upright in bed.

“What is this?”

He had gotten home from Jamsil near two in the morning. His ears had still been ringing in the shower — sixty thousand voices chanting one name do not check out of your skull just because the show wrapped.

He had thrown a rough cut together. Barely thought about the title.

the upload · title as filed, 2:11 a.m.

Baek Si-on FREE Concert VLOG | Is it actually free? I went in and found out the man even managed my butt

Posted. Phone facedown. Unconscious before the first view counter ticked.

On a normal week, fifty thousand views was his ceiling — a feast his subscribers grudgingly paid for.

And now.

Views: eight hundred thirty thousand.

Refresh: eight hundred sixty thousand.

Refresh again: nine hundred thousand.

Kim Min-jun’s soul left through the ceiling.

Kim Min-jun stared at “Butt: thank you, Baek Si-on” for a long time.

Then pulled the blanket up to his chest in silence.

It was dawning on him that he might be about to become famous.

Not because the footage was good. Not because his fish cake soup review had been sincere. Because last night’s concert had been so far past reasonable that even a mid shooter with a genuinely happy stomach could walk one lap with a camera and get shoved in front of the entire nation.

He opened the back end. The counter kept going.

Nine hundred eighty thousand. One million.

Kim Min-jun looked at the number and felt, briefly, like crying.

Then the professional kicked in. Because a mature YouTuber, facing the first million-view video of his life, knows the first order of business is not gratitude.

It’s the title.

He thought, hard, for three seconds. And retitled:

the upload · title, professionally revised, 8:41 a.m.

Saved. The click-through will only go up from here.

Korean internet being Korean internet, the fighting continued, the sniping continued, the professional haters stayed professionally on duty.

And yet some things, laid out on the table, shut even them up. A cushion on a cold December bench. A hot drink at a free show. A bus home for fans who came from far away. None of it was sentimental. None of it was glamorous. All of it was specific — specific enough that pretending not to see it took actual work.

The truly targeted victims were the entertainment companies.

YG Entertainment, planning office, weekly meeting in progress. Today’s agenda: merchandise strategy for the year-end family concert.

Instead, one minute in, the PR lead threw Kim Min-jun’s video onto the conference-room screen.

On screen: Kim Min-jun, seated on a gray cushion, light stick in hand, hand warmer in his pocket, half a cup of hot yuja tea in front of him, telling the lens:

“This concert charges no admission — but Baek Si-on already spent the money on our butts, hands, stomachs, and emotions.”

The conference room was silent for three seconds.

Someone swore under their breath.

“Madman.”

Someone else adjusted their glasses.

“The problem is the fans have already started using it to yell at us.”

He switched the screen to the forum threads.

A sentence like that, spoken out loud in a major entertainment company’s planning meeting, was objectively absurd.

But this was Day One After Baek Si-on’s Concert. The first day in Korean entertainment history that a boardroom had seriously convened on the subject of the fans’ butts.

The concert’s side effects refused to stay in boardrooms.

A Gangnam Under Armour flagship had been open for barely an hour when a knot of customers formed around the down-vest and training-jacket wall. The clerk assumed they wanted the Si-on-worn training line. The first customer handed him her phone instead.

“Excuse me — this vest? Do you have it?”

He looked. A fan-cam photo from outside Jamsil last night: volunteers in uniform Under Armour down vests, out in the wind, handing out light sticks and hand warmers. And next to it, a second shot: Baek Si-on in a backstage corridor in nothing but a black Under Armour sweatshirt, mid-conversation with staff.

Blurry. Barely legible. Did not matter — the internet had circled the brand marks with exam-level thoroughness.

“The volunteer one is a down vest — we carry something similar, but it may not be the exact model.”

“And the sweatshirt he was wearing?”

“We have that one.”

“Is it warm?”

The clerk inhaled to begin the trained monologue — fabric technology, moisture management, athletic use cases — and a second customer answered first:

“He wore it running around a stadium all night in December. I’m going to go out on a limb and say yes.”

The clerk: “……”

As sales patter, that was unsound.

As sales patter, it was devastating. Because a hundred repetitions of “thermal technology” in an ad cannot compete with: on a December night, a man wore this through a full workday and then sang for two hours on top of it.

A guy came up next, pointing at the black training jackets.

“Is this the Baek Si-on model?”

“A similar cut.”

“Then I’ll try it on.”

“Do you work out in this kind of thing?”

The guy paused.

“I don’t work out.”

“……”

“But I’d like to look like someone who works out.”

The clerk kept smiling. He was beginning to understand the new economy. After last night, Under Armour was no longer selling athletic equipment.

It was selling a very specific illusion: put it on, and stand a little closer to the man who sang ‘Legend’ in front of sixty thousand people.

By noon, searches for the black training jacket and the down vest had jumped on Under Armour Korea’s site. The customer-service inbox was getting more specific by the hour:

Can I buy it if I don’t work out?

The agent considered the last one for a long time, then replied, with flawless professionalism: “It is suitable for everyday wear.” She felt the answer was far too polite. What the customer had actually asked was whether wearing it would make him look like a man who can carry things.

Over at Yongsan Electronics Market, a wall of LG’s flagship screens was running a licensed tvN highlight cut of the concert, assembled overnight after the broadcast.

The white light splitting open over the main stage. Sixty thousand light sticks turning over in one cold-white sea. The chorus of ‘Legend’ lifting the entire bowl. The guest stages at their peaks.

The licensing fee was nothing to LG. Because you can print “high brightness,” “high contrast,” “true color” a hundred times, and none of it sells like a sea of light rising out of a screen at you.

Two high school girls were walking past. They were not there to buy a television. One held a milk tea. One wore a hagwon backpack.

As they passed the display, the cut happened to reach Red Velvet.

Cold-white light coming down. The lens pushing in. Bae Joo-hyun’s face, filling an entire wall.

For the half second of her raised eyes, both girls stopped walking at the same time.

The salesperson on the floor had been about to open with resolution, color gamut, refresh rate. Watching them, he concluded that today’s most effective pitch might not involve a single parameter.

This screen was clear. Clear enough that a rookie idol could be remembered, by name, off a concert highlight reel, by two girls who came to buy nothing.

He kept smiling.

“This model is very strong on concert replays. It feels… close to being there.”

Both girls turned to look at him. He paused, then heard himself add:

“It’s also very good at catching people.”

He regretted the sentence the instant it left his mouth.

The two girls nodded at him, gravely.

“It’s true.”

Salesperson: “……”

He suspected he had just invented his new pitch. He was probably right.

Variations on this scene were happening all over Seoul at the same hour.

Someone at a coffee counter heard the barista and a regular relitigating last night’s free-drink station. Someone on a bus watched a clip of Baek Si-on telling sixty thousand people to get home safe. Someone in a subway station stood in front of a Hyundai Department Store Duty Free teaser and finally connected three words — Baek Si-on, duty-free, and last night’s free buses — into one shape.

And someone walked into a mall and heard ‘Way Back Home’ playing live over the store speakers.

The concert was over, of course.

It had not actually stopped at nine-something the night before. It had been cut into ten thousand clips, screenshots, GIFs, and lunch conversations, and it had flowed into phone shops and clothing shops and electronics floors and convenience stores and subway cars and ordinary people’s breakfast tables.

A trending topic cools in a day.

Something that enters your daily life doesn’t leave on a schedule.

What Baek Si-on’s free concert made famous wasn’t just the songs. It wasn’t even the guests. It was that a list of brands that normally existed as names on a sponsorship page suddenly had shapes in ordinary people’s mouths.

Under Armour was no longer an athletic brand. It was “the vests the volunteers looked good in.”

SK was no longer a carrier. It was “the network that didn’t crash with sixty thousand people on it.”

LG was no longer a spec sheet. It was “the screen that made two girls stop and ask who Irene is.”

Not exposure.

A reason to be remembered.

Brands would kill for that reason. People are no different.

Some promises stay jokes as long as they live in the mouth. To become real, a promise needs a physical shape.

Say — a cat.

Afternoon. While the entire Korean internet was still litigating the fate of the national posterior, Baek Si-on — cap, mask — stood inside a Seoul cat house, making eye contact with row after row of cats.

In the glass room: one cat asleep face-down. One cat watching him with its tail flicking. One ragdoll pressed to the glass, staring at him with the expression of an examiner deciding whether this human was qualified to scoop its litter.

The shop assistant stood by, professionally smiling.

“What kind of cat are you looking for?”

Baek Si-on studied the roomful of fluffy candidates and answered with complete seriousness:

“Is there one that doesn’t shed, has a gentle personality and likes people, doesn’t wreck the house at night, and is unlikely to push a first-time owner toward giving it up?”

The shop assistant: “……”

That did not sound like a cat inquiry. That sounded like a job interview for a long-term cohabitant: emotionally stable, low-maintenance, available immediately.

She thought about it.

“If shedding is a dealbreaker — you could look at a Sphynx.”

A beat. Then, delicately:

“The, ah. Looks may take some accepting.”

“Where.”

She led him to the back. In the last temperature-controlled glass room, a small pink-white creature sat on a cushion.

No fur. Enormous ears. Skin going wrinkled everywhere it folded.

The overall effect was of something freshly off shift at an alien pet daycare, its face still carrying its grievances about Earth.

Baek Si-on stopped.

“This is a cat?”

“…Yes.”

She was not hearing this for the first time. The Sphynx look had talked many a customer off the ledge and back toward the fluffy aisles — the polite ones said “let me look around a little more” and never came back.

Baek Si-on didn’t leave.

He crouched down and looked at the cat through the glass for a long time. The cat looked back. Two creatures, evaluating each other in silence.

“Does it get cold?”

The shop assistant nodded.

“You’d want some cat clothing, and the room should stay warm all day.”

Baek Si-on absorbed all of it, item by item.

Paid. The assistant loaded the cat into a carrier and handed it over. The cat shifted once inside, then went still — apparently having accepted, with unusual speed, that it now belonged to a stranger.

Strong adaptability. Noted.

An apartment in Chengbei. A little after four in the afternoon.

Choi Jin-ri sat on her sofa, phone screen lit, and had been that way for the better part of half an hour.

She had only meant to glance at the concert’s reception. The glance had become a descent. Cushions. Hand warmers. Hot drinks. Shuttle buses. Every item made her eyes bend into a smile.

She liked watching people praise Baek Si-on.

Which was strange, when she thought about it. None of the praise was aimed at her — and it still made her happy, line after line. Like knowing about an incredible candy before everyone else, and then watching the whole world taste it and agree it was sweet.

Except the candy didn’t belong only to her.

Her smile cooled a degree at that thought. She kept scrolling. Her thumb stopped.

“Baek Si-on IU bunny ears.”

She stared at the trending tag for three seconds. Swiped it away with decision.

A mature person does not read such things.

Next.

“Irene on the big screen.”

Her thumb stopped again.

Also not reading that one. A mature person especially does not read such things.

Finally she opened the safest tag in the entire list.

“Baek Si-on spent the money on our butts.”

The very first comment had someone’s GIF embedded: IU, standing behind Baek Si-on last night, both hands raised over his head, face in perfect deadpan, bunny ears at full mast. Baek Si-on in front, guitar, oblivious.

Choi Jin-ri watched it with no expression whatsoever.

A few seconds later she turned the phone face-down on the sofa.

Wonderful. So even the butts weren’t safe.

The living room went quiet. The saffron water on the coffee table had stopped steaming. She sat for a while — then lost, flipped the phone back over.

This time she didn’t touch the trending list. She just stared at her chat list.

No new messages at the top. No Baek Si-on.

Made sense. He was flying to the States today. The concert had barely cooled and the Korean cleanup alone was a mountain, and American radio runs waited on the other side of it.

He must be slammed. Too busy for bunny ears, too busy for big screens, too busy to know she was sitting here losing an argument with a trending list.

Baek Si-on locked up entirely.

He had predicted something. Not this.

Choi Jin-ri stepped back half a pace. Her ears had gone red to the tips. She watched his face, heart sprinting like she’d just run four hundred meters.

He said nothing.

One second.

Two seconds.

Three seconds.

Choi Jin-ri began to panic.

He’s not… upset, is he?

Her brain went to full throttle. Too impulsive? Was he composing a polite refusal? Was he already drafting the version where nothing happened? The worse it got, the worse she felt — until she located an exit, fast:

“If…”

Her voice went small.

“If you don’t like it.”

She looked down, fingers twisting together.

“You can wipe it off with a wet wipe… provided you actually carry wet wipes.”

Baek Si-on looked at her.

The corridor lamp put its warm light on her face. Red ears. Bright eyes. The particular stubbornness of a person who is visibly terrified and still insisting she has just said something devastatingly cool.

The AMAs came back to him. Taylor, his right cheek, months ago.

He had wiped that one off with a wet wipe. He had even photographed the used wipe and sent her the picture.

This time.

He didn’t reach for anything.

He just said:

“Didn’t bring one today.”

Choi Jin-ri blinked.

“A wipe?”

“Mm.”

“You… didn’t you say you’d always carry them from now on?”

“Forgot today.”

Choi Jin-ri studied his face.

He said it dead seriously. Seriously enough that she genuinely could not tell whether he had really forgotten — or had simply decided, today, to forget.

But either way.

He hadn’t wiped it.

That fact outweighed any possible answer.

Choi Jin-ri looked down, bit her lip — and smiled.

“Then don’t wipe it.”

Baek Si-on didn’t pick up that thread.

“Take care of it.”

“I will.”

“Call the cattery if anything comes up.”

“I know.”

“And.”

Choi Jin-ri looked up at him.

Baek Si-on paused half a beat.

“Blin… is a decent name.”

Choi Jin-ri stared.

Then the smile came out at full size.

“Then go, you’re leaving!”

She scooped up the carrier and stepped backward into the apartment.

“Planes don’t wait.”

Baek Si-on turned. Two steps. Then looked back.

“Merry Christmas.”

“You too.”

He walked to the elevator. This time, Choi Jin-ri didn’t jam the door.

She stood in the corridor and watched him reach it, press the button, step in.

The doors slid toward each other slowly. Through the last slit of light she could see him standing inside.

Hands in his pockets.

Face carrying nothing at all.

And the spot on his left cheek.

He never touched it. Not once.

…

After he left, Choi Jin-ri carried the carrier inside and let Blin out.

The tiny Sphynx stood on the living room floor, surveyed the new territory with visible suspicion, and then crossed it on thin legs and attached itself to her ankle.

Choi Jin-ri crouched down. Blin pushed its bald little head into her palm.

“You get cold too, huh.”

Blin meowed, softly.

Choi Jin-ri took Baek Si-on’s down coat off the sofa, spread it across her lap, and Blin burrowed straight in.

She looked down at the small, wrinkled, warm life kneading into the coat, and said, quietly:

“Then from now on, the two of us wait for him together.”

Chapter One

The Camo Backpack and the March Wind

March · Seoul Somewhere on the way home The long ride with the woman in the passenger seat

“Get in.”

Baek Si-on pulled open the door and slid into the passenger seat, tossing the camo backpack into the back before the door had even shut. The engine turned over. The car moved.

Outside the window, it was Korea in March — the trees not yet green, the sky a clean, cold blue. Si-on leaned back into the seat and let his knees fall a little open. The cabin was cramped. He was long-legged, and his kneecap pressed into the glove box in a way that was never going to be comfortable.

The woman driving was named Yoon Hye-ja. Mid-forties. Short hair. Eyes like they’d been honed against a whetstone. His mother — this mother, this life’s.

Yoon Hye-ja

Si-on's mother

Mid-40s · steel behind a calm face

Short hair, hard eyes, and a way of putting two billion won on the table because she doesn't want her son eating meatless lunchboxes on set.

Yesterday he’d been in a rented room, running lines. The last thought to flash through his head before his heart gave out had been something trite: that scene tomorrow has no emotion in it.

Then he’d blinked, and he was someone else.

This person is called Baek Si-on. Twenty-two. Just discharged from the military. He used to be an idol — the kind that was so buried in obscurity he didn’t even have a bottom to fall out of. He picked up his discharge papers yesterday; today his mother came to drive him home. That’s it. That’s the whole file.

Baek Si-on

The protagonist · Debt collector, then actor

Korean-American · 22 · reincarnated

Raised between Seoul and Los Angeles, pushed onto the stage by a father who believed the Hallyu wave would carry his son to the top. He spent two years in the Korean military, then woke up carrying the name — and the memory — of a life he never lived.

Si-on cracked the window a sliver. The wind poured in, cold and clean, and it was good. He squinted at the buildings sliding past and thought:

Not bad. This life, at least, has someone waiting with the engine running. The last one never did.

Two traffic lights. Then Hye-ja spoke.

“Your father put you in front of the cameras when you were a kid.”

Si-on waited. There was more.

“He said K-pop was national policy. That the wave would carry you.”

“Mm.”

“He’s gone now.”

Si-on turned his head. Hye-ja didn’t look back. She kept her eyes on the road, expression unmoved.

“Your life is yours to make now. So… what do you want to do?”

Si-on glanced into the rearview mirror. A short crop. A brow that stood out like a ledge. A jaw that cut a clean line. Whatever else could be said about him, this face had been built for a camera — even buried, it was the sort of face the gods hand out to exactly one person in a million.

“He sent me down that road because he wanted me to make something of myself in the industry, I think.”

Hye-ja didn’t answer.

“I’m going to keep trying.”

Her eyebrow moved.

“An idol again?”

Si-on shook his head.

“I want to try acting.”

“Directing?”

“Acting.”

“…”

Hye-ja said nothing more. She just pressed the accelerator, clean and decisive, and cut back in front of a taxi that had been trying to wedge into the lane. It was the kind of move a person makes when they’ve already made up their mind and don’t feel like explaining it.

She pulled up outside their building, put the car in park, killed the engine, and unbuckled. Before she got out she dropped a single word over her shoulder.

“Understood.”

Then she was out the door, bag over her arm, up the stairs, no wasted motion at all.

Si-on sat in the passenger seat for two seconds.

Understood. That’s it?

He laughed, once, quiet, and got out.

The March wind hit him in the face. Still a little cold. He swung the camo backpack onto his shoulder and looked up at his home for this life — an ordinary apartment block, an ordinary floor, an ordinary balcony strung with ordinary laundry.

Fine. Good enough.

He went in.

❧ ❧ ❧

Upstairs was a three-bedroom apartment. The wood floors were polished to a shine. One whole wall of the living room had been turned into bookshelves, packed floor to ceiling. The air carried the faint scent of fabric softener, layered over the savoury smell drifting from the kitchen. Clean. Calm.

Somebody lives here carefully. Like they mean it.

On the chest of drawers beside the hallway sat a frame. A black-and-white photograph.

Baek Jeong-hwan

1968 – 2008

The father of this body. The man in the photo wore thin-rimmed glasses and a mild, bookish smile. He did not look like the sort of man who would have shipped his son off to become an idol.

“Dinner’s ready when you are.”

Hye-ja had changed into slippers and was already at the sink, sleeves rolled. Si-on answered with a small sound and pushed open his own door.

Single bed. Desk. Wardrobe. Not a single poster on the wall. A young man’s room so clean it was almost a statement — the room of someone who had once been an idol and now wanted no trace of it left.

On the desk: a stack of CDs. He went over and pulled one out at random.

A’ST1 · Debut Album

A’ST1

Six half-grown boys with terrible hair, in sequined stage outfits that hurt to look at, striking poses they imagined were cool.

Member list — Korean, Chinese, Japanese, all together. This body’s name was third.

Released · August 2010

Underneath was another disc. A single, from his solo era.

From the kitchen came the hum of the range hood kicking on. Si-on pulled out a chair and sat, slipping the solo single into the old CD player on the desk. He hit play.

The intro hit. A wall of over-produced synth blast out of the speakers, loud enough to hurt. Then, this body’s voice.

“…”

By the time the song ended, Si-on had his answer.

The breath support was solid. The high notes climbed without wavering. The fundamentals were there and they were good. The tragedy was that this was a voice made for something quiet — warm, broken, the kind of thing that could make an audience cry — and someone had forced it to sing noisy electronic dance trash. There was even a nonsense English rap jammed in the middle, the kind that made your toes curl.

Hand this kid a guitar and a quiet room and he could break a thousand hearts. Give him an OST and he’d be unstoppable. No wonder he floundered. Whoever planned his career had water where the brain should be.

Si-on pressed stop and popped the disc out, turning it over. The release date: August 2010. Almost four years ago.

Four years. Long enough to be entirely forgotten.

He was weighing whether to seal this whole pile of black history in a box and bury it somewhere when Hye-ja’s voice came through the door.

“Eat.”

Si-on got up and went out.

The table was set. Three dishes and a soup. Mackerel pan-fried golden on both sides. Japchae slick and glossy. A big plate of fiery stir-fried pork. And a pot of doenjang-jjigae bubbling away. Beside them sat a row of small saucers — kimchi, pickled radish, the usual.

Si-on sat and took the first bite of pork, and it hit him the way only real food can. Sweet-spicy sauce and fat bursting in his mouth, the meat sliced thin and crisped at the edges.

God. This is food. Living, breathing, honest food.

He didn’t speak. He lifted the steaming bowl of rice and went at it in earnest. A mouthful of rice and pork, then a searing sip of soup, then a bite of fish. His movements weren’t rough, but they were fast, both cheeks packed, eating like a man who hadn’t seen a meal in weeks.

Across the table, Hye-ja watched him work for about two minutes.

“Were you starving in the army?”

Si-on nodded over a mouthful of rice and made a vague sound of agreement. Two years of service, and meat was a guest; it was only the occasional leave, out to eat beef intestines, that had kept him from shrinking into a stick. Hye-ja didn’t press. She just picked up a piece of mackerel and laid it in his bowl.

Si-on froze. In the last life, his mother had gone early. Nobody had put food in his bowl in fifteen years.

He looked down and kept eating. He didn’t lift his head.

He finished one bowl. Went back for a second. On the third, Hye-ja set down her chopsticks.

“I called your uncle.”

Si-on looked up.

“He has a script. He’ll bring it by tomorrow.”

He swallowed.

“What kind?”

“I don’t know.”

Hye-ja lifted her soup bowl, took a sip, set it back down, and added, offhand:

“He said it’s an indie film.”

Si-on nodded. He kept eating.

A film. Good. Whatever it is, whatever a mess it might be — if it lands in my hands, it becomes the ladder. Because this is the one thing I know how to do.

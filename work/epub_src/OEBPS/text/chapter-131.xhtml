Chapter One Hundred Thirty One

The Endorsement Fee That Made the Whole Korean Industry Look Up

The morning after the Billboard number one 10:30 a.m. Los Angeles → Beverly Hills On one continent the noise arrives as news. On this one, it arrives as an identity crisis with a soundtrack.

The reaction in America ran on an entirely different channel from Korea.

Korea was in shock.

America was… processing.

More precisely, the mainstream American music business was undergoing one of those extremely rare collective reconfigurations of understanding.

An Asian face had taken an English-language rock anthem and stood it on top of both Baek Si-on’s sharpest rivals — Meghan Trainor and Taylor Swift. No wait. He had stepped over Taylor Swift and Meghan Trainor.

In the context of North American pop, this was not a “dark horse upset.”

This was a “system error.”

Because every weighted component of the Billboard Hot 100 formula leans, by design, toward artists whose first language is English and whose home market is the one the chart measures. Airplay. Sales. Streaming. Each one was built by the American industry, for the American industry.

For a Korean artist to beat Taylor Swift on all three at once was the musical equivalent of a left-hander walking onto the Centre Court at Wimbledon and beating Federer while playing right-handed.

Theoretically, not impossible.

But the moment you saw it happen, your first reaction was the same as everyone’s:

Hold on. Is this… allowed?

✿ ✿ ✿

Ten-thirty in the morning.

Twitter.

Twitter · worldwide · 10:30 a.m.

1 — #Legend Number 1 worldwide

2 — #Baek Si-on

3 — #BillboardHot100

4 — #TomFordGangster

The fourth entry is what gives the whole episode its very American layer of absurdity — because it means that, for a meaningful slice of the American internet, the reason Baek Si-on took the chart is not that he can sing, but that he wore Tom Ford and nearly ran onto an NBA court to fight.

Most people, of course, were still earnestly discussing the music.

Rolling Stone · quick take · 11:00 a.m.

“Baek Si-on is not K-pop. He does not dance. He does not sell cuteness. He does not use visual spectacle to paper over hollow music. He simply stands there — with the confidence and force of an American rock singer — and sings a song about not conceding. And the American listeners, the middle-aged white men raised on the Super Bowl and NBA arenas who should by every rule be the least receptive to an Asian face, stood up. This is not cultural invasion. This is cultural recognition.”

Rolling Stone, eleven in the morning, on the record.

Pitchfork did not publish a full review. They put a short item in the news section, in that tone of theirs — the one that could freeze a soup:

Pitchfork · news

A stadium-rock single, pushed by esports, the NBA and social-media spectacle, tops the Hot 100.

Baek Si-on’s success reads less as a victory of the traditional pop-industrial machinery than as a concentrated eruption of cross-media storytelling inside the chart system itself.

Translated into plain human speech, that sentence meant:

“We haven’t decided whether to praise this yet.”

Billboard published a piece of their own.

The wording from the editorial desk was careful, but the data was laid out in detail:

Billboard · charts desk

“Legend posted strong gains this week in both digital downloads and streaming. Downloads rose 67%, driven primarily by the nationwide broadcast effect of the Lakers’ season opener and the still-spreading global athlete challenge. Meghan Trainor’s All About That Bass — slipping three places to No. 4 amid a plagiarism controversy — vacated the room Legend needed to move up.”

Translated: he got there half on his own legs, and half because the opponent stumbled.

The ESPN reaction was the most interesting of all.

They were not a music outlet.

They were a sports outlet.

And yet, that day, they ran more content about Baek Si-on than about the Lakers’ loss.

A sports channel spending an entire morning discussing a song — that itself was the best possible footnote on how far Legend had broken out of its lane.

ESPN · live · the morning desk

A commentator’s remark during the broadcast — later clipped into a GIF that circled the entire internet.

“This Korean has kidnapped the entire NBA with one song. Right now, in every locker room, before every player does his pre-game ritual, the background music is ‘Won’t stop till we’re legends.’ This is not product placement. This is cultural hijacking. And we are — volunteers.”

The musicians split into two camps.

One camp was genuinely impressed.

Twitter · Adam Levine

@adamlevine · the photo: VIP row one, two men side by side, the Staples Center stands behind them

The One.

That was the entire caption. Two words.

The replies underneath were a solid wall of fire emojis.

Pharrell Williams retweeted Billboard’s official post with a single added line:

Twitter · Pharrell Williams · retweet

@pharrell · on Billboard’s announcement

When the music is this good, nationality is irrelevant.

The Game kept pushing the nickname he had helped invent.

Twitter · The Game

@thegame · 10:41 a.m.

Tom Ford Gangster tops the chart.

Under it, a queue of rappers lined up to comment.

The Game · the replies · a queue forms

@west_coast

Heroes recognise heroes.

@loyalty_card

This kid knows how to ride for his people.

@mob_deep

That live-or-die presence. You can’t fake it.

@studio_time

I genuinely want a verse with this man.

Bruno Mars said nothing publicly.

But his producer said one thing in a private industry group chat, and someone screenshot it out into the world:

private group chat · the leak

Producer: “If this Korean keeps this level on his next album, we are all in trouble.”

The other camp was sour.

Not publicly sour. The quiet kind — the kind that never makes it into print, but circulates in LA recording studios and managers’ offices like low-grade flu:

“His songs are fine. But isn’t it really Kobe and LeBron carrying him? If there were no Kobe in the video, if the athlete challenge had no LeBron — could he have gotten to number one himself?”

Nobody dared say that out loud in public.

Because saying it out loud meant admitting the following:

You think your song is better than “Legend.”

Then go find Kobe to star in your music video.

You can’t? Then your song isn’t good enough.

Because if your song were good enough, Kobe would come to you.

The logic loop was too perfect to argue with.

Perfect enough that the sour people could only be sour in private.

In public, everyone was congratulating him.

✿ ✿ ✿

And somewhere between all the public congratulations, the private sourness, the media analysis, and ESPN’s “cultural hijacking” soundbite —

the man being discussed was in a French restaurant in Beverly Hills. Nothing flashy about the building. But every dish on the menu cost like it had been seasoned with gold dust.

And he was currently being hugged by Kevin Plank.

“Wow — would you look at who’s here!”

“Our new Billboard champion! The coolest Tom Ford Gangster in America!”

Baek Si-on:

“……”

At this point he was growing numb to that nickname.

Scooter Braun, by contrast, was laughing like he owned the joke.

“Kevin. You got here faster than I expected.”

“Of course.”

Kevin Plank stepped back from Baek Si-on and exchanged a second hug with Scooter:

“If I didn’t move fast, the Nike people would have moved faster.”

Kevin had not, of course, chartered a plane and flown down from Baltimore on the spot.

He had already been in Los Angeles for days.

Under Armour’s West Coast team had a round of internal meetings lined up: the next phase of the Curry basketball line, the expansion of the women’s training category, and the question of how the brand moved from “hardcore performance gear” into a broader set of lifestyle scenes.

Then Baek Si-on’s court-side clip detonated.

The meeting topic changed on the spot.

It went from “how do we become cool” to “why is that Korean so cool, and can we get him into UA?”

That was the speed of commerce sometimes. Faster than a hater changing his tune.

Before the appetisers arrived, Kevin Plank spent five minutes on small talk. Then the real subject landed.

“Baek — I can call you that?”

“Yes.”

Kevin Plank leaned back in his chair.

“I’ll say it straight. Ten million dollars. Three years. Global endorsement.”

“You wear our clothes. You appear in our ads. You take part in our global marketing campaigns.”

If Baek Eun-ah had been in the room, she would probably have bitten her straw clean in half at that point.

Unfortunately, Scooter Braun had her parked back at SB Projects, processing the album pre-orders, the bonuses, and the S.W Studio payment entity.

So the table held only Baek Si-on, Scooter, Kevin Plank, and both sides’ legal and brand people.

Baek Si-on said nothing.

He looked at Scooter instead.

Leave the professional stuff to the professional. He trusted Scooter to negotiate a better number.

And sure enough, Scooter spoke first:

“Kevin. That’s a handsome price.”

Kevin Plank smiled.

“I know.”

“But it isn’t enough sincerity.”

Kevin Plank:

“?”

For a very brief moment, his face went blank.

Ten million dollars.

Three years.

That number sat slightly below Curry — and above every other athlete Under Armour had under contract.

On an Asian artist, it was a tier-zero offer, the kind of money that would stand alone in the history books.

If that was not “sincerity,” what was?

“That’s an athlete’s endorsement price.”

Scooter pointed at Baek Si-on.

“But he is not an athlete. He is not a rapper. He is not a traditional K-pop act.”

“He is an Asian face who just made middle-aged white American men, NBA fans, esports players, the hip-hop world and the fashion press all take an interest at the same time.”

“There is no template for that in the market.”

“No template means no old price.”

Kevin Plank listened, and tapped two fingers on the tabletop.

Then he folded both hands on the table.

“Say your piece.”

That was the sentence Scooter had been waiting for.

“On top of the ten million over three years — open a separate sports-fashion line.”

Kevin Plank raised an eyebrow.

“A sports-fashion line?”

“Yes.”

Scooter raised a finger, indicating Baek Si-on.

“Baek does not just wear your clothes. He takes part in defining the line’s image, its concept launches, its global advertising, and part of its product design.”

Baek Si-on turned his head to look at him.

His eyes were calm. His meaning was not:

I cannot design.

Scooter received the message, apparently, and added smoothly:

“Of course — if he is too busy, he can simply supply the core inspiration and serve as the name-line’s creative director in title.”

Baek Si-on looked away again.

Mm. In title only. That worked.

Kevin Plank nodded.

“And then?”

Scooter showed no embarrassment whatsoever.

“And — five percent of that line’s sales.”

Kevin Plank laughed.

“Scooter.”

He shook his head.

“Were you people raised, from childhood, to multiply the other party’s psychological ceiling by three every time you open a negotiation?”

Scooter was not offended in the slightest. His smile only widened.

“Kevin. In my culture we call that ‘a precise awareness of one’s own value.’”

“In mine we call it greed.”

“Then your culture simply hasn’t had time to appreciate mine.”

“This offer of yours is insane.”

“Insane is exactly what makes it reasonable.”

Scooter spread his hands.

“Kevin. Signing Curry was a well-played move. He is a killer on the court. The three-pointer changed how basketball is played. This season the Warriors have a real chance to take the whole thing.”

Kevin Plank nodded.

Curry was the biggest card Under Armour held.

“But here’s the problem—”

Scooter paused.

“Your shoes and your clothes only get worn inside gyms and basketball arenas.”

“Nobody walks into a nightclub wearing UA.”

“Nobody walks the Grammy carpet wearing UA.”

“Nobody goes on a date with a Victoria’s Secret angel wearing UA.”

Baek Si-on glanced at him.

The sentence sounded an awful lot like a dig at Adam Levine — the man who had just swapped numbers with Baek Si-on the night before last.

Scooter did not care. He kept going.

“Your product is extremely functional. The compression wear genuinely outperforms Nike and Adidas. Your training line has a great reputation. But ‘works well’ is not a premium. ‘Cool’ is a premium.”

“And you — are not cool.”

Kevin Plank’s smile faded by a degree.

The words stung.

Stinging did not make them wrong.

Under Armour had grown fast for years on professional performance gear, training scenes, hardcore masculine energy, and a superstar like Curry who could write a whole new story on the court.

But that was exactly the problem.

It looked too much like “equipment.”

Not enough like “style.”

Nike could be worn into the street. Adidas could be worn into a music festival. Jordan could be worn into a nightclub. And UA was the thing you wore when you were serious about your workout.

Practical.

But not sexy.

Scooter looked at Kevin Plank.

“Baek can make the pickiest rich kids in Los Angeles think that a UA hoodie with a Rolex is a sexy look.”

“That is something Curry cannot do.”

“Not because he isn’t good enough — because he is too good. So good that everything he wears looks like his mother picked it out.”

Baek Si-on:

“……”

That was a little mean.

It was also, unfortunately, true.

Curry’s image was clean. Safe. Family. The kind of image that sells basketball shoes beautifully. It sells youth camps. It sells “even a mother feels good about her kid wearing it” products.

But safety was not cool.

Cool required a little danger.

A little uncertainty.

Baek Si-on happened to carry exactly that.

Kevin Plank understood this perfectly well. It was, after all, why he was sitting at this table today.

“The price is too high. You are asking for almost Curry-level treatment.”

Scooter smiled.

“I did not ask for equity.”

Kevin Plank:

“……”

That sentence made him want to hit Scooter even more.

Scooter continued:

“And I am not asking out of thin air.”

He looked at Baek Si-on.

“Consider Hyundai. The model Baek endorses — honestly, it is not a cool car.”

Baek Si-on finally spoke up.

“I still endorse it.”

Scooter amended himself smoothly:

“The product positioning is… mature.”

Baek Si-on:

“……”

Kevin Plank laughed.

Scooter pressed on:

“But Baek made it cool.”

“Look at Tom Ford. It was only a piece of haute-couture suiting. And now? Tom Ford has broken from the fashion world into sport, hip-hop, even esports.”

“He can drag a thing out of its original circle and drop it into a completely unrelated cultural context.”

“That is Baek’s ability.”

Scooter paused.

“Of course — those are all track records. Track records are the past. What you want is the future. So let me give you a promise.”

Kevin Plank looked at him.

“Within three years—”

Scooter said it word by word:

“—Baek’s line will surpass the market share of Adidas’ equivalent line in the sports-lifestyle category.”

Kevin Plank raised an eyebrow.

A performance clause.

Interesting.

“And if it doesn’t?”

“If it doesn’t — no sales share.”

Kevin Plank asked quickly:

“None at all?”

“None at all.”

Scooter answered just as fast:

“But the base endorsement fee stays. That fee buys Baek’s global usage rights. The sales share is the bonus for opening a new market for you.”

Kevin Plank’s fingers tapped twice, slowly, on the table.

He was calculating.

The core logic of the performance clause was risk transfer.

If Scooter’s judgement was right, Under Armour traded ten million in endorsement money plus five percent to buy the biggest brand breakthrough in its history.

If Scooter’s judgement was wrong, Under Armour spent ten million, and the damage was contained.

Either way, he could not lose.

The only question left was —

“Five percent is too much.”

Kevin Plank said.

“Curry only gets five percent.”

The subtext sat under the sentence like a foundation:

Curry is Under Armour’s absolute core — the basketball face the company bet its whole future on, the cornerstone of the brand strategy.

He gets five percent.

And Baek Si-on also gets five percent?

A Korean singer who just arrived, with no track record on any product line yet, taking the same share as the man about to change basketball history?

That was not reasonable.

“Kevin. Then have Curry wear a UA hoodie to a talk show. Put him on the Grammy red carpet. Put him on a Vogue cover. And let us see how it goes.”

“But until that result is in, I cannot guarantee Baek will not go to Nike.”

Kevin Plank’s eyes sank a little.

That was the knife.

Nike was too mature. Too dangerous.

If Baek Si-on went to Nike — even on an ordinary two-million-dollar cooperation deal — Under Armour would lose a potential new gateway into the lifestyle market that could tear it open for them.

Worse.

Nike did not necessarily need Baek Si-on.

But Under Armour did.

That asymmetry decided who at this table was in a hurry.

Kevin Plank was silent for several seconds.

Then he smiled.

“You really know how to rob people.”

Scooter smiled back.

“I prefer ‘create value.’”

“Four percent.”

“Done.”

Kevin Plank:

“……”

He pointed at Scooter for a full three seconds.

Shook his head.

Only then did he turn to Baek Si-on and extend his hand.

“Baek. Welcome to Under Armour.”

Baek Si-on shook it.

“Thank you.”

Kevin Plank waited a beat.

“That’s it?”

Baek Si-on thought about it.

“When does the food arrive?”

Kevin Plank:

“……”

How did a person get to look like that?

Next page. Balcony. Jacket over the shoulder. White shirt two buttons open. Wind taking a corner of cloth. He clicked his tongue. Same country. Same two eyes, one nose, one mouth. Where did the gap come from. Genes. Lights. The photographer. Or a previous life spent saving a galaxy.

He looked at the picture for five seconds. Then he did the thing every man has done and no man will admit. Phone. Front camera. His own face: bangs a little fallen, a hint of under-eye, features his mother had once called even. He held the phone at the magazine’s angle. Window pose. Side face. Eyes past the lens. Chin up. No smile. Click. Comparison. The gap was not a little.

Second try. The balcony. He had no jacket. He pulled a thin work jacket off the chair-back, hung it on the left shoulder. Side. Jaw tight. Eyes as cold, as far, as deep as a convenience-store aisle would allow. Click. Worse. Si-on wearing a jacket looked like a European board had just ended and a man had gone to a Venice rail to take air. Han-teuk wearing a jacket looked like a site had just closed and a man had slung the uniform to go eat malatang.

He stared at the two pictures until the silence became a kind of philosophy. Third: the black-and-white close face. No prop. No jacket. A face and a correct light. He found an angle he believed in, turned left, tucked the chin, dropped the eyes, made a line of the mouth. Focus.

“Oppa, what are you doing?”

Ji-eun, two metres behind him. The finger jumped. Click. Not the picture he wanted. The picture of a man leaving cold-and-deep and arriving caught-by-the-boss: eyes round, mouth a little open, chin tucked so far it made a second chin.

The phone went into the pocket at a speed that should have been studied.

“Nothing.”

“I saw.”

She came around and stood in front of him. The height meant she had to look up. The height had never once decided who ran the air. He cleared his throat, took a box of chocolate from the other pocket.

“Here. You looked tired.”

She took it. Wrap. Box. One piece. Two chews. The free hand went left, very naturally, and took the Vogue Korea. Then the mouth did a small thing. In the pictures he had not arranged to look handsome. Because he had not arranged it, anyone arriving to pick a fight looked cheap. You could not say too much. He had not done too much. You could not say only this. The eye would not listen. It stopped on the page for two extra seconds.

She swallowed.

“Why is your chocolate this bitter?”

He looked at the sleeve. Sugar forty-seven percent. Bitter. Where was it supposed to be bitter. He opened his mouth. She was already not looking at him. She turned pages. Pictures ended. Interview.

She had not meant to read it. Then Way Back Home caught the edge of the eye and the eye went back to the start of the paragraph. Venice. Method. The double job of actor and singer. Then the last block.

Vogue Italia · October · picked up in Vogue Korea

Way Back Home is the first song of yours to find ears in Europe and America. What does it mean to you?

It told me a song gets heard not only because of a melody. Someone has to put a real feeling into the words.

She looked at the answer a long time. It sounded ordinary. Ordinary enough that any singer could have said it into a recorder. She tasted thanks in it anyway. Who was the someone. She was. She had written those words. Maybe he had not been thanking anyone. Maybe he had been saying a fact. She decided to hear it the first way.

She closed the book. Looked at Han-teuk.

“A grown man, reading Vogue.”

Three questions lined up in his mouth — could a man not learn how to wear clothes, could a man not look at another man’s eye, was buying a friend’s international issue a crime — and she put the magazine under her arm and walked back toward the tunnel.

“Confiscated.”

₩22,000. His. CU. His card. The hurt, and then the buy.

The voice was already coming from behind the entrance drape. He stood. Sighed. A manager was an artist’s asset. A manager’s things were, therefore, also. He sat again. Took the phone out. Permanently deleted the caught-in-transition self-portrait.

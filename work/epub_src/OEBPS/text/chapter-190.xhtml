Chapter One Hundred Ninety

Since When Does Car Talk End in Bed?

Late February · Seoul, a training base for the end of the world Zombie boot camp rules · a reverse-opening door announcing the ordinary world’s temporary exit · a safekeeping arrangement nobody buys · one sentence said into a phone before she could stop it · a translation too accurate to rebut · a brick doctrine · and a realist road picture at golden hour The padded training room → the gate → the parking area → the open road An actress learns to fall properly, a seven-in-the-world car learns to be parked, an unni proves that love is real but never blind, and several extras learn that hierarchy cuts both directions.

Pre-production on Train to Busan was even less like a film set than Choi Jin-ri had imagined. Nothing about it flattered an actor’s dignity. There was only a training room with padded floors, a cohort of actors learning how to fall, flee, scream, and be tackled without actually being injured — and a row of extras rehearsing what the call sheet called “zombie posture.”

Director Yeon Sang-ho’s requirements for the infected were specific. Joints displaced. A neck folding suddenly the wrong way. A body that moved like something had snipped its strings from the inside.

❝

Don’t act like ghosts. Don’t act like drunks. You are the infected — not the three-a.m. crowd on Hongdae street.

— zombie boot camp, day one, on the difference

Goo Hara, sitting in the corner of the training room, very nearly sprayed her first mouthful of iced americano across three extras.

She had come today to accompany Choi Jin-ri. “Accompany,” in this case, meaning compensate: KARA’s Japan run had eaten the last stretch of months, and by the time she finally surfaced, her little sister had jumped straight from million-admissions lead of Green Bottle Fly into a zombie-film training base. As the close unni, she considered it her duty to verify with her own eyes whether the film industry had scammed the girl into serving as a human sandbag.

After half an hour of observation she reached her conclusion. Not scammed. Korean film people’s mental state simply wasn’t much healthier than an idol company’s.

Choi Jin-ri was being walked through it by the action instructor: up off the ground, two steps, a look back at the “zombies” behind her. She was practicing seriously. So seriously that Goo Hara’s smile thinned a little as she watched.

Jin-ri had always worked hard. It was just that, for years, the direction of the effort hadn’t been “so I get better” — it had been “so they stop hating me.” This was different. She genuinely wanted to get a character right. Even a character in a school uniform, running ragged down a train corridor, screaming at the infected, was closer to being alive than the years of being torn apart for phoning in choreography under stage lights.

And then — a low roar outside the training base. Half the room turned its head on instinct. When the second roar came, even the action instructor stopped mid-demonstration.

“What is that sound?”

someone whispered. A few staff members went out to look. Within seconds, the exclamations drifting back in could no longer be suppressed. Goo Hara rose, strolled to the window, took one look — and locked up entirely.

“Choi Jin-ri.”

“What?”

“You need to come see this.”

Choi Jin-ri walked over, entirely baffled. Parked at the gate of the training base was a red hypercar, and beside the few ordinary vehicles nearby it created a contrast so brutal it should have required a permit. She squinted at it for two seconds — and then saw who was stepping out of the driver’s seat. Black suit. White gloves. She knew him. Baek Si-on’s driver. Something in her chest dropped one full floor. Goo Hara was already turning to her, eyes fully loaded with premonition.

“You know him?”

“……Mm.”

“The car?”

“No idea about the car.”

The driver came through the crowd, walked up to Choi Jin-ri, and bowed to standard depth.

“Miss Choi Jin-ri.”

“Ne…”

Her bow in return came out slightly glitched. With both hands, the driver presented a black document pouch and a car key.

“Mr. Baek Si-on asked me to deliver the car.”

The gate of the training base went silent for one instant. And after the silence came whispering, denser than before. Choi Jin-ri didn’t take it. She looked at the key. Looked at the car. Looked at the driver.

“Are you sure you haven’t delivered to the wrong address?”

The driver was very calm.

“I haven’t.”

“But—”

“Mr. Baek says that if you have questions, you may call him.”

The sentence was extremely effective. Choi Jin-ri at once carried her phone a few steps away. Goo Hara started after her, made it two steps, and stopped — she did, in the end, have boundaries. She stood where she was and stretched her neck instead, openly. This is called caring about your little sister. It is not called eavesdropping.

Why does it have to be me?

This question he had, in fact, solved back in the Middle East, by elimination. Why her? Because Yoon Hye-ja was a professor at Ewha Womans University. If his mother drove a globally limited Lykan into a university parking lot, the sociology department could open a brand-new course by the next morning. The course title would be The Class Betrayal of the University Professor in the Neoliberal Era.

As for Baek Jeong-hoon — even less viable. The man still maintained a Korean indie director’s proper creative state through poverty, anger, cigarette smoke, instant-noodle buckets in the edit suite, and a low-grade disdain for chaebols. If that car actually rolled into his garage, Korean independent cinema might lose a hardship-narrative auteur on the spot.

Baek Eun-ah — also no. She would dare to drive it, certainly. The problem was that her reverse parking still competed, to this day, at bumper-car finals level.

He ran the full elimination once more and found that the only person equipped to catch this car was Choi Jin-ri.

The old Choi Jin-ri didn’t say this kind of thing. At least not this directly. But after the aurora, some sentences had quietly lost their difficulty. Especially over a phone. When I’m done here, I’ll come back.

She hung up on him, furiously. Then she stared at the phone screen for two seconds, and the corner of her mouth refused every order to come down.

Goo Hara had already sidled over.

Choi Jin-ri stuffed the phone into her pocket and pretended not to have heard. She walked back and accepted the key and the pouch from the driver with both hands.

Goo Hara circled the car once, raised a hand to touch it, and stopped mid-air.

“Can I touch it?”

The driver answered at once, with courtesy:

“You may.”

“Jin-ri-ah.”

“What?”

Goo Hara cleared her throat and reproduced, with precision, his usual flatline tone:

I’m very busy, I can’t come back to Korea, I want to make you happy, and I’m afraid of burdening you — so I have packaged a gift as a safekeeping service.

Accurate. Accurate beyond appeal.

The crowd at the gate kept growing. Drawn out by the noise, director Yeon Sang-ho emerged as well — and behind his glasses, his eyes audibly paused for two full seconds on the red Lykan. But a director was a director.

“Jin-ri-ssi.”

“Ne, director-nim.”

Choi Jin-ri turned at once. Yeon Sang-ho glanced at the key in her hand and asked exactly one question:

“Good.”

What cars actors received in private did not interest him. As long as training wasn’t delayed, the apocalypse could wait until after work.

And so the Lykan was, for the time being, driven off by the driver to a safe parking area outside the base.

The red Lykan, doing absolutely nothing at all, still managed to make every car around it look freshly hauled off a used-car lot. The filthiest mechanism in the industry was exactly this: so many people know perfectly well how filthy their words are, and simply assume the target will swallow — because idols have images to keep. Because women have decorum to keep. Because seniors have magnanimity to perform. Because an actress out of an idol group, above all, must learn to be deaf.

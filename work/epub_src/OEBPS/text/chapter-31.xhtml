Chapter Thirty-One

The Venice Countdown and IU's Lyrics

June 19 Noon Outside LOEN A detour before going home

It was nearly noon when Si-on walked out of LOEN.

The sun hung straight overhead, white and flat, the kind of noon that made you squint and walk with your head down. He stood at the foot of the building, looked at his phone, thought for a moment, and decided against going home.

He flagged down a cab and had it swing past the Bon Juk in Apgujeong first.

One bowl of abalone congee, one bowl of beef congee, an order of seafood pancake, and a samgyetang, all packed to go. The clerk bagged it in three layers, the way a person who'd done this a hundred times bagged food for a person who wouldn't stop to eat it.

June 19 Noon Baek Jeong-hun's studio The shut window, the blue screens

When Si-on got to his uncle's studio, he let himself in with the spare key.

The curtains were drawn. All of them, tight.

Editing and colour-grading hated light nearly as much as they hated interruption, so Baek Jeong-hun had sealed that south-facing window the day he started the rough cut. At midday the room might as well have been midnight. The only light left was the two monitors on the cutting desk, their cold blue glow laying a wash over the side of his uncle's face like an ungraded negative.

Jeong-hun sat in the swivel chair, headphones around his neck, right hand on the mouse, left hand cradling his temple. His eyes were fixed on the timeline, frame by frame, but the focus had already drifted somewhere behind it.

On the desk: three stacked ramen cups, an empty coffee cup, a water bottle with no cap. In the ashtray, seven or eight crushed butts, the topmost one still rising a thin, stubborn thread of smoke.

Si-on set the bags down and, first thing, stubbed out the one still smouldering.

Then he pulled up a chair, sat beside his uncle, opened the abalone congee, slid the spoon in, and nudged the tub to where it was in reach.

“Uncle.”

Jeong-hun's eyes came off the screen, and it took about two seconds for them to find focus.

“Si-on? What are you doing here?”

“Came to check on you.”

Jeong-hun looked down at the tub, picked up the spoon, and took a mouthful of the abalone congee.

The abalone, sliced thin and dissolved into the rice, ran warm and salty from the root of the tongue all the way down to the pit of the stomach. Si-on cut the seafood pancake and laid it on the tub's lid like a plate while his uncle ate, and put the samgyetang where it sat within easy reach.

Then he turned his head to the monitor.

The timeline stretched long and dense, cut points packed in like a row of broken teeth. The playhead sat about two-thirds of the way along, cursor blinking, waiting on a decision that kept not coming.

“How far's the rough cut?”

“Two-thirds.”

Jeong-hun spoke around a mouthful of congee, the words a little indistinct.

“Venice's submission deadline is July first. Rough cut, subtitles, compression — the latest I can mail it is the twenty-eighth.”

Si-on did the math.

Today was June nineteenth.

Nine days.

“Is it doable?”

Jeong-hun didn't answer. He took another mouthful of congee.

That silence was the answer. Whether it was doable or not, it had to be done — a deadline doesn't push back a day just because you didn't sleep.

Si-on stood and came around behind his uncle, laid both hands on his shoulders, pressed his thumbs into the trapezius, and worked downward slowly.

It didn't take long.

Jeong-hun finished the whole tub. When he set the spoon down he tipped his head back, resting the back of it against Si-on's stomach, eyes closed, and let out a slow, long breath.

“Si-on-ah.”

“Mm.”

“If this film doesn't get into Venice, I can face anyone except your mother. Two hundred million won's been sunk into it.”

Si-on didn't stop working his shoulders.

“It'll get in.”

“How do you know?”

“Because you're Baek Jeong-hun.”

“……”

After a few seconds, he opened his eyes, sat up straight, put the headphones back on, and laid his hand on the mouse.

The playhead started moving again.

Si-on set the beef congee and the samgyetang at the far corner of the desk where they were in reach, gathered the empty tub, and threw the three ramen cups in the bin. At the door he looked back once.

Jeong-hun's side profile had gone blue again in the monitor light, but his spine was a little straighter than before, his shoulders a little looser.

June Baek Jeong-hun's studio Support staff

For the next few days, Si-on didn't go back to Hapjeong-dong.

Jae-joon's arrangement work needed time; the demo was already tracked, and what was left was the producer's side of the table. Si-on standing around in the studio would only get in the way.

He moved himself and Eun-ah into Baek Jeong-hun's studio.

Not to help with the edit. He couldn't touch the edit, and Eun-ah least of all — two people sitting in front of a cutting desk would only give Jeong-hun two more obstacles to politely work around.

They came as support staff.

June 20 8:00 a.m. Baek Jeong-hun's studio The rough-cut tally begins

June twentieth. Si-on arrived at eight in the morning. Opened the door, set down the congee, stubbed out the cigarette, worked the shoulders. Jeong-hun looked up from the timeline, took three spoonfuls, said “First act's cut,” and went back down.

Eun-ah arrived at noon. She brought a change of clothes and a blanket, swept the pile of papers off the sofa onto the floor, laid the blanket out, and forcibly peeled her father off the chair and onto the couch for forty minutes. Jeong-hun kept his eyes closed and insisted he wasn't asleep. Eun-ah said he'd been snoring.

June twenty-first. Si-on switched the order from abalone congee to pumpkin congee, because his uncle had said his stomach was riding a little high. The ashtray's butts went from seven or eight down to four. Not because Jeong-hun had cut back on his own — because Eun-ah had hidden the pack and left only four on the desk each day. He'd turned out all three drawers looking for it, sworn under his breath, and gone back to cutting.

June twenty-second. The playhead pushed to three-quarters. Jeong-hun stared at a single cut point for forty minutes, dragging it back and forth, and finally deleted it. Si-on remembered that shot. It was the Han River scene, the one where Sang-hun lay crying on Yeon-hui's lap. What he cut wasn't the crying — it was the empty shot before it. A moon's reflection on the water, holding for two seconds. Lovely. But where it sat, it made the rhythm go soft.

June twenty-third. Eun-ah brought in a potted pothos and set it on the windowsill. Jeong-hun said the curtains were shut, so what was the difference between a living plant and a plastic one in here. Eun-ah said a living thing nearby changed the energy of a room. Jeong-hun said that's exactly what your mother used to say. Eun-ah said thank you.

June twenty-fourth. The playhead reached four-fifths. Jeong-hun's posture started to tip forward; his state was improving. Not because of the abalone congee, not because of the pothos. Because, as the material slotted together block by block on the timeline, he could finally see what this film was.

June 25 3:00 p.m. Baek Jeong-hun's studio The lyrics arrive by KakaoTalk

June twenty-fifth, three in the afternoon. Si-on was leaning back on the sofa with his eyes closed. Eun-ah had gone out to run an errand. Jeong-hun, headphones on, was cutting the last scene.

His phone buzzed.

He opened his eyes and picked it up.

KakaoTalk.

A work account, no name saved.

He opened it.

The last message was from six days ago — the demo of the melody he'd sent over. Since then, two people hadn't exchanged a single word. The thread sat there clean and empty, like two people walking the same road who chose not to greet each other.

The new message was one thing.

The lyrics, pasted straight in.

Si-on opened it.

The lines unrolled across the screen, one by one.

Si-on stared at the screen.

Somewhere behind him, the noise of the edit rolled on, and a city that had no idea it was about to become a song kept its lights on for the ones who didn't come home.

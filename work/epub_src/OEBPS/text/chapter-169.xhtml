Seven Wolves: A Symbol of Patriarchal Authority

December 20 · wheels-down to past eleven at night One jet-lag injury claim · one hamburger embargo lifted · one belt, brand-unverified · one warning issued by a nervous man Los Angeles · LAX → a car → KIIS FM → a corridor with cat pictures → a hotel room phone A country turns out to have weather that favors him, a radio host discovers the cushion quote translates, a superstar shares cat logistics, and a manager tries to fence off a singer with a mountain range made of other people’s breakups.

December 20. Los Angeles International Airport.

The cabin door opened and the first thing that reached Baek Si-on was the temperature difference.

Seoul had spent the week sinking below zero at night — Jamsil’s wind had a documented specialty in finding sleeves. Los Angeles in December came back with sunlight bright enough to wash the color out of your eyes and twenty-some degrees of air, as if the planet itself had been caught playing favorites.

Baek Eun-ah trailed behind with two suitcases, yawning between steps.

“Cousin.”

“Mm.”

“I think jet lag should be legally classified as a workplace injury.”

Park Ji-hun rubbed his eyes beside her.

“Seconded.”

The two bodyguards said nothing. They flanked Baek Si-on, one left, one right, professional to the degree of being two moving walls.

Baek Si-on tipped his head back at the sky, eyes narrowed against it.

“Have Finance log it.”

Baek Eun-ah came awake slightly.

“Really?”

“Logging it doesn’t mean it gets paid.”

“……”

Good. The cousin remained the cousin.

At customs, the officer read the passport, then looked up and gave Baek Si-on a second look. Recognition, apparently — the manner warmed several degrees.

“Welcome back, Mr. Baek. Wishing you a smooth trip.”

Baek Si-on inclined his head.

“Thank you.”

Stamp.

Baggage came quickly. The party pushed carts toward arrivals.

Outside the hall, a black Escalade waited at the curb. Scooter leaned against it, sunglasses up his nose, a coffee cup in hand — dressed, to the untrained eye, not as a manager but as a wealthy man conducting a routine inspection of the vineyard grounds surrounding his Malibu estate.

“White!”

They hugged. Scooter clapped his back.

“I expected you to look more tired.”

“I expected you to look more like someone who works.”

Scooter looked down at his own old-money situation and shrugged.

“This IS how America works.”

“……”

Everyone got in. LA sunshine poured through the windows the whole way — palm trees, wide roads, low buildings, the occasional billboard sliding past.

The whole assembly reads as money that has never once needed to raise its voice. Sunglasses deployed on the nose like a man who owns the sun’s reception schedule.
The staging Lean angle against a black Escalade, one hand around an iced coffee with a green straw. The pose communicates: I did not come to the airport; the airport happened near me. As management doctrine, questionable. As posture, immaculate.

This is how America works, allegedly.

Baek Si-on’s verdict was two sentences long and neither of them was a compliment. The coffee, notably, stayed in the shot the whole time.

Scooter took a pull of the coffee and got to business.

“Bad news — ‘Legend’ slipped to five.”

“Good news — ‘Love Yourself’ climbed from twenty-two to eighteen.”

“Better news — the full ‘Love Yourself’ MV isn’t even out yet. Everything so far is audio streaming. There’s a lot of headroom.”

“Worse news — you’re on radio this afternoon.”

Baek Eun-ah sat upright instantly.

“We JUST landed.”

Scooter said:

“Billboard doesn’t pause the counting because you just landed.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

She knew. She had known before asking.

“What time?” Baek Si-on said.

“KIIS FM. Two p.m.”

“What else?”

“A Time team is shadowing you for a while. They’re building a feature on Asian pop-culture export. You’re one of the cover candidates.”

Then he added:

“One of. But basically you.”

The name Time carries real weight for an Asian artist. Rain had been in it. PSY had been in it. But both of those, to some degree, had come dressed as a phenomenon being observed from outside — the Hallyu as a foreign weather event.

This feature’s working title was different: Asian pop-culture export.

The weight sat on the second half. Export. It meant the American press had stopped asking what came out of that region and started asking why does it travel.

Scooter watched Baek Si-on’s face while he laid out the rest:

“Three reporters, half a month, everything — radio, shows, daily life.”

“Don’t perform for them. Walk your normal pace.”

“One condition. No swearing.”

Baek Si-on looked at him:

“When have I ever sworn?”

“Precaution.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Understood.”

Scooter, having received agreement this clean, grew suspicious of it instead.

“Do you actually understand?”

“Mm.”

Baek Si-on looked out at the palm trees going by one after another and asked:

“Is there time to eat?”

Scooter checked the schedule on his phone.

“Not eating-time specifically.”

Baek Eun-ah looked up.

“He hasn’t had a proper meal since last night.”

Scooter amended course instantly:

“Are burgers okay? We buy on the way, you eat in the car.”

“Okay.”

Two p.m. KIIS FM, Los Angeles.

Baek Si-on walked into the studio and found a middle-aged man in a baseball cap sitting across the desk.

Ryan Seacrest.

One of the most famous radio hosts in America — a man whose position in the culture runs roughly parallel to Yoo Jae-suk’s in Korean variety. The kind of name ordinary listeners may not tune in for daily, but which they could not avoid knowing.

Ryan stood to shake hands.

“Welcome to the show.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Thanks for having me.”

Ryan grinned at him.

“I hear you ate three hamburgers on the way here?”

Baek Si-on sat down.

“Mm.”

Ryan swung toward the production glass immediately.

“Ladies and gentlemen — confirmed. He admits it.”

Somebody behind the glass laughed out loud.

Scooter, for his part, was deeply satisfied. The one thing American radio fears is a guest without bits. And their guest had landed in the country, walked to a car, and handed the show a hamburger cold-open.

The countdown ended. The ON AIR light came up. Ryan set his headphones and opened:

KIIS FM · live · 2:00 p.m. · the opening monologue

“Welcome back to the show. Today’s guest is a very special one. Less than forty-eight hours ago he was playing a stadium for sixty thousand people in Korea. Now he’s sitting across from me. He has a Billboard number-one single, a Best Actor trophy from one of Europe’s three great festivals — and one new title I don’t think anyone will ever take from him.”

A pause. Then: “He is the first man in Korean history to trend nationwide for caring about his fans’ butts.”

The producer behind the glass folded in half.

Baek Si-on looked at him.

Ryan spread his hands.

“Don’t look at me like that. It’s what your Korean netizens said.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Accurately translated.”

Ryan stalled a beat — then laughed twice as hard.

“See, I expected you to deny it.”

“Why would I deny it?”

“Because it doesn’t sound like normal PR.”

Baek Si-on said:

“The cushions are normal.”

Ryan smacked the desk.

“There it is, folks — first headline of the day: Baek Si-on responds to butt-trending scandal, insists ‘the cushions are normal.’”

In the corner, Baek Eun-ah closed her eyes. She could already write the Korean headlines herself, in three major outlets’ house styles.

Scooter’s expression had gone delicately complicated. He was suddenly sure the Time reporters had arrived too early — the first paragraph of the Asian pop-culture export feature was on track to become a history of cushion diplomacy.

Ryan kept going:

“Seriously, though. Why did you prepare all of it? The light sticks, the hand warmers, the hot drinks, the cushions, the buses to take out-of-town fans home. It doesn’t feel like a concert. It feels like a municipal works project.”

Baek Si-on said:

“My fans buy the albums. They stream the songs, they watch the films, they support the work.”

“The reason I could stage that concert at all is that support.”

“So none of it is an extra handout.”

“It came from them. It went back to them.”

Ryan nodded.

“That’s a beautiful answer.”

Half a second, then the kicker:

“And still classier than ‘because the chairs were hard.’”

Baek Si-on said:

“The chairs were also hard.”

Ryan cracked up.

“You see, this is why you’re dangerous. You say one sentence that belongs in a magazine feature, and the very next sentence you drag the whole thing back to butts.”

The room loosened up completely.

Ryan didn’t let the cushion topic outstay its welcome. Music, next.

“Here’s an interesting problem you have. America met you through ‘Legend’. That song is stadiums, basketball, boxing, training, adrenaline.”

“‘Love Yourself’ is the opposite direction entirely. Are you worried American listeners won’t take the switch?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“If they only want ‘Legend’, they can keep playing ‘Legend’.”

Ryan raised an eyebrow. In the corner, Baek Eun-ah closed her eyes again. She knew. There was no universe in which this man reached for the gentle American-talk-show marketing script.

Baek Si-on kept going:

“I’m not trading ‘Legend’ for ‘Love Yourself’.”

“I’m opening a second door.”

The answer came out harder than Ryan had expected.

Which made it better.

“So you don’t consider this a reinvention?”

“No.”

“What is it, then?”

“The same person, talking in a different room.”

Ryan looked at him for a second, and laughed.

“I’m keeping that. Now — the ‘Love Yourself’ MV drops tomorrow, right?”

“Yes.”

“The teaser — you and, ah, IU — has already started a lot of conversation. People are saying it’s less a music video than a short film.”

“The director will be glad to hear that.”

“Director Hwang Soo-ah?”

“Mm.”

Ryan grabbed the thread:

“So the director is the most important part of this video?”

“The song is the most important part.”

Ryan nodded, about to move on — and Baek Si-on added:

“But without the director, it would have become a very easy song to misunderstand.”

“Misunderstood as what?”

“A healing anthem about girls loving themselves.”

“HAH—”

Ryan laughed so hard the mic clipped.

Outside the glass, a staffer flashed the hand signal: live performance segment.

Ryan leaned back.

“All right — let’s hear a live, stripped version of ‘Love Yourself’.”

He turned toward the glass:

“Listeners — if you’re still living inside the ‘Legend’ version of this man, the next three minutes may not match.”

Baek Si-on took the guitar a staffer handed him and tested two low notes. The studio went quiet.

When the first string sounded, the man sixty thousand people had been chanting at a day and a half ago sat in a room that held barely a dozen, and began a very light song about a very clean breakup.

‘Love Yourself’ · KIIS FM · live, stripped

The session wrapped near four.

Handshakes, photos, a stack of signed boards — Baek Si-on finished all of it before coming out of the studio. The day was stacked: forty minutes to the next commitment, and the gap was supposed to go to Scooter, walking through the next few days.

But Scooter had taken a call. Five minutes, he said.

Eight minutes had gone by.

Baek Si-on looked down at his phone.

Choi Jin-ri had sent a photo half an hour earlier. In Korea it was already the next morning: Blin on the living room floor, wearing the little cat hoodie he’d bought, head tipped sideways at the camera.

It did, in fact, look like a goblin.

He was composing a reply when perfume arrived from somewhere to his left.

“Oh my God — you have a Sphynx?”

Baek Si-on looked up.

Taylor stood beside him, sunglasses up on her head, coffee in hand, wearing an expression of completely sincere scandal.

Year-end radio season. It would have been stranger if she hadn’t been here.

Her assistant, one step back, had opened her mouth to herd her toward the booth — and lost her entirely, because Taylor’s attention had locked onto the cat on Baek Si-on’s phone.

She was still sharing — Scooter came back around the corner of the corridor.

And saw the two of them.

Standing in a radio-station hallway. One looking down at a phone, one earnestly explaining litter-box doctrine. The distance was nothing, technically. But the atmosphere — two people trading lived experience like old acquaintances — crawled up the back of his scalp harder than any collaboration pitch ever had.

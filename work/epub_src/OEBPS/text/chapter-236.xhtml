Chapter Two Hundred Thirty-Six

Chocolate in Hand, Off to Drag Someone Into Overtime

Monitor · the church · final take

The muzzle levels at the altar. Kim Hye-soo kneels beneath the statue of the Holy Mother, her white gown soaked through, a viscous dark red pooled around her knees. The religious murals behind her are perforated with bullet holes; shattered stained glass litters the floor; the pews of the hall lie toppled in rows. The believers who were singing hymns minutes ago are now only bodies, strewn where they fell.

Baek Si-on stands before the altar, his costume stained past its original color, pistol loose in his right hand at his side.

Kim Hye-soo lifts her face. The Holy Mother’s habitual mercy is gone.

“You think killing me ends the game?”

He raises his arm.

“I’m dying.”

The muzzle stops at her forehead.

“No time for chess.”

Trigger. BANG—! Muzzle flash lights the wrecked hall. She falls backward under the altar; blood crawls down the white steps and soaks the Bible that has already been trampled underfoot.

“Cut.” — Baek Jeong-hoon, from behind the monitor.

The armorer moved in at once and took the prop gun out of Baek Si-on’s hand first.

Kim Hye-soo lay before the altar, holding her post-shot pose. The costume team waited beyond the cordon for the director’s ruling. Baek Jeong-hoon dragged the playback back to before the shot: “I’m dying.” “No time for chess.” The crack. The Holy Mother falling. The blankness on Baek Si-on’s face, preserved whole in the frame.

The playback ran to its end.

Baek Jeong-hoon picked up the walkie.

“Passed.”

The stage crew started pushing the cleaning rig forward — and the walkie crackled again.

“Nobody touch anything yet.”

The staff froze at the edges of the hall. Baek Jeong-hoon rose from behind the monitor.

“Baek Si-on.”

Baek Si-on turned around.

“‘The Three Evils’ — all your scenes. That’s a wrap.”

A beat of silence first.

Then the applause broke — props, camera, armory, and extras together, somebody whistling over the top of it. The hall that had been full of corpses and cult believers seconds before turned, on the spot, into a raucous wrap party.

The stage crew came up with the flowers.

“Congratulations on the wrap.”

Baek Si-on took the bouquet, bowed his thanks to him first, then turned to the departments.

“Thank you all — truly — for these weeks.”

The applause came again.

His personal scenes had been scheduled to finish at the end of July. They had been hauled forward to early July. Bluehole’s pre-visualization had eaten the blocking problems whole, and that efficiency had handed Baek Si-on back nearly three weeks.

❧ ❧ ❧

Blood is hard to wash. The makeup artist spent an entire pack of wipes on the dark red behind his ears and along his neck.

The costume stayed with the production. The buzz cut stayed too.

He changed back into his own black tee and trousers and carried the wrap bouquet into the nanny van. The door slid shut on the noise outside.

Baek Eun-ah’s schedule sheet lay on the tray: page one, ‘Sado’. Page two, America.

The schedule, as thinned

Page 1: ‘Sado’ — press conference, media screenings, twelve stage greetings.

Page 2: America — the Hollywood debut.

Unassigned days remaining: two. A normal actor would spend them asleep at home, confirming he still recognized his own bed.

What Baek Si-on had in mind for the two days was Riot’s S5 theme.

The melodic frame of ‘Legends Never Die’ had already been roughed out on the ‘The Three Evils’ set; the chorus concept had landed too. Two days would not produce a deliverable master — but a complete skeleton, a settled vocal structure, and a demo good enough for Riot to judge direction: that much, barely.

He knocked on the driver’s partition.

“To Jung Jae-joon’s studio.”

The driver acknowledged from the front. The van left the set.

❧ ❧ ❧

That evening

One dark studio · one host stranded in Japan

Jung Jae-joon’s studio · the control room

The corridor outside the studio was lit; inside the door was dark.

Baek Si-on keyed the code and let himself in. The sealed, soundproofed room held the faint electronic smell of recording gear that never fully powers down. He found the wall switch; the tubes came on section by section. Console, monitors, speakers, mic stands — all in order. In the corner of the sofa, the blanket Jung Jae-joon had used last time still lay where he’d abandoned it.

The man had clearly not come back.

Baek Si-on stood in the middle of the control room and dialed.

Baek Si-on → Jung Jae-joon · voice · somewhere in Japan

The call connected to Japanese station announcements first, luggage wheels and crowd chatter underneath them.

“You’re still in Japan?”

“Mm. The trip got extended.”

Goo Hara’s Japanese solo album had entered formal release preparation. ‘Uchiage Hanabi’ was one of its double leads, and it involved a Japanese male singer’s featured section. If the goal was to truly enter the local market, the Korean leads flying out to grind through pronunciation, tracking, and arrangements in person was the baseline of good partnership — having the local singer shuttle to Seoul to accommodate Korean team habits was convenient, and quietly arrogant.

So Jung Jae-joon had gone with her: the male vocal, plus lyric diction, arrangement details, and album bonus material with the local team.

“When are you back?” Baek Si-on asked.

“Three or four days, if it goes well.”

In three or four days he would already be standing at the ‘Sado’ press conference.

Jung Jae-joon heard the silence and asked:

“Another job?”

“Riot’s Season 5 theme.”

“You’ve started?”

“I have a frame.”

“Wait for me?”

“Can’t.”

Jung Jae-joon took the answer without surprise. Baek Si-on’s working tempo had never once been computed on a normal human calendar.

“The project templates are on the main machine — my presets are all yours.”

“Got it.”

Baek Si-on walked to the console and lifted off the dust cover.

“Go do your thing.”

The call ended.

The main machine booted. He imported the rough session he’d built on set, put the headphones on, and began dismantling from the chorus backward. Which word carried the accent; how fast the melody climbed from the low register; how heavy the verse should sit; how much room the drums needed before the chorus arrived. All of it had lived as outlines in his head — inside the software, every single choice had to be made again.

Strings spread. Low bass entered. Drums advanced from a distance.

The demo, as versioned

Version one — exposed immediately: too many electronic patches. A lively chorus that sounded exactly like a game promo doing its assigned job. Riot could use it. Nobody would ever press play twice.

Version two — the main electronic tracks deleted, thicker string layers stacked in. Grand. The problem changed shape: it sounded like a film trailer. Every bar instructing the listener — now feel the heat, now feel the moved, now prepare to applaud the hero. Too hard. Too performed.

Version three — the pre-chorus string pile struck out, drums pressed back to center, electronic lows carrying the competitive edge, strings entering only where the melody truly lifts. Verse low. Drums restrained. The emotion climbing stepwise until the chorus shoves the whole sound-field open.

Direction: found.

❝

A legend does not need the music to kneel on the ground and explain how great she is.

— the note-to-self, after version two

“Legends never die.” It could be handed to the man fallen off his throne, and to the veteran walking out of the arena for the last time.

❝

They have lost. They have aged. They have been dropped by a new era. And the name stays anyway — in every place people remember.

— the chorus thesis, as it finally stood

❧ ❧ ❧

The song’s skeleton stood. Next: the vocal demo.

Baek Si-on armed the recorder and walked into the booth.

The one-man session, as suffered

Verse one tracked. Headphones off, back to the control room: gain too low. Adjust. Back into the booth.

Chorus down. Out again — pitch check, double-track positions. Second track needs different harmony. In again. Out again.

The two doors are maybe fifteen steps apart; enough round trips turn any creative fire into cardio. Worse: the singer and the engineer share one pair of ears, so every problem the ear can’t catch live waits for the playback chair.

Verdict: a solo overdub session is possible. An efficient one is not — what he needed was someone who could read the melody, run the session, and drop a temporary harmony while she was at it.

He sat at the console and thought. His fingers tapped the chorus rhythm on the desk, and a name surfaced on its own.

Lee Ji-eun.

He picked up the phone and called Jeong Han-teuk.

Baek Si-on → Jeong Han-teuk · voice · the agent’s office

“Does Ji-eun have a schedule today?”

“She was at this year’s ‘Infinite Challenge’ music festival a few days ago — she’s deep in writing at the moment.”

“Where is she?”

Jeong Han-teuk went instantly wary.

“What do you want with her?”

“There’s a song that needs a demo vocal.”

“Today?”

“Now.”

A short silence; then Han-teuk read out the address, and added:

“Her writing mood is bad, so her temper will be worse.”

“When is her temper ever good?”

“Tell her that yourself.”

“I will.”

Han-teuk went quiet for a while. He suspected his warning had protected no one — and had, in fact, booked Lee Ji-eun a head-on collision.

The address was already sent. The rest belonged to fate.

❧ ❧ ❧

That night

One unscheduled visit · one paper bag of chocolate

Lee Ji-eun’s building · the intercom

On the way, Baek Si-on had the driver stop at an imported-foods store — European chocolates, biscuits, coffee on the shelves. He worked the aisles and picked out several boxes of the brand Lee Ji-eun was always eating. The cashier packed them into a paper bag.

Paid. Back to the car.

The van stopped below her building. Baek Si-on pulled on cap and mask — chocolate bag in his left hand, the laptop with the session files in his right — stepped down, and pressed the unit number Han-teuk had sent.

The intercom buzzed.

The work was improvised. The visit was unannounced. The chocolate at least made the doorbell look less like a pure overtime snatch.

As for how it would actually go — that depended on Lee Ji-eun’s mood when she opened the door.

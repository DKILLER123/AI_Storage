Chapter One Hundred Fifty Six

Best Female Singer, Forced Into the Turn

December 3 · 9:47 p.m. Mid-ceremony · one phone call about to ruin three dinners Hong Kong · a hotel suite, an Mnet control room, and one sugar division desk in Seoul The call tree of a conglomerate is a beautiful machine: it takes one cousin’s sentence, adds seasoning at every branch, and delivers it to the top floor as a market event.

tvN president Lee Myung-han had been in a fine mood.

Baek Si-on’s ‘Human’ opening had printed a beautiful rating peak; Lee Ji-eun’s medley had hauled the graph right back up a second time. Both of them were locked names on the S.W Studio free concert — which meant the forty-billion-won broadcast package he had bought was being validated, live, over and over, by the very ceremony he was watching.

Then Baek Eun-ah’s call came in.

He listened, and went blank. Put her on hold and had his assistant dig. Five minutes later, the picture came back, and Lee Myung-han understood.

MAMA tradition. The meat-sharing going wrong enough to spoil the main course — first time? Please. It was practically the festival’s founding ritual.

Honestly — and he would never say this aloud — in his opinion it was nothing. If anything, Lee Ji-eun was being a touch precious about it. Your own company’s Melon runs the MMAs, and didn’t the big prizes land there? Two hit songs and suddenly one artist expects to sweep all four year-end ceremonies of the entire Korean music industry? The table is only so big. Be content.

That was what he thought.

What he said, and where, were different matters — because the woman in question was now load-bearing for the biggest project he had ever staked money on. He had not spent forty billion to broadcast a concert. He had spent forty billion to climb: to use Baek Si-on to push tvN up one more floor, from “the cable channel with the good dramas and variety” to something that could arm-wrestle the terrestrial year-end song festivals face to face. If the concert landed, tvN would ride his global heat, his domestic fandom, the overseas rights, and the whole CJ apparatus to grind KBS’s year-end song festival into the ratings curve.

An opportunity like that comes along once every several years, if that.

If a plate of badly cut MAMA meat annoyed Lee Ji-eun into shrinking her role — or skipping the concert entirely — Lee Myung-han would dismantle the monitoring wall with his own hands.

He picked up the phone and called Mnet’s chief, Shin Hyung-kwan.

Lee Myung-han → Shin Hyung-kwan · 9:51 p.m. · from the hotel suite to the control room

Director Shin. A favor: give IU the awards she’s actually earned. All of them.

A full second of silence, the audible kind.

Why?

Lee Myung-han nearly laughed in fury.

What do you mean, why? Do you not know tvN paid forty billion for the broadcast rights to Baek Si-on’s concert?

I know. So what?

So what?

He repeated the two words slowly, the way a man repeats something while his blood pressure files for independence.

Do you understand that this touches the entire programming strategy of this network?

Myung-han-yah. Did directing a few decent dramas make you the savior of CJ?

A cold little laugh came down the line, and then the seniority came out, unsheathed.

In ’94, when I was cutting tape through the night at CJ, you were still pouring tea for your elders at KBS. What gives you the standing to order me to change a winners’ list? I’ll tell you this once: even if the King of Heaven himself walked in here tonight, that list does not move. Mnet’s rules are not yours to break, outsider.

Dud. The line went dead in his ear.

Lee Myung-han listened to the busy tone and rubbed his chest with his free hand.

Wonderful. Blood pressure: rising.

In 2011, CJ had paid a fortune to pull him out of KBS’s entertainment division, where he had been running 2 Days & 1 Night. In the three years since, riding the two SSR-tier cards he’d brought with him, he had grown tvN from a cable station people openly joked about into a national one — and now there was nobody inside CJ who didn’t know tvN as the fastest-growing, loudest nameplate in the group. Tonight, a self-declared twenty-year veteran of the company had told him to his face that he was nobody.

Seniority. It was always seniority. That was the poison at the bottom of Korean office life: some people simmer themselves old and decide the simmering made them the rules.

Lee Myung-han let the breath out slowly. Then he opened his contacts, found a number, and dialed.

When it connected, he did not complain loudly. He simply told the story — with the seasoning increased at every sentence, the way one does.

…

Mnet control room.

Shin Hyung-kwan was still wearing the hard face from the tvN call. The room churned around him: monitors showing Ma-ryong’s stage just ending, the arena at full boil; staff shuttling in both directions; one desk watching realtime ratings, one watching the ad breaks, one watching the artists’ seats for faces.

He put the phone down on the desk. The fire hadn’t gone out.

He had joined CJ as one of Mnet’s founding-floor producers, back when the channel was new. From the early days of M Countdown to turning MKMF into what the world now called MAMA — planned, built, and steered by his own hands, start to finish. And Lee Myung-han was what, exactly? An outsider. A parvenu carried by two producers, Na Young-seok and Shin Won-ho, like a chair carried by two strongmen. And this parvenu thought he could phone in an order to change MAMA’s list?

The phone rang again.

He looked down at the display and stood up so fast his chair rolled back. He received the call with both hands, physically cradling the phone, and his spine bent of its own accord.

Shin Hyung-kwan ← Lee Seon-ho · the MAMA control room · incoming

Young Master. You were looking for me.

The other end was quiet for a beat. Then a young man’s voice came through, mild as milk.

Director Shin, you’ve got the wrong person, I think.

In this company, you’re the elder. The department head. I’m a lowly assistant manager in the sugar division — for you to call me ‘young master’…

Shin Hyung-kwan’s cold sweat arrived on schedule.

The whole CJ Group knew who this was. The young man carrying the business card of an “assistant manager” was the chairman’s eldest son — the single, unambiguous heir to the empire.

No, no — you honor me too much.

What are your instructions?

A small laugh from the other end.

Instructions? Nothing so grand.

Just a junior colleague in the field, reporting a little difficulty at work.

The young master’s name was never spoken. He didn’t need it. Lee Seon-ho sighed — a real sigh, performed at the exact resonance of a junior employee genuinely worried about his KPI.

I’ve recently been handling global marketing data for the sugar division.

Mr. Baek Si-on is our newly signed endorser. He’s supposed to help pull up our North America business.

But just now I heard that Mr. Baek is upset over something at MAMA, and that it might affect future cooperation.

Shin Hyung-kwan opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

Lee Seon-ho kept going, unhurried:

You’re an elder of this group — you’ve watched CJ grow since the beginning. You understand our situation better than anyone.

Father is where he is. Aunt is in the States. The whole group is walking a wire, and the wire doesn’t take gusts.

So teach me: if one trophy makes Mr. Baek stop working, and CJ’s market cap evaporates by several trillion tomorrow — what do I tell the shareholders? And what do I tell my father, from where he is?

Shin Hyung-kwan: ……

The control room roared around him — cued graphics, talking heads, a stage manager shouting about a rundown change — and yet in his ears the whole room reduced to one phrase on a loop: market cap, several trillion.

Wait.

Wasn’t this about Lee Ji-eun?

How had it become about Baek Si-on? How had it become about CheilJedang’s market cap? How had defending Mnet’s rules, one phone call ago, turned him into the risk source of the group’s entire global business?

He hurried to explain:

No — no, it’s not like that.

Yo— Assistant Manager Lee. We have treated Mr. Baek Si-on with the utmost seriousness.

He was given the opening stage. The center of the artist seating. The camera time — generous, you can check the rundown.

When he asked for chairs for T-ara just now, we moved without one word of argument.

If his album release hadn’t fallen after the voting cutoff, the three grand prizes were — frankly — being prepared for him anyway.

It came out in one breath — so fast that several staff members at the neighboring desks developed sudden, total deafness while staring very hard at their screens.

Is that so.

On the other end, Lee Seon-ho pressed two fingers to his temple.

Not one lamp in this entire generation of old guard burned anything but oil. When Lee Myung-han had called, the story arrived pre-seasoned to “CJ stock limit-down by tomorrow’s bell.” Having now seen the raw material himself, Lee Seon-ho could report: the underlying incident was nowhere near that grave. And every single person who touched it shoved it one degree closer to grave anyway. That was CJ’s internal weather system. Four dozen managers in their late forties, each privately convinced he was the one irreplaceable pillar of the group — while the “assistant manager of the sugar division,” whose actual daily job was walking between those pillars keeping them from toppling in the same direction at once, aged one year per quarter.

Worse than prison, honestly. Prison doesn’t make you mediate your own subordinates.

In any case — I’d like this event managed sensibly, Director Shin.

If a trophy allocation creates feelings on Mr. Baek’s side, which then touches the sugar division’s global endorsement and tvN’s broadcast contract…

He let the sentence stop and stand there.

That kind of chain reaction is beyond what one assistant manager can absorb.

Shin Hyung-kwan heard every word that wasn’t said. The meaning came through clean as a stage direction: if CJ ENM wants to fight, fight with the doors closed. Don’t put it in front of partners and capital markets. And do not splash grease on the sugar division’s global endorsement.

Lee Seon-ho was not out to demonstrate brilliant leadership in a storm season. He wanted exactly one thing: stability. No incidents. No embarrassments. No version of the future where his father and his aunt came home, pointed at him, and said: Lee Seon-ho — you couldn’t even manage one small awards show?

Shin Hyung-kwan was nodding before he knew it. The phone couldn’t see him. His voice could, and the spine in the voice had folded.

Understood. I’ll re-coordinate the remaining awards immediately.

Lee Seon-ho asked:

Won’t that break the rules of your bureau?

The probe was audible. Shin Hyung-kwan hurried over it:

You flatter me by asking.

At CJ, your — that is, the group’s interest is the only rule there is.

A small laugh.

Then thank you for the trouble, Director Shin.

Some other day, on my father’s behalf — I’ll buy you a proper cup of tea.

The call ended. Shin Hyung-kwan lowered the phone from both hands to the desk, and the control room’s noise came flooding back into his ears.

He thought about what he had said into this same phone, not thirty minutes ago, to a different caller. Even if the King of Heaven himself walked in here tonight, that list does not move.

The King of Heaven had not come.

The group’s young master had.

Mm.

Not perjury, strictly. The young master was not the King of Heaven.

The young master was the King of Heaven’s son.

Different concept. Entirely different concept.

Shin Hyung-kwan raised a hand and beckoned. The executive director jogged over.

“Chief?”

Shin Hyung-kwan dropped his voice.

“Change the remaining awards.”

“Which ones?”

…

On stage, the host’s voice came back up.

“Next, we present the Best Female Singer of the 2014 MAMA!”

The big screen lit. Five names, five faces, one after another.

Each name detonated its own fan section in sequence — five separate weather systems sharing one arena.

The package film ended. The presenter walked out with the sealed envelope and smiled into the camera.

“The winner of Best Female Singer at the 2014 MAMA is…”

He looked down. His fingers had barely touched the envelope’s flap when a staffer burst in from the wing.

The director, a professional to the marrow, cut instantly to Baek Si-on.

The arena blinked collectively. Best Female Singer — why are we looking at him? But bewilderment has never once interfered with screaming at a beautiful face; the volume didn’t drop by a single decibel.

The staffer whispered a few sentences into the presenter’s ear, bowed at the waist, and jogged off.

The director cut back. The presenter looked down at the envelope.

Inside, in plain black on white card stock, the card said: HyunA.

He raised his head. The smile returned to his face like a stagehand resetting a prop.

“—IU! Congratulations!”

The arena went still for exactly one heartbeat.

Then the cheers and the screaming hit like a wave hitting a sea wall.

Lee Ji-eun heard her name and froze for a second. She had been sulking over a Most Popular Singer trophy — and now the ceremony had turned around and stuffed a Best Female Singer into her arms.

Then she looked at Baek Si-on.

Baek Si-on was applauding for her, expression utterly blank — the face of a law-abiding, music-loving member of the general public for whom nothing at all had happened tonight.

She drew a small breath and walked to the stage, applause and screaming pressing in from every direction. She took the second trophy of the night from the presenter. The smile stayed on — but this one wasn’t the furious kind. It was the kind that leaks out when a situation becomes so absurd it circles back into funny.

She reached the microphone.

“Thank you to MAMA for the Best Female Singer award.”

Applause rolled.

“I already went up once tonight, so I truly didn’t expect to come up again.”

She kept going, steadier now:

“As a singer, I’ve always believed that being heard by many people is a kind of luck.”

“Being judged seriously — that’s the rarer thing.”

“Thank you to everyone who listens to my songs, and to the staff, my family, and my friends who stay with me.”

Her eyes went over the floor seats — and crossed one particular face for a quarter of a second.

“I’ll keep working — to be a singer worthy of the stage, and worthy of the trophy.”

The applause that followed was heavier than the first one. She bowed and came down.

Best Male Singer followed, and it went to Baek Si-on with all the suspense of a sunrise. Nobody contested it; the other nominees reportedly considered sharing a ballot with him its own honor. He stood, and — as he rose — the entire row of six from T-ara came up with him, applauding hard, with the commitment of people settling the bill for the best spectator seats in the building.

…

Coming off the side of the stage with the trophy, he was intercepted by the female host, all smiles.

“Baek Si-on-ssi, this way first, please.”

He stopped. Lee Ji-eun was already standing beside her — one hand around a microphone, the other around the new trophy, the leftover award-smile still not fully collected from the corners of her mouth.

The two of them lined up at the side zone of the stage, and the host faced the cameras.

“The 2014 MAMA has just revealed its Best Male and Best Female Singers.”

“Winners — say hello to everyone!”

Lee Ji-eun went first, microphone up.

“Hello everyone, this is IU.”

Sweet voice. Steady smile. The nation’s little sister at full retail mode. Nobody watching would have guessed she had spent a chunk of this very broadcast flipping the venue off through a trophy speech — she had hidden it that well.

When she finished, she and the host, near-simultaneously, extended their microphones toward Baek Si-on. Two mics arrived at his mouth from the left and the right.

He looked at them for a moment.

Then he chose hers.

“Hello everyone, this is Baek Si-on.”

He handed it back. As Lee Ji-eun took it, her fingertips crossed his knuckles.

Whether that was an accident, or careful, was not recorded anywhere.

“With Best Male and Best Female Singer now decided — do the two of you have ambitions for the grand prizes? Song of the Year, Album of the Year, Artist of the Year?”

Lee Ji-eun glanced at Baek Si-on. Thought about it. Then held her microphone out to him. The gesture required no translation: you first.

Baek Si-on took it.

“If they give them to me, I’ll gladly accept.”

The fan sections detonated on cue. The host, visibly unprepared for an answer with zero diplomatic padding, swung to Lee Ji-eun.

Having heard that, Lee Ji-eun saw no further use for modesty.

“Same. If they give them to me, I’ll gladly accept too.”

“Then — have you predicted who takes the grand prizes this year?”

Lee Ji-eun tilted her head, thought, and answered:

“I think it’ll be Baek Si-on-ssi.”

She held the microphone out. He took it.

“I also think it’ll be me.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

The host, sensing two ships about to collide, tried to throw out a fender.

“For instance — IU-ssi also has a very real chance, no?”

Baek Si-on considered it.

“That would be insincere.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

She turned and looked up at him, her smile one twitch from structural failure.

No. Was mutual graciousness on camera — warm, humble, lovable — some kind of forbidden technique? He could have said IU’s songs were wonderful too, just once, as a courtesy! Instead he straight-up claimed the trophy with his whole chest, which retroactively converted her into a straight man in somebody else’s comedy sketch — the feed spot, the one whose entire job is being steamrolled on cue.

The female host laughed so hard she nearly lost the rundown. Laughter broke across the artist seats too.

the six-chair peanut gallery · the “I also think it’ll be me” incident

Jiyeon had both hands over her mouth, shoulders going like a faulty washing machine.

“He really does not do politeness. At all.”

Hyomin, sotto voce:

“But it lands so naturally, though.”

Soyeon checked the camera positions and snapped upright into her most ceremonial posture.

“Stop laughing that obviously. The director is evil.”

The director was, empirically, evil: the cut to the T-ara row landed before she finished the sentence, and six spines straightened in one wave, faces arranged into perfect public-interest serenity.

The host fought back to professionalism and consulted her cue cards.

“Coming up later — the winner of Song of the Year at the 2014 MAMA. Stay with us.”

Baek Si-on and Lee Ji-eun bowed to the arena together and turned off the stage. Her voice dropped low as they walked.

“That answer just now cost me face.”

“Which one?”

“‘I also think it’ll be me.’”

“You also think so.”

“The issue is not what I think. The issue is that you were supposed to say something else.”

“Such as?”

“Such as — IU-ssi has a very real chance too.”

“That would be insincere.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

Excellent. Cindy state: rising, stable, weatherproof.

…

What followed was a conveyor of stages, which is MAMA’s true core competency: lifts, fireworks, mass choreography — boy groups splitting into formation like Machinery, girl groups swapping outfits inside the scream, impromptu hype bars folded into the hip-hop sets. The organizers packed every minute full, visibly terrified that the audience might, in a quiet moment, start wondering how the meat had actually been carved.

But part of the house had stopped watching. The evening’s award logic had developed a visible fault line — a before and an after, with the after making no geological sense whatsoever — and everyone with a scorecard had noticed.

Then the stage went dark for real.

The presenter returned for the first grand prize of the night.

“Song of the Year.”

The big screen lit; a low tone rolled around the bowl of the arena. The nominee package began.

BAEK SI-ON — ‘Way Back Home’

Five songs, and every one of them a thing you heard drifting out of café doors on any given street in Korea. A genuinely monstrous year. The fan sections answered each title with its own standing wave of noise.

Lee Ji-eun tipped her head toward Baek Si-on.

“Nervous?”

“No.”

“You said it’d be you.”

“I still say so.”

She rolled her eyes at the far wall. She should not have asked. Truly.

“What a contest,” the presenter said to the floor, smiling, and looked down to open the envelope. The camera began its slow tour of the nominees’ faces: EXO sitting in saintly formation; SISTAR smiling up at the stage; Taeyang with his arms crossed; , expressionless; , smiling on schedule.

“The winner of the 2014 MAMA Song of the Year is—”

The whole arena went quiet — nothing left in the air but the drum-roll cue, tightening.

The presenter opened the envelope. Drew the card. Looked at it. Looked up.

“Song of the Year goes to—”

He stretched it, professional to the last syllable.

“IU — ‘Friday’! Congratulations!”

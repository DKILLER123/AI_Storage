Chapter One Hundred Ninety Three

Not Exactly a Proper Arrangement Lesson

The same Milan evening · 9:40 p.m. → the small hours One handover brief at a fashion-week backstage · a brand dinner exited politely on the grounds of “a lesson” · a dress code no teacher’s union would certify · a syllabus in four bullets · a demo called Cindy_23_draft · two kisses timed to a metronome · and a bridge that dropped half a key, which is the most anyone is getting out of her tonight The backstage corridor → a Gucci hotel, ninth floor → suite → sofa → door → sofa again The manager flies west, the watchdog pleads illness, the student learns fast enough to be annoying, the teacher loses control of the curriculum — and the file gets saved under a name that contradicts itself on purpose.

Before flying to Los Angeles, Baek Eun-ah cornered Jung Han-teuk at the fashion-week backstage.

“Han-teuk oppa.”

She handed over a tablet: Baek Si-on’s Milan schedule for the next several days.

“I’m flying to LA in a bit.”

He nodded. He knew. Charlie Puth’s new single ‘Marvin Gaye’ was about to drop, and the American rookie was about to run radio promo with Meghan. As S.W Studio’s nominal head of artist management — Eun-ah had to be there to babysit the company’s first signed artist, whose current state existed somewhere between “future prospect” and “nervous rookie who might seat his own boss on a pedestal during a live interview.”

Han-teuk took the schedule, glanced at it, and looked back at her.

“So?”

Eun-ah said, very seriously:

“For the next few days, please keep an eye on my cousin for me.”

“Keep an eye… on which aspect?”

She thought about it.

“Work — don’t bother, he works by himself. Food — don’t bother too much, he finds things to eat when he’s hungry.”

Han-teuk went quiet. At first listen it sounded like handing over an artist. At second listen it sounded like handing over a large feline predator that hunts its own meals.

Eun-ah kept going:

“Mainly — don’t let him get carried off by actresses.”

The corridor stayed quiet for two seconds. Han-teuk’s expression held perfectly stable.

“Milan’s public order should be adequate.”

She looked at him. He went on pretending to understand nothing whatsoever.

“Also, I might have a bit of an upset stomach tonight. Not sure I can be out and about.”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

She elected not to expose him. Exposing him would mean staying herself, and Charlie Puth had already sent her four messages asking what time you landing.

❝

Handover brief, Milan station: work — self-managing. Food — self-hunting. Actresses — deny at all costs. Watchdog’s acknowledgment: “Milan’s public order should be adequate.” Watchdog’s own status: suddenly, tragically, unwell.

— the backstage corridor, on delegation

❧ ❧ ❧

9:40 p.m. Baek Si-on stood outside Lee Ji-eun’s suite. The wall lamps glowed a soft warm yellow; night at a Gucci hotel always came with a perfume-scented hush. He had put in an appearance at the brand dinner — one glass of champagne, ten minutes with two Italian editors on the relationship between cinema and clothes — and then excused himself, citing “a lesson.” The brand PR had asked which kind of lesson. An arrangement lesson, he said. They hadn’t understood a word of it, but agreed it sounded extremely cool.

The door opened. Baek Si-on knew from the first look that this lesson would not be a proper one.

the teacher’s dress code · doorway, 9:40 p.m. · as observed by the student

TOP — cream silk shirt. Two buttons open. Sleeves rolled to the forearm.

BOTTOM — black high-waisted skirt. Length: safe. Post-seating safety: not assessed, cannot be certified.

FACE — lighter than daytime, and not bare. Specifically: the forty minutes of work engineered to read as five.

Curriculum neutrality: compromised before roll call.

Lee Ji-eun, registering his pause not at all, stepped aside and held the door.

“Come in.”

Baek Si-on didn’t move at once. She raised an eyebrow.

“What — a Billboard champion afraid of an arrangement lesson?”

“Afraid the teacher prepared too seriously.”

The root of her ear changed color by a very slight degree. If the corridor light hadn’t happened to fall from the side, he might have missed it. Her expression, meanwhile, presented not one crack.

“Then you’re free not to learn.”

Her hand was already on the doorframe, ready to close the door. Baek Si-on looked at her for two seconds — and stepped inside. The door shut behind him. The latch made a very small click.

❧ ❧ ❧

The room held a laptop, a small MIDI keyboard, headphones, two bottles of sparkling water, and several packs of snacks brought from Korea. The computer was already on, a Logic project open on the screen. Lee Ji-eun had, in fact, prepared for class. Prepared seriously. She sat down on the sofa; the skirt rode up a little with the motion. Baek Si-on, standing beside the sofa, looked down at the not-large coffee table, and then at her legs.

She looked up.

“Planning to learn standing?”

He sat down, impeccably, hands on knees. The corner of her mouth moved and failed to produce a laugh.

“Ground rule: I only teach basics. Ask anything too professional, go ask Jung Jae-joon.”

“He’ll overthink it.”

“You know that?”

“That’s why I came to you.”

Her fingers paused — and she gave a small hmph.

“As if I wouldn’t overthink.”

“Would you?”

“I would not.”

He looked at her. She opened a new project with exemplary serenity.

“Eyes on the screen.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Once the lecture actually began, the atmosphere normalized somewhat. At least on the surface. She taught clearly.

Arrangement 101 · the syllabus, four bullets, delivered without a single flushed note

01 — Creating tracks: separating piano, drums, and vocals.

02 — Recording the melody in your head straight through a MIDI keyboard; no sheet music required — the producer only needs the direction.

03 — Quantization: the machine forgives what your fingers mangle.

04 — Looping: four bars into sixteen. The skeleton of a song, before it has anything to say.

Baek Si-on listened quietly. When she finished the pass, he reached over and took the trackpad. A few taps on the screen: new project, piano track open, a four-bar chord progression played in on the MIDI keyboard, quantized, copied, a basic drum bed laid under it. Lee Ji-eun looked at the arrangement he had correctly pulled out of the machine, and her fingers stopped in mid-air.

“……Students like you are obnoxious.”

Baek Si-on pushed the trackpad back.

“Failing is obnoxious, and learning too fast is also obnoxious?”

“The teacher gets no sense of achievement.”

“Then you could teach something harder.”

“Are you here for a lesson, or to provoke me?”

“At present: both, with no scheduling conflict.”

She wanted to wind the headphone cable around his neck. As a thought only. She still had a teacher’s dignity to maintain — although nothing about today’s teacher dressed like a proper one. She turned the laptop back and opened another project.

“Fine. Since you’re so capable — we go straight to practicum.”

The file name was simple: Cindy_23_draft.

“It’s the song I’m preparing for Cindy in ‘Producers’. Not finished. The direction’s there, but something’s missing and I can’t name it.”

His gaze traveled from the file name to her face and back.

“Cindy’s song?”

“Mm. There’s a stage performance in the show. I want to write it myself.”

“The production doesn’t assign it?”

“I talked to Writer Park. She agreed to let me try.”

Baek Si-on nodded. She pressed play. A thin drum line came out of the speakers, unhurried — and then her voice.

‘Twenty-Three’ · Cindy’s demo, take one · played out loud in a Milan suite

“I’m twenty three —”

“It might hurt you —”

“But don’t miss me —”

— his gaze lifted, one degree, at the third line. She spoke before he could —

“I bloom in your arms —”

“Darling, when the moon rises I’ll go —”

“I’ll wear what I’ve never worn —”

“I’ll show an expression I’ve never shown —”

She had watched his eyes move, and fired first:

“It’s the character.”

Baek Si-on turned to look at her.

“I didn’t say anything.”

“Your face said it.”

“What your face said was: this character writes lyrics rather well.”

Lee Ji-eun: “……”

She had three explanations loaded and ready — it’s Cindy’s persona; it’s what the script needs; it is not Lee Ji-eun — and he hadn’t questioned. He’d complimented. Completely off-script for her. The song kept playing. The room’s heating seemed to be running a little strong all of a sudden. She reached for the sparkling water; the cap wouldn’t turn. Baek Si-on took it, opened it for her, and set it back at her hand. A natural motion. Natural in the way that is more dangerous than anything deliberate.

❧ ❧ ❧

For the next hour and more, the two of them took ‘Twenty-Three’ apart and put it back together. Lee Ji-eun deleted a chord run in the chorus that was too sweet. Baek Si-on proposed drier drums — less room reverb, every hit landing like a fingernail rapped on a tabletop.

“The bass needs to drag behind, too.”

He played the two lowest keys on the MIDI keyboard.

“Why?”

“Because Cindy doesn’t walk on the beat. She’s always one step slower than everyone else before she turns around.”

She tried it. The effect was immediate: the bass line that had locked flush with the drums got dragged back half a sixteenth-note, and the whole song’s groove grew a lazy, indifferent, yet precisely controlled pulse.

❝

Remix notes, ‘Twenty-Three’: drums like fingernails on a table. Bass a half-sixteenth behind its own shadow. Because Cindy doesn’t walk on the beat — she’s one step slower than everyone before she turns around.

— the second draft, on its subject

They played the final demo from the top. Against two hours ago it was a different song entirely. Colder drums. Lazier bass. A piano motif like a sigh. A voice that had stopped trying to explain anything. It no longer sounded like a twenty-three-year-old girl saying please understand me. It sounded like a twenty-three-year-old saying you don’t have to understand me — but you’ll remember me.

The playback ended. Lee Ji-eun pulled the headphones off and set them on the keyboard.

“Do you think Cindy’s bad?”

He thought for a moment.

“She just learned to take the knives other people gave her, and hold them in her own hand.”

Lee Ji-eun turned her head. Baek Si-on was already looking at her. It was the closest their eyes had come all night — close enough that she could see the laptop’s blue light reflected in his. She asked, abruptly:

“Then what about me?”

“You’re more complicated than she is.”

“Is that a compliment?”

“She’s a character. You’re a living person.”

Lee Ji-eun went quiet. He didn’t see her as Cindy. He didn’t see her as the national little sister, or the MAMA Best Female Singer, or a piece of fire-safety equipment, or any of the other labels other people had issued her. He said she was a living person. Living meant complicated, unpredictable, dangerous, real. She looked down at her hands on her knees. The skirt had, in fact, ridden up. She didn’t pull it back.

❝

She’s a character. You’re a living person.

— the thesis of the entire evening, six words long

“There’s one more line I haven’t figured out.”

Baek Si-on looked at the screen.

“Which one?”

“‘I’ll show an expression I’ve never shown.’ That one.”

He waited. She didn’t look at the screen. She turned to face him, one hand braced on the sofa’s backrest.

“Aren’t you a Best Actor? Help me rule on it — what does that expression look like?”

Baek Si-on looked at her. She didn’t look away.

“This is character analysis, and also practicum. So you’ll have to cooperate.”

Before he could answer, Lee Ji-eun leaned in. The moment her lips arrived, the metronome in the headphones was still ticking. When they separated, she withdrew about fifteen centimeters. Her ears had gone completely red — red enough that the small patch of skin on the side of her neck had followed — but her eyes didn’t dodge. They held on him, straight. His face registered no surprise, no panic, no visible weather of any kind. He just asked:

“Does this count as instruction?”

Her nail tapped once on the sofa fabric.

“Mm. You learn too slowly… so I switched methods.”

Her voice wasn’t steady, but she finished the sentence. Baek Si-on looked at her for three seconds. In those three seconds she ran every scenario. He could stand, say “thank you, teacher, I learned a lot today,” and walk out the door. He could deflect the mood entirely. He could even pretend not to understand, and swerve the conversation back to Cindy —

“Then the teacher had better see it through.”

Her brain went blank for roughly half a second. In that half second, Baek Si-on reached out, rested his hand lightly on the backrest behind her, and erased the fifteen centimeters she had retreated. When he kissed her this time, she still had half a sentence in her head — who told you to see it through — and the sentence never made it out. This one ran longer than the first. Long enough that her hand migrated, at some unregistered moment, from the sofa fabric to the back of his neck. The project file on the laptop sat paused on the last bar of the chorus. The metronome hadn’t been turned off. In the headphone channel it went on ticking, unhurried, one beat at a time. Sixty-seven BPM. Exactly the speed of a heartbeat.

❧ ❧ ❧

After the second kiss, the room seemed quieter than before. Quiet enough to hear far-off Milan traffic, and one blurred laugh from a room somewhere below.

The first thing Lee Ji-eun did after they separated was not look at Baek Si-on. She reached over and turned the laptop screen square to herself, then pretended to inspect the project — two drags of the trackpad.

post-lesson error log · two operations, both wrong, both undone, face never changing

OP 01 — mute the drum group. Error. Undone.

OP 02 — piano track volume: maximum. Error. Undone.

Operator’s explanation, verbatim: “This does not go into the study record.” Follow-up exchange, verbatim: “Which part?” — “The part where you’re asking when you know exactly well.” — “The pedagogy?” — “Baek Si-on!” — “Understood.”

Her shoulders eased down a little. For one second. Because he added, immediately:

“Though the teacher’s instructional methods do have real personality.”

A pillow flew at him. He caught it.

“Violent pedagogy,” he assessed.

Her face had gone past controllable red. She turned to the laptop and began coiling the MIDI cable in a posture of extreme unnaturalness, with the result that the cable ended up in a knot more complicated than the one it started in.

“Shall we call it here for today?”

An “mm,” delivered without turning around. He walked to the door and paused.

“Good night.”

The door closed.

❧ ❧ ❧

After he left, Lee Ji-eun stayed on the sofa. The living-room light was still on; so was the laptop. She looked at the project file’s name: Cindy_23_draft. She moved the mouse to it, clicked Rename, and hovered over the keyboard for a while. Then she typed: Cindy_23_Milan_final. She looked at it for two seconds — and added a parenthetical:

the file system · one rename, one contradiction, both saved

BEFORE — Cindy_23_draft

AFTER — Cindy_23_Milan_final

FINAL — Cindy_23_Milan_final (not the final version)

The contradiction is the point, and the operator knows it: it is absurd on purpose, which is the only kind of honest available at 1 a.m. She looked at the name, and the corner of her mouth bent. Save. Quit Logic. Lid down.

The phone screen lit. A message from Jung Han-teuk:

“Ji-eun-ah, class finished?”

She sent back two characters’ worth:

“Finished.”

And she didn’t put the phone down. She scrolled her contacts, finger sliding, and stopped on a name: Yoo In-na. She opened the thread and typed:

“Eonni, I think I just did something very unwise.”

Read it for two seconds. Deleted. Typed again:

“Eonni, he kissed me back.”

Finger over the send button. Deleted. She closed her eyes, took a breath, and at a speed of pure surrender typed the final version and sent it before cowardice could file an objection.

Lee Ji-eun stared at that word for five seconds. In the five seconds she considered every delivery format — direct, hinted, metaphorical, rhetorical — and typed:

Ten seconds of silence preceded the last row.

She set the phone face-down on the sofa. She knew exactly what was happening in In-na’s room right now: sitting bolt upright in bed, glaring at her phone with an expression that said you think I believe that? And she wasn’t going to answer. Because there is no normal-human-language formulation of “I kissed Baek Si-on, then he kissed me back, and then we both pretended it was the practicum segment of an arrangement lesson.” The bridge dropped half a key. That was the most honest sentence tonight was getting.

She pressed her face into the pillow he had caught and put back. It carried a faint trace of laundry detergent. Nothing complex, nothing expensive. She breathed it for two extra seconds anyway. Then she took the pillow away from her face and looked at the ceiling.

“Lee Ji-eun.”

She addressed herself by her own name.

“You’re finished.”

The room did not argue. Outside, Milan kept its night on. The laptop was off, but the metronome was still going in her head. Sixty-seven BPM. Neither fast nor slow.

❝

Being twenty-three, it turns out, is not nearly as bad as advertised.

— the ceiling, final assessment of the evening

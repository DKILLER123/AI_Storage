Chapter Thirteen

The Lunch-Box Slayer Beats a Deadbeat, Stunning the Professor

April The set The machinery begins to turn

Three more days, and the tempo of the set had slipped into place. A dozen or so people, each at their own job, no one needing to be told. A broke crew had its advantages — fewer bodies, cheaper communication, faster to click.

Nobody saw her arrive. By the time Baek Jeong-hun registered it, someone was sitting beside him.

“Sis… sister-in-law?!”

“Do your job.”

Yoon Hye-ja didn’t look at him. Her eyes stayed on the monitor.

On-screen, Si-on was shooting a debt-collection scene. In a cramped rental room, the debtor crouched on the floor, hands pressed together in pleading.

A deadbeat cornered in a one-room rental

Action

The camera stays tight on Si-on. The debtor is booked off-frame.

The debtor crouches, hands clasped — “Please, just a few more days, the kid’s school fees—”

A boot goes in. The man topples, hits the wall.

He walks over, crouches, takes the man by the hair, drives a fist down at his face — stop a few centimetres short, off-camera.

“I don’t give a damn if your son goes to school? Pay what you owe! Open your mouth again and I’ll take it out of your kid!”

Every punch landing for real — well, off-camera — the cursing loud enough to shake the room.

Baek Jeong-hun sat behind the monitor and snuck a glance at his sister-in-law’s face, hardly daring to breathe.

That was it. Let a university professor watch her own spotless son, on screen, mouth full of filth, beating a common citizen. The whiplash was too much.

“Cut! Good, that’s good!”

Baek Jeong-hun called it in fast. The menace was still smeared across Si-on’s face when he turned to the actor on the floor and apologised.

“I’m sorry, sunbae-nim.”

The actor waved him off and scrambled up.

“No, no. You did great.”

Si-on nodded and headed toward the monitor to check the playback. Two steps in, he froze.

Yoon Hye-ja was sitting beside Baek Jeong-hun, watching him.

His shoulders dropped. His pace slowed. When he reached her he even looked a little guilty.

“…Mom, what are you doing here?”

“Can’t I come?”

“…You can.”

Si-on stood at her side, hands going behind his back of their own accord, then letting go, then going back up again.

Yoon Hye-ja finally raised her head and looked him over, from head to toe.

“You’ve lost weight.”

“Have I…?”

“You’ve lost weight.”

She held out the cloth tote she’d been carrying.

“Eat.”

Si-on took it and opened it. Inside was a lunch box. On top, a bowl of braised short ribs, fall-apart tender, surrounded by a few pieces of potato and carrot. In the middle, a mackerel-pike fillet fried golden on both sides. Beneath it, spinach tossed in sesame oil. And at the very bottom, a packed layer of rice, a small scatter of black sesame over the top.

It was the plainest, homiest food. But the moment the smell rose, the set-hands gnawing on cold gimbap went green in the eyes.

Si-on was about to dig in when his eye caught a figure out of the corner of it. Choi Jin-ri sat on a folding chair, script in hand, but her gaze kept drifting over. Not at the food.

At his mother.

A look, a quick glance away, down at the script as if flipping a page she wasn’t reading. Two seconds later, another look.

“Want some?”

Si-on carried the box over. Jin-ri looked at the meal right in front of her, then up at him, and shook her head.

“Thank you, no.”

“My mom’s delicious.”

“I already owe you a meal.”

She meant that evening at the unmarked shop in Yeonnam-dong — she’d intended to treat him as thanks, and in the end Si-on had beaten her to the bill. He made a small noise, went back to the plastic crate in front of his mother, and sat down with the box.

First bite, the beef. Braised to softness, it fell apart at the touch, the salty-sweet of the soy mixing with the beef’s own richness, bursting across his tongue. Second bite, the rice. Fresh, chewy, and taken together the two balanced out perfectly.

He ate quickly, chopsticks barely stopping. Yoon Hye-ja watched him wolf it down and frowned.

“Doesn’t the crew feed you?”

“They do.”

“Then why are you eating like a starving ghost?”

She pulled a tissue from her bag and tossed it onto the crate in front of him, disgusted.

“It’s because yours is delicious.”

She didn’t take the bait. She just watched him eat.

Si-on got a few bites in and looked up.

“Did you eat?”

“Yes.”

“At home?”

“Mm.”

“What did you make?”

“Same as this.”

Si-on looked down at the box, then back at her. Yoon Hye-ja wasn’t looking at him. She was watching the playback on the monitor. The scene where Si-on grabbed the debtor’s hair and drove a fist downward. The frame held on that fierce face.

She looked for a moment.

“You acted well.”

Si-on blinked.

“…Thank you, Mom.”

“Eat.”

He went back to it. When he’d finished, he closed the lid, tucked it into the tote, and stood. Yoon Hye-ja rose, took the bag.

“I’m going.”

“Let me walk you—”

“No.”

She took a few steps toward the alley mouth, then stopped and turned back, looking at him.

“Take care of yourself.”

And went on her way.

April The set The fight the camera wanted

After she was gone, the machinery of the set began to turn faster. Sang-hun and Yeon-hui’s story was being stitched together, piece by piece, in the old lanes and broken rooms of Ansan. The crew started to notice that these two actors were like two monsters without an off switch.

Si-on’s fight scenes grew more vicious. The light behind Jin-ri’s eyes grew darker. They barely spoke. But the moment they stood in front of a lens, the hopelessness of two people in the sludge bit together with a perfect seam.

So time compressed.

Days on set grew longer, from the initial ten hours a day to fourteen, fifteen. On the fifteenth day, at one in the morning, in a stairwell of an old complex in Ansan, the lamps burning dim.

Tonight’s night shoot was the emotional peak of the whole film. Sang-hun’s father had lived all his life under the guilt of having destroyed his wife and child. Beaten and cursed by his son daily, the blood-soaked sins he’d tried so hard to bury kept getting dragged back into the light. Finally the old man reached the breaking point, and in that grimy bathroom he chose to end it, slitting his wrists.

Si-on pushed the door open and saw the blood pooled across the floor — and in that instant the armour he’d built from cursing and fists shattered to pieces.

“Ssibal, what right do you have to die?!”

Swearing as he ran, he carried his blood-soaked father on his back through the midnight streets. The reason was so very “Sang-hun.”

“You haven’t paid back what you owe my mother and my sister! You’ll live carrying that guilt the rest of your life! I won’t let you die — you don’t even have the right to die!”

But at the ER, when the doctor said the blood loss was too great and he’d need a transfusion, Sang-hun fell apart completely. He grabbed the doctor by the collar, eyes red, veins standing out on his neck.

“Take mine! Drain my blood and give it to him! Ssibal! Save him!”

Yeon-hui’s defences collapsed too. Because at the dinner table there’d been no meat. The crazy father with his post-war trauma had suddenly flown into a rage and overturned the table. Doenjang-jjigae went everywhere. Yeon-hui had had enough. At that breaking point she screamed the words she’d kept bottled up her whole life at her father.

“Why don’t you just die?!”

What came back was her father, grabbing a boning knife from the kitchen, eyes red, coming for her. Yeon-hui didn’t even have time to put her shoes on. Barefoot, stepping over shards of broken dish and spilt soup, she ran like a madwoman from that suffocating house.

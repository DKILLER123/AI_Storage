Chapter Twenty-Nine

The Tune Smiles, the Words Weep

The same morning After the walk-out LOEN · lift lobby, then the office IU on the credit. A deep alley. The tune smiles.

When Han-teuk pushed the fire door at the end of the corridor, Baek Si-on was already standing at the lift.

Not waiting for him. Not hanging him.

Just waiting for the lift.

The floor light between three and four, jumping slow.

Han-teuk took two quick steps.

“Si-on—”

“If you’re here to talk me back, don’t open your mouth.”

The line caught Han-teuk on the starting block.

In his head he dumped all three prepared speeches into the recycle bin.

“I’ll ask you one thing.”

Si-on didn’t say “ask.” Didn’t say “don’t.”

Han-teuk took it as leave to speak.

He came up beside Si-on, also facing the lift door. Shoulder to shoulder, both watching the number crawl.

“Right now you need her to write. Correct?”

Si-on didn’t pick it up.

“A lyric credit that says IU, and a lyric credit that says anyone else, are not the same concept.”

The number jumped from four to six.

Si-on of course had the count.

Way Back Home, in the time he remembered, had not made a splash after release.

What actually blew it open was short video.

Dance covers on a certain app, BGM reworks, the challenge that left the circle, wave after wave fishing it up from the bottom.

But this was 2014.

No short video.

No that app, no TikTok, nothing.

Then how did a newcomer’s song get heard?

Company push? He had no company.

Music shows? He didn’t even have a proper management team.

Luck? Last life’s luck had already been spent on the World Cup.

So only one answer left.

The lyricist’s name itself was the publicity.

IU, thirty-three digital number ones. Every song she put out came with a search-trend attached.

If her name sat in the lyric credit, the song would have a topic before it even dropped.

Good wine still feared a deep alley.

Never mind that this bottle hadn’t even been opened.

The lift arrived.

A ding. The door opened.

Two people inside, badges on their chests, folders in their hands, looking like they were going to another floor for a meeting.

Si-on stood in front of the door.

Didn’t go in.

One second.

Two seconds.

Han-teuk glanced at Si-on’s face, turned at once, bowed to the two in the lift.

“Sorry. We’ll take the next one.”

The man inside looked at them once, hit close.

The door shut.

The corridor quiet again.

Han-teuk straightened, let a breath out in secret.

“Come on.”

He put a hand on Si-on’s shoulder, pushed back a little.

Si-on didn’t move.

Han-teuk added half a measure of force.

“Lowering your head once isn’t shame. I do it eight hundred times a day. Do I look shamed?”

He pointed at his own face.

“Fine. Still pretty handsome.”

Si-on gave him a look from the side.

The subtext of that look was very clear: the last three words can go.

But Han-teuk could feel he could be moved now.

The two of them started back.

Two steps.

“Walk slow.”

Han-teuk’s feet stopped.

“Ah?”

Si-on both hands in his pockets, the stride unhurried.

“Don’t let her think I came back easy.”

Han-teuk’s feet stopped all the way.

He turned, looking at Si-on with a very complicated face.

This sentence shape.

This rhythm.

Three minutes ago, at the office door, he had also heard a line with the exact same structure — “Walk slow. Don’t let him think I’m in a hurry.”

✿ ✿ ✿

When Han-teuk pushed the door he made a point of keeping it light.

Si-on came in behind him.

Lee Ji-eun at the desk, the look coming off the screen, stopping on Si-on’s face less than a second, then going back:

“What’s the song called?”

“Way Back Home.”

The corner of Lee Ji-eun’s mouth moved.

Way Back Home.

Very straight.

Straight enough to be a little rustic.

She opened her mouth, wanted to say something, then her own titles flashed through her head:

Lost Child.

Nagging.

Good Day.

The mouth closed again.

She pushed the chair back a little, pulled a notebook from the edge of the desk, turned to a blank page, picked up the pen.

“What image are you trying to put across?”

Image.

When Si-on heard the word, the first thing in his head was a bitter laugh.

A man who had copied a song, asked “what artistic conception are you trying to put across.”

The model answer to this question, he didn’t know. The original writer knew. The original writer might still be in high school.

But he could not say he didn’t know.

He could only, from the feeling that melody had given him in memory, from the pictures that had come up in his head in last life’s countless late nights looping the track, from the only grasp a man who had acted more than ten years had on the two characters for “emotion,” make something up.

“Roughly…”

Si-on stopped, put the words in order.

“Wherever across oceans, wherever over mountains. Even if the world turns over, the place I finally belong is you.”

Finished.

Lee Ji-eun’s pen didn’t move. She looked at Si-on, blinked once.

“A love song?”

Si-on watched her face, the head running fast.

This expression he had seen last life in countless table reads.

When you talked a role and the direction went off, the director wore this face.

Understood.

Not that a love song was bad.

Love was the eternal subject. From break-up to crush to heat to long-distance, written back and forth for decades, people still listened, people still cried.

But this song’s weather was Tropical House, the temperature of a walk on the evening beach in summer.

Put “wherever across oceans, the place I belong is you” straight onto it, and the two things pinched together would become bubblegum.

And bubblegum did not need IU to write it.

Grab any trainee in a convenience store and they could write it.

“This ‘you’ is wide. It can be a person. It can be a past self. It can even be a place that will take you.”

Lee Ji-eun’s pen-tip landed on the paper.

Didn’t write.

But it landed.

Si-on took the signal in.

Went on.

“The scene is roughly this—”

“A young person working in Seoul, overtime done, two in the morning, walking the street, empty inside.”

“Because he doesn’t know what he’s fighting this hard for, and he doesn’t know where ‘home’ even is.”

“The rental is a place to sleep, not home. The company is a place to clock in, not home. Even the little town he grew up in, when he goes back, feels strange.”

“So he’s looking. Looking for himself, or for a place that makes him feel he’s arrived. But he turns and turns, and he has walked very far.”

“In the end he finds the way home isn’t at any endpoint. It’s in the moment he’s willing to stop.”

The pen in Lee Ji-eun’s hand started to move.

At the top of the blank page, one word.

Lee Ji-eun · notebook

Homeward.

Then a parenthesis beside it, a line of small characters inside.

Si-on across a desk couldn’t read what she’d written.

She finished that line, lifted her head:

“Urban loneliness?”

Si-on didn’t nod. Didn’t shake his head.

Because he wasn’t sure whether what he had just said was even right.

What the original writer of this song had meant to say might not have been this at all. It might have been a simple long-distance love song.

He himself had stuffed too many things into it that did not belong to the song.

But Lee Ji-eun was already writing.

Wrote about ten-odd seconds, stopped, turned the notebook, read it herself, turned it back, kept writing.

“The weather of the tune is light, right?”

“Right.”

“But the core you just described is lonely.”

Lee Ji-eun laid the pen on the notebook, leaned back.

the tune, and the words

The tune smiles. The words have to cry. The listener bounces all the way through, and only when they turn back to the lyrics do they find this person has been crying the whole time.

That’s what you want?

This time Si-on nodded.

Because he suddenly felt that perhaps what this song truly wanted to say was this.

Maybe not the original writer’s meaning.

But when this song lived once more in this time, through his mouth and her pen, what it ought to say was this.

Chapter One Hundred Eighty

The Four-Leaf Clover Gets the Final Say

January 1 · New Year’s Day, dumplings to clover An army-stew summit · one physics lecture with no survivors · two afternoon tickets to Canada · a schedule rewritten under the table · a lawn that declines to testify Koreatown, Los Angeles → Melrose Avenue → the Four Seasons, Beverly Hills, back garden A mother answers a romantic question with magnetospheric physics and loses the room; a singer books a miracle for two; a manager’s schedule dies in his throat; a marketing director’s smile freezes on live microphones; and science is respectfully overruled by a lawn.

The New Year’s dinner was set for Koreatown.

Koreatown in Los Angeles has a strange quality about it. You are, on paper, in America. But push the door open — the smell of kimchi stew, the fat spitting on the grill plates, the staff’s fluent Korean patter — and you’d swear you’d just turned a corner into some back alley in Gangnam.

Baek Eun-ah had booked the table days ahead. None of the show crew were invited. Those people had worked all night; they were owed a New Year’s Eve with their own families.

What remained was Baek Si-on, Yoon Hye-ja, Choi Jin-ri, Baek Eun-ah, Park Ji-hun — and Scooter, who had invited himself and refused all appeals.

Scooter sat down and studied the menu with the gravity of a man defusing something.

“I need someone I trust to tell me which of these dishes will not kill me.”

“Army stew,” Baek Eun-ah said.

He examined the menu photo — the slices of lunch meat, the cheese.

“Are you sure this isn’t American food that enlisted in the Korean army and came back like this?”

“An accurate reading.”

Scooter was silent for two seconds.

“Then I’ll have that.”

The food came fast: grilled beef, tofu soup, cheese ribs, spicy rice cakes, bibimbap in a stone bowl, and one enormous, steaming pot of army stew at the center of it all.

Korean food in Los Angeles is, some of the time, genuinely better than the Seoul original. Not because the chefs have unlocked some secret overseas technique. Mostly it’s the ingredient conditions — beef with enough fat on it, seafood close enough to the boats, vegetables that never run out. Add the gentle fusion every immigrant kitchen builds to please local palates, and you get a kind of shameless, no-rules abundance.

Yoon Hye-ja picked up a piece of grilled beef with her chopsticks and ate slowly. Baek Eun-ah was starving and eating head-down with real battle spirit — the same spirit of the day Baek Si-on had hauled her off the edge of unemployment and deposited her in front of a grill. The family motto — when the sky falls, feed the belly first — was, clearly, being faithfully upheld.

Choi Jin-ri sat diagonal across from Baek Si-on. She had wanted to sit closer. But Yoon Hye-ja was here. So she had, with great good sense, taken the one seat that let her advance to watching him serve the side dishes, or retreat to pretending she was simply very invested in her soup.

Tactical positioning, you could call it.

Halfway through the meal, the television on the restaurant wall started playing a travel promo.

The footage was the northern Canadian snowfields. Under a deep-blue night sky, the aurora spread over the ice and the black pines like a slow curtain of green gauze — green, violet, and once a single thread of pink — churning soundlessly, doubled on the frozen lake below. A low-voiced English narration kept murmuring something about a once-in-a-lifetime sight.

The restaurant was loud.

Choi Jin-ri was snagged by the screen in a single glance. Chopsticks between her teeth, eyes fixed, unblinking — the exact face of a kid passing a shop window where a toy she can’t afford is sitting on display.

Baek Si-on followed her gaze. On the screen, a caption was surfacing.

Koreatown · the restaurant wall TV

Canada Aurora Journey — Meet the Light at the Edge of the World

The narration keeps murmuring once in a lifetime. The next table keeps arguing about dipping sauces. Only one person at the corner table is watching.

He looked at Choi Jin-ri. Still watching.

Finally he turned to Yoon Hye-ja.

“Mom — would you like to go see the aurora?”

Choi Jin-ri turned her head. Carefully. She did not dare turn it quickly; the celebration on her face was not yet house-trained.

Yoon Hye-ja glanced at the television.

“The aurora is a luminescent phenomenon produced when charged particles from space enter Earth’s magnetosphere and collide with atoms in the upper atmosphere.”

“Which means it is strongly governed by solar storms and magnetic fields. It is not visible every day.”

Baek Si-on: “……”

Choi Jin-ri: “……”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

Scooter spoke no Korean. But reading the triple silence, he concluded that Mother Baek had just said something extremely formidable.

“Meaning: you’re not going?” Baek Si-on asked.

“Mm. Standing out in the freezing cold all night, losing sleep — my joints would never forgive me.”

Choi Jin-ri lowered her head and worked very hard at looking like someone who simply found her soup delicious.

Baek Si-on thought for a moment, then looked at Baek Eun-ah.

“Book me two afternoon tickets to Canada.”

“Done.”

It took Baek Eun-ah a beat to catch up.

“Two?”

“Mm.”

“What about ME?!”

He looked at her.

“You won’t keep your aunt company?”

Baek Eun-ah: “……”

With the cap of unfilial behavior nailed down like that, she could not locate a single word of rebuttal. She looked across the table at the girl pretending, heroically, not to be listening — then lowered her head and stabbed her rice twice, hard.

Yoon Hye-ja raised the warm water at her elbow, took one unhurried sip, and said nothing at all.

“WAIT!”

Scooter could finally sit still no longer.

“What is happening here? Would somebody consider my feelings, please?”

Baek Eun-ah translated the whole affair for him in fast English. His face changed on the spot.

“No way, Baek!”

“The Under Armour pop-up needs you there — your own collab line, in person. And the CJ dumpling endorsement has New Year’s events. These need your actual body.”

Baek Si-on looked at him and asked:

“Tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow Billboard updates, and the TIME reporter sits down with you.”

“Day after?”

“The day after, you also have—”

Scooter pulled out his phone and went scrolling through the calendar, about to read on —

Baek Si-on wasn’t listening to him anymore. He had turned to look at Choi Jin-ri.

Every item out of Scooter’s mouth, her eyes dimmed one more degree. By the end she had simply lowered her head and started stabbing the rice cakes on her plate.

“Just tomorrow, then.”

Scooter’s sentence died in his throat.

Baek Si-on wasn’t looking at him. He was still looking at her.

“Is that all right?”

Choi Jin-ri’s chopsticks stopped. She raised her head, slowly.

His expression was no different from any ordinary day. Not tender. Not deliberate. None of the gazing-into-your-eyes production values.

“It’s all right.”

Choi Jin-ri finally let the laugh out.

Scooter sighed, clutched his phone, and started thumbing painful messages to the brand teams.

❝

American capital, Asian family duty, a girl’s one wish, and a New Year’s dinner table — four things dropped into one pot, and messier than any army stew.

— Koreatown, the corner table, January 1

Baek Eun-ah, meanwhile, was quietly reworking her own calendar under the table.

Baek Eun-ah · the calendar rewrite · typed under the table

Jan 2 — the aunt: mine. The cousin: Choi Jin-ri’s. The aurora: Canada’s. Scooter: collapsed.

Priorities unchanged. Logistics merciless.

On the wall TV, the Canadian travel promo was still running, the green curtain of light spread across the whole night sky.

Choi Jin-ri sneaked one more glance at it.

This time she wasn’t just gazing longingly through the glass. Somebody had already plucked that light out of the television and written it into her tomorrow.

❧ ❧ ❧

By two in the morning, the grill joint’s lights were going out one by one. The five-person New Year’s dinner had run just over an hour, and it had solved exactly nothing and settled everything.

Back at the hotel, Baek Si-on said only “See you tomorrow,” and everyone drifted off to their rooms.

Ten o’clock in the morning. Los Angeles, Melrose Avenue.

The Under Armour pop-up had taken the entire corner storefront. The facade was matte black, and dead center, in white minimalist type, sat two lines.

Melrose Avenue · the pop-up facade

UNDER ARMOUR × BAEK SI-ON

GLOBAL LAUNCH. JAN 1, 2015.

Nothing else on the wall. The queue arrived before the doors did.

The line already ran from the door to the corner of the block. And almost none of it looked like Under Armour’s traditional customer profile: local skate kids, fashion bloggers, music bloggers, and a handful of young Hollywood actors in sunglasses with their cap brims pulled low.

It was absurd, honestly. A brand built on professional athletics, wearing — courtesy of one man’s personal collab — the hunger-marketing mystique of a streetwear drop.

Outside the pop-up’s glass, a small media pen had been tacked together for the morning. Baek Si-on stood at the brand backdrop.

He was in his own line today: the black technical shell, a gray-white hoodie underneath, the matching track pants, and the low-top trainers that hadn’t even shipped at scale yet. The whole outfit was cleanly cut. A sports label’s worst fear is the personal-trainer look — but standing there, Baek Si-on pressed the technical edge flat into something street.

The shutters never stopped. The flashbulbs went off in steady beats.

“Baek! Over here!”

“This way!”

“A little to your left!”

Baek Si-on turned cooperatively, angle by angle.

The Under Armour marketing director stood off to the side, smiling the smile of a man who has just watched the quarterly report grow legs and walk itself into his office. He had believed the collab would sell decently. He had not believed this.

The night before, right after the concert, Baek Si-on’s selfie video had gone out — this exact hoodie in frame. At two in the morning, the site’s reservation page crashed once. By seven, the customer-service inbox was stuffed with size questions. And now the queue spilling down Melrose Avenue was the most direct answer available to science.

The photo call ran about ten minutes. Then a short group interview. The questions clustered on the design philosophy and his read of sports fashion, and the answers were terse, as ever.

“Comfort is the whole feature.”

“If a piece of clothing isn’t comfortable, no design in the world saves it.”

One reporter pushed: “Do you even work out?”

“Does eating count?”

The press bench laughed. The marketing director’s smile froze in place and stayed there.

❧ ❧ ❧

Same hour. Beverly Hills, the Four Seasons, back garden.

The January sun was as generous as ever, and the lawn had been mown into green velvet, its edges straight as if a ruler had been laid down first.

Choi Jin-ri crouched on this perfect lawn, fingers carefully parting the grass, flipping through it leaf by leaf.

“Three leaves…”

“Three…”

“Still three…”

The aftershock of Professor Yoon’s hardcore physics lecture was still running. Every word of it had said the same thing: the aurora is not a thing you simply get to see. Science is cruel.

But Choi Jin-ri had no intention of handing her hard-won day alone to cold probability. If science refused to guarantee it, then only one branch remained: mysticism.

The four-leaf clover.

She’d heard it since she was small — find one, and luck finds you. The theory’s scientific footing sits, admittedly, at roughly the same altitude as the aurora’s romance: both filed under things humans tell themselves. But what Choi Jin-ri needed right now was, precisely, something to tell herself.

“…Are there even any four-leaf clovers in Los Angeles lawns?”

She shifted her squat-numbed foot and moved on to the next clump.

“Clover. Come out for a second.”

She was negotiating with the lawn now, in a small voice.

“I’m not here to wreck the ecosystem. I only want to borrow a little luck.”

The lawn did not respond.

Very American. Free, but indifferent.

The hotel gardener passed at a distance and stopped when he saw the Asian girl crouched intently over the flowerbed edges, flipping leaves. He debated going over to remind her that guests weren’t allowed to pick the plantings — and then decided he couldn’t bring himself to. Besides, she wasn’t actually pulling anything.

At a clump she’d long since lost count of, her hand suddenly stopped.

The little plant under her fingertip had small leaves, crowded in among the green.

One.

Two.

Three.

A fourth.

Choi Jin-ri’s breathing stopped. Then she leaned in even closer.

Four. Really four.

“Found it!”

Like she’d unearthed a national treasure, she yanked out her phone and got a photo. Then she looked at the tiny four-leaf clover on the screen, and her eyes curved into crescents.

❝

Tomorrow night, the aurora will be there. It will. Science doesn’t get the final say — the four-leaf clover does.

— a lawn in Beverly Hills, January 1, mid-morning

A Field Guide to Korean Honorifics

Seoul runs on deference. Before the lights come up and the cameras roll, every name in this novel bends to a dozen unspoken rules of age, rank, and intimacy. Use this guide like a cheat sheet — the honorifics stay in the dialogue, and the meanings live here.

How to Read the Cards

Each term is given in romanised form, with hangul beside it and a quick gloss. A meaning line tells you what it roughly translates to; a use line tells you when it appears on the page of Seoul.

01 Honorific Suffixes

-ssi 씨

Mr. / Ms. Polite address to an equal or a near-stranger; the everyday safety net when you don’t know someone’s title.

Baek Si-on-ssi — the politest way a stranger names him.

-nim 님

Honoured one. The most respectful suffix. Attached to titles, names, or relatives to show deep deference.

Sajang-nim (boss) · Abeo-nim (father) · Seonbae-nim (senior).

-ya / -a 야 / 아

Hey you. Familiar, sometimes sharp. Used for juniors, children, or very close friends — the blunt end of the politeness scale.

“Ya, little thing.” How Si-on first summons the girl.

02 Age & Seniority

Oppa 오빠

Older brother. A woman’s word for an older man — brother, boyfriend, or a male senior she is close to. Warm, slightly sisterly.

“Jong-han Oppa.” Ji-eun’s easy manner with her manager.

Hyung 형

Older brother. A man’s word for an older man he respects — male friend, senior, or colleague. The masculine mirror of “oppa.”

Si-on uses it for his debt-collecting seniors, even when he doesn’t like them.

Nuna 누나

Older sister. A man’s word for an older woman — sister, cousin, or a female senior he feels at ease with.

“Nuna,” the affectionate way a young man addresses an older woman.

Eonni 언니

Older sister. A woman’s word for an older woman — girlfriends, older trainees, or female seniors in the industry.

“Ji-eun eonni,” Kim Se-jeong writes in her letter.

Sunbae 선배

Senior. Someone ahead of you in school, work, or a company — by graduation, hire date, or debut.

“The sunbae-nim will see you now.”

Hubae 후배

Junior. Someone behind you; it carries an obligation of care in return for the senior’s guidance.

“He’s a good hubae.”

03 Workplace Rank

Sajang-nim 사장님

President / Boss. The head of a company, addressed with the honorific suffix. In the novel, the boss of the debt-collection office.

“Sajang-nim, this one’s clean.”

Siljang-nim 실장님

Team leader / Director. The department head — the person who actually runs the day-to-day.

Bae Jong-han is Ji-eun’s siljang at LOEN.

Daepyo 대표

CEO / Representative. The official head of an agency or label.

Used when people talk about the people who sign the cheques.

04 Family Address

Samchon 삼촌

Uncle. Literally a father’s younger brother — and, in the novel, how Si-on addresses his uncle Baek Jeong-hun.

“Samchon, the first scene’s wrong.”

Abeo-nim 아버님

Father (honoured). A respectful, slightly formal way to address one’s father, or another person’s father.

The poetically formal address, even for a father like Si-on’s.

Eomeo-nim 어머님

Mother (honoured). The equally formal word for one’s mother or another’s.

The respectful form that keeps a little distance.

Ajumeoni 아주머니

Auntie / Ma’am. A polite word for a married middle-aged woman — often the shopkeeper or the neighbor.

“Ajumeoni, open up.”

05 Social Address

Ajumma 아줌마

Auntie. A casual, sometimes slightly blunt word for a middle-aged married woman. Softer than it sounds, sharper than “ajumeoni.”

“The ajumma at the fish stall.”

Ajeossi 아저씨

Mister / Uncle. A casual word for a middle-aged man — the taxi driver, the shop man, the stranger on the street.

“Ajeossi, excuse me.”

Agassi 아가씨

Young lady / Miss. For an unmarried young woman; can be polite or a little old-fashioned.

“You a young miss, are you?”

Kkoma 꼬마

Little one. Affectionate (or teasing) for a child or a small, young girl.

How Si-on taunts the girl he’s sure isn’t a threat.

06 Recurring Expressions

Ssi-bal 씨발

The F-word. Korea’s most common curse. Rough, loaded, and everywhere in the unvarnished dialogue of the debt-collection world.

“Ssi-bal.” Si-on, waking from the nightmare.

Aegyo 애교

Acting cute. The deliberate, winsome charm idols deploy on camera — and the very thing Si-on’s past self once lived on.

The wink and the aegyo of a boy who hasn’t been born yet.

Fighting 화이팅

Go for it / You got this. The rallying cry of encouragement, borrowed from English but Korean to the bone.

What a fan would shout at a struggling trainee.

Gwenchana 괜찮아

It’s okay. The quiet reassurance that winds through the quieter moments — and the cry of the song that breaks the girl’s heart.

“It’ll be alright.” — the last line of Someday.

Note on spacing: Korean names in this book use the family name first (Baek Si-on, Lee Ji-eun, Kim Se-jeong) in keeping with the source. Honorifics are never translated away — they are the colour of the scene. Hold them in your ear and the whole world turns on who calls whom oppa.

November 23 · evening, before airtime The stage outfit is already hanging Los Angeles · the Nokia Theatre · the private dressing room Regal from a distance. Not a museum theft up close. That is the brief, met.

When Baek Si-on walked into his private dressing room, Park Ji-hun had the stage outfit already hanging.

The KING stage outfit, on the throne — one red beam, hidden gold.

The overall visual: from afar, the authority of a throne. Up close — not a museum artefact on the run.

Changed, Baek Si-on went out to join the dancers. The eight were already in their black stage kit — a hybrid species, honor guard crossed with street crew.

The Black dancer whistled the moment he saw him.

“Damn.”

He looked Baek Si-on up and down, grinned, and stuck a thumb up.

“Now you really look like a king.”

The Latina dancer rattled her wrist — the coins, presumably, still in her mental pocket.

“Tonight we kneel for gold and glory.”

The whole room cracked up. The sixteen ounces had obviously not been spent in vain: since that afternoon, the single-knee drop had a new name in their mouths — the golden knee.

Baek Si-on stepped into their middle and put out his right hand. Eight dancers closed around it. Nine hands stacked.

He looked at them.

“Let’s go.”

Nine hands pushed up together. Broke. Everyone to their marks.

November 23 · night House lights down, season open Los Angeles · the Nokia Theatre · the floor A newly throned man who has not yet announced his tax rates, watching the opening act.

By the time he slipped back into the artists’ floor, the ceremony was ready to begin. The seats filled tier by tier — stars, producers, actors and label executives up front; fans, guests, media and camera-bait industry people behind. Baek Si-on took his assigned seat, while Baek Eun-ah and Scooter Braun watched from the backstage monitor area, where the director was cutting between opening positions.

Scooter studied the black-gold stage outfit on the monitor and nodded, satisfied.

“Very good.”

“Good how?”

“Like a man newly seated on a throne who hasn’t got around to announcing his tax rates yet.”

An odd review.

The lights died. The screams arrived a step ahead of them. The big screen lit — and Taylor took the stage, seated inside a set dressed as a Victorian parlour, and opened her mouth:

the AMAs · the opening performance · Taylor Swift — “Blank Space”

A Victorian parlour, built centre stage. A male-model dance partner. Lights that turn cold without warning.

“Nice to meet you, where you been—”

Her face switches expressions at the speed of a shutter — candy-sweet one second, and the next, the sense that somewhere in this pretty house a knife is quietly dripping.

The song is a bomb wrapped in sugar: she plays the media’s “mad woman” in the first person, absorbs every demonization, amplifies it into absurdity, and sings it saccharine. The media call her unstable — so she turned the instability into the opening show.

The applause and cheering lifted the roof. The host came up to warm the room, and the American pace took over — jokes, jabs, audience cams, artists laughing on cue. He mined tonight’s running gags: the sales velocity of Taylor’s new album, the social-media voting, and “all the people who want to become a legend tonight.” When the camera swept across Baek Si-on, the room screamed again; he raised a hand a few degrees, and the director granted him exactly two seconds. Two seconds is enough to set a barrage on fire — and enough to keep a little mystery.

Then: three awards handed out, six acts performed. Country. Pop. Rock. R&B. That is the AMAs all over — none of the Grammys’ academic chill; what it wants is a room that never stops boiling, and one Tweetable image every few minutes.

Finally the host came back to centre.

“Next — the 2014 American Music Award for New Artist of the Year.”

The nominee reel played. Names flashed; the fandom sections surged in turn. The presenter opened the envelope. Paused. Looked up.

“The 2014 AMAs New Artist of the Year…”

The camera toured the candidates. Suspense, professionally milked. The presenter smiled, and said the name:

“Baek Si-on!”

The cheering hit from all four sides like a wall.

The AMAs sometimes publishes the vote split on the big screen after announcing. This time, nothing was published — because of the risk of national heartbreak. A twenty-million-vote lead, put on that screen, would have turned tomorrow’s headline from “Korean singer wins” into “Have Asian fandoms occupied the American awards?” Bad for the emotional stability of a nation.

Baek Si-on rose through the cheering and the Legend BGM. He nodded to the artists left and right, then lifted the hem of the black-gold coat — the coat was long, and he had no intention of marking his first AMAs win by tripping on his own hem and performing a Crown Prince Sado faceplant for the entire planet. The camera tracked him down the aisle; “Legend!” from one section, “King!” from another. On stage he took the trophy.

The AMAs statuette and the Venice Volpi Cup do not belong to the same aesthetic system. Charitably: modern. Less charitably: a crystal display stand from IKEA. His hands were perfectly steady taking it.

At the standing microphone he looked out first. The lights were too bright to read faces — just a sea of people and a scatter of camera dots. He began.

“Thank you, AMAs. Thank you to everyone who voted for me.”

The room quieted.

“I came here from Korea as an actor. And then one song — Legend — brought me to this stage.”

“Tonight, people are calling me Best New Artist. The way I see it, ‘new’ was never about being young. It means the heart is still warm, the appetite for learning is still open, and it is still possible to set out all over again.”

He looked down at the trophy.

“So I intend to keep walking, and set out again.”

“Thank you to Scooter, to SB Projects, to my team, to my family — and to every fan of mine, at home and abroad, who likes what I do.”

At last, into the lens:

“And to all of you who stayed up all night voting… please go get some sleep.”

The laughter was bigger than the cheer.

His eyes lower a degree; the voice stays pressed, the tail of each line sinking, carrying a grain milled fresh out of exhaustion.

Wide-medium cut: the eight dancers enter at last, flanking the throne — cutting steps on the left, shoulder-drops on the right, their centres of gravity always beneath his.

The camera arcs around the side. Throne, red drapes, gold filigree, black silhouettes — stacked layer on layer, until the stage finally has a full geography: not a man singing in a chair, but a man seated at the centre of a throne while everyone and everything re-orders itself around him.

“But I will never run when destiny comes”

“I’m dipping my hand in gold”

His right hand rises — and in the red, a gold spotlight lands exactly across the back of it. Before the chorus, the drums empty out for one instant. The room holds his voice and one falling breath. Nothing else.

“It’s good to be king”

Every light opens. Red-gold floods the frame from the back of the stage. The dancers converge — four flanking the throne, four half-kneeling in the low front — arms opening and closing, an invisible frame being hung around him. He stays seated, chin up, and takes the lens head-on.

“It’s good to be king”

The second line lands and the court comes alive around him — shoulder, hand, turn, press — nothing complex, every beat locked to the strobe. The denser they move, the stiller he sits; the stronger the contrast, the more it looks like an enthronement. The crowd’s roar arrives half a beat late — not cold: suppressed. No fire pillars, no sprint blocking, and still the Nokia’s breathing has been slowed by force.

The front rows — a handful of producers — started clapping first. Then the house. Then the fan sections detonated upward: “King!” “Baek!” “Legend!” The director cut to the seats.

Ariana Grande clapped with both hands at her chest, eyes bright as a limited-edition Pokémon. Taylor, across the floor, leaned forward slightly, watching the throne with an expression worth studying: she had just turned the media’s “mad woman” into an opening show; he had turned the word King into an enthronement liturgy. Two people who take the labels the world sticks on them and hand them back as weapons.

the AMAs · the KING stage · part two — the walk

The first chorus releases, and the drums are pulled out from under everything. The red-gold dims layer by layer until only the relief behind the throne still glows. And then he rises from the throne — and the eight dancers fall back half a step as one.

Next beat: they split into two files in front of him and go down on one knee. The camera finds a low side angle. He steps out and walks the corridor between them, the black-gold hem sliding past his legs, heads bowed, red light paving a narrow road across the floor — and the stage’s power flips: a moment ago the throne carried him; now everything follows.

The dancers rise and snap into the wedge behind his back. The drums press in again. He cuts the upper-body set with them — arms opening from the chest, shoulders pressing, the body turning, feet half a beat off to the right — clean in a way nobody braced for.

“I’ve been climbing up all these mountains for so long.”

The lighting shifts red to dark gold. Every step he takes, the formation behind him changes its angle once.

“But I will never run when destiny comes.”

The drums gain weight. He stops dead centre; the dancers close from both sides — eight shoulder-lines level, arms cutting forward in unison — and he runs the identical move at the centre of it. Nine bodies, one blade line, the first true formation dance of the night. The lights flash three times. The screams climb again. American audiences may not know the Korean grammar of a blade formation — but uniformity reads in any language, and so does momentum, and so does the reversal of the throne’s occupant stepping into his own ranks.

“It’s good to be king.”

Every light up: red, gold, white pressing the stage flat. He advances from centre; the dancers split into two wings behind him. First beat, the hand rises. Second, the turn. Third, the shoulder drops. Fourth, every dancer lands at once.

“It’s good to be king.”

The last note falls and the drums stop dead. He stands at the exact centre — mic hand down at his side, the other hand rising slightly, palm down. The eight dancers hold a staggered ring around him: half-kneeling in front, leaning back behind, arms flung open at the flanks. The throne behind. The red-gold relief burning at maximum on the big screen. Every light in the building freezes — end pose. One second later, the venue detonates: screams, applause, whistles, layer over layer, burying the last ring of the PA. He lowers his hand and bows with the eight — not a king saluting subjects, but actors taking a curtain call — which pulls one more cheer out of the room.

What people would actually remember were the moments that couldn’t be faked.

the big screen · the montage

Old television footage. Music shows from the sixties and seventies. Screaming teenagers. A host with a microphone, frozen mid-introduction.

Dick Clark.

The founder of the American Music Awards — the man who built, in 1973, an award driven entirely by the market, by sales, by the public’s vote, and aimed it straight at the Grammys’ academy taste. The Grammys say art. The AMAs say the people bought it. Two logics warring for decades, neither able to finish the other — two rivers, mirroring each other. None of which is tonight’s point.

“Next, we present a very special honour.”

On the screen: Dick Clark Award for Excellence.

A polite first layer of applause went round. And to hold up the weight of a “first-ever,” the organizers had brought out America’s national-treasure queen of soul — Diana Ross.

The moment she walked out from the back of the stage, the house rose. Some people make the lights seem smarter just by arriving. Diana Ross reached the microphone, glanced at her card, and looked up.

“Tonight, this award goes to a young woman who changed the rules of modern pop music.”

The camera cut to Taylor. The cheering had already started. Taylor’s hand came up over her mouth — she had clearly worked out what was happening.

“She is the only artist in history with three albums that sold more than a million copies in their first week.”

“She has written her growth into her songs, and defined her era with her own pen.”

“Tonight, the first-ever Dick Clark Award for Excellence goes to……”

A pause. The whole building stopped breathing.

“Taylor Swift.”

The applause broke like weather.

Taylor’s first move was not toward the stage. She turned, wrapped up Selena — barely off stage, tears barely dry — and kissed her cheek. Selena laughed mid-cry, and the house cheered. Everyone understood: best friends; she had just made the entire room weep; a kiss on the cheek is entirely normal. Then Taylor reached past Selena, grabbed Sam Smith, and gave him a cheek kiss too. More screaming — also understandable: Sam Smith, the male Adele, a voice like all the rain in Britain landing on one man’s windows; friends too; emotions high; perfectly reasonable. Sam beamed like a British shorthair caught mid-purr by a surprise kiss.

The kiss offensive had put the whole room into a loose, party-state of mind.

Then Taylor turned her head.

Baek Si-on was standing there, applauding. Their eyes met. His applause slowed half a beat, and he stepped backward, half a step.

Taylor saw him retreat — and laughed. The laugh’s meaning needed no translation: what are you hiding from? It won’t help.

It couldn’t help. He was out of room: the chair behind him; Taylor to his left; an AMAs official camera rig to his right. It occurred to him, absurdly, that facing eight dancers going down on one knee had not felt this dangerous.

She leaned in and kissed his cheek, lightly. Polite. The textbook American award-show cheek kiss, delivered on a crest of emotion.

The only complication was the recipient.

The room detonated. Actually detonated: the front-row artist seats went up first with a wall of hooting, and the fan sections’ screaming came crashing in behind. The director lost his mind entirely — instant close-up — and Baek Si-on’s one-second blank expression was captured live, complete, for all of America.

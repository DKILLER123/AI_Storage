Chapter One Hundred Ninety Five

The Disdained ‘Shape of You’

London · the O2 · BRIT Awards night, then a very late room-service hour Europe’s map, stamped · an award that cannot be won by fan votes · a double champion who thanks his cat, his guitar tech and a fried-chicken shop · a buffet that explains the industrial revolution · a rhythm too sticky to escape, disdained by its own author · two phones, two memos · a trophy photographed as garnish · and the real reason a guitar goes to Milan The O2 arena → the after party’s half-closed hall → a London hotel, past midnight A man who has everything to do with one song’s future meets the man who hasn’t written it yet; England surrenders on its own food twice in one night; and the hardest-to-refuse sentence of the tour’s off-season is four words long.

The BRIT invitation had arrived three weeks ago. Scooter’s framing had been simple: Europe is the one map you haven’t formally set foot on. Billboard and the AMAs are North America; the BRITs are Europe. Go once, stamp the territory. So Baek Si-on came. Milan to London, two hours — less time than he had spent in a certain person’s hotel suite last night.

The BRIT Awards were held at the O2. His seat was third row, house right. Beside him sat Sam Smith, who recognized him instantly and had clearly not forgotten the trending topic Taylor had wrecked that night.

“Last time we met — was that before or after Taylor kissed you?”

“After. You got kissed first,” Baek Si-on said.

“Right, but you’re the one who trended.”

“Because when she kissed you, you didn’t retreat.”

Sam Smith considered this.

“Fair. I did feel quite honored at the time.”

“……”

The BRIT run of show was far tighter than the AMAs’. The British approach to ceremonies carried a built-in “let’s not dawdle” efficiency — nothing like the American format of a three-minute VCR package plus two minutes of orchestrated emotion per award. Then: International Male Solo Artist. When the name Baek Si-on was read, he stood, buttoned his jacket, and walked up.

the BRIT Awards · London · the winner’s thirty seconds, as logged by four British journalists who were already writing tomorrow’s headlines

CATEGORY — International Male Solo Artist

WINNER — Baek Si-on

SPEECH, PART ONE — “Thank you, BRIT. I hear this award can’t be won by fan votes.”

ROOM STATE — one beat of silence, then laughter. Emcees looking down, smiling.

SPEECH, PART TWO — “So tonight, my Korean fans can finally sleep.”

Laughter, larger. Several journalists wrote a single line in unison, figuratively: the man thanked the academy by pensioning off his own fandom.

❝

I hear this award can’t be won by fan votes. So tonight, my Korean fans can finally sleep.

— the O2 stage, to a room that wasn’t ready

Baek Si-on turned to the camera for the last of it.

“Thank you to British listeners for being willing to hear songs from somewhere very far away — and for hearing yourselves inside them.”

“……”

The applause came up. He walked off, and the show moved on. Ed Sheeran took two awards — Best British Male and Album of the Year — and in his speech thanked his cat, his guitar technician, and the fried-chicken shop in East London he frequented. The record label went unthanked. The applause stayed warm anyway. Baek Si-on watched from his seat and thought: the man looks like a busker, and yet stands on that stage with zero sense of displacement.

❧ ❧ ❧

The BRIT after party took a half-enclosed banquet hall next to the O2. Its buffet gave Baek Si-on a functional understanding of why the industrial revolution happened in Britain: a people facing that cuisine had to invent machinery, or lose all confidence in civilization. He took one fry, bit it once, and grew more certain. He put the fry back on the plate — and looked up to find a short redhead carrying a beer toward him.

Ed. Tonight’s double champion, the big winner on home soil, looking exactly like a pub singer three songs into his set, lining up one more drink before clocking out.

“Hey.”

Ed tipped his beer in Baek Si-on’s direction.

“Don’t worry — that thing is not the peak of British food.”

Baek Si-on raised his mineral water in reply.

“Yeah?”

“It’s the average.”

“……”

Excellent. The English had surrendered on their own food, unprompted, for the second time that night.

They found a high table in a corner. Around them, everyone networked — cards, clinks, photos, performed interest in each other’s new albums. Ed clearly did not belong to that mode. He stood in his corner drinking, and there was a cross-cultural resonance between him and the man who had eaten ham alone in a corner at a Venice dinner. Ed raised his glass again.

“Been wanting to meet you.”

“Because of ‘Love Yourself’?”

“Because of my dead draft.”

He laughed at his own phrasing.

“Justin told me he’d given you that song. I thought he was drunk. Then the royalties statement came in, and it turned out he wasn’t drunk — he’d done the single most correct thing of his year.”

He clinked his beer against Baek Si-on’s water.

“Congratulations on singing my dead draft to number one on the Billboard Hot 100.”

Baek Si-on gave him a look.

“And congratulations on selling a dead draft at a champion’s price.”

Ed’s mouth opened and got stuck there. Three full seconds of silence.

“I’m starting to regret it. If I’d known my dead drafts were worth that much, I’d have put a lock on them.”

“There’s still time.”

“A lock?”

“No — mail it to me.”

Ed stared.

“You’re serious?”

“Of course. That’s how human civilization advances.”

“By collecting my dead drafts?”

“By you throwing them out first.”

Ed laughed hard enough to nearly spill his beer. Talking to this man, he had discovered, was a strange experience: you knew perfectly well you were being taken advantage of, but the advantage was taken with such unshakeable self-righteousness that you ended up feeling like the insufficiently generous party.

Ed took a pull of beer.

“Actually — I’ve got this stupid rhythm stuck in my head. Been circling for days. Won’t leave.”

“What?” Baek Si-on asked.

Ed knocked it out on the tabletop, humming a blur of groove over his knuckles.

“Dum, da-da-dum, da-da-dum… something brand new…”

“……”

Baek Si-on’s water glass did not move a millimeter. Inside his skull, however, the blurry groove had already been matched, automatically, against a chorus that would one day play on repeat in malls, bars, gyms, weddings, and taxis across the entire planet.

the projection · one corner table, one hummed rhythm, one complete future

INPUT — a rhythm its own author currently describes as “stupid.”

MATCH — ‘Shape of You’.

PROJECTED DEPLOYMENT — every shopping mall. Every bar. Every gym. Every wedding. Every taxi, worldwide.

CLASSIFICATION — one of Ed Sheeran’s most terrifying cash machines, not yet assembled.

The owner of the future machine currently wants it gone. The buyer at the table is exercising inhuman patience.

Baek Si-on drank his water and asked:

“Why do you think it’s stupid?”

Ed stopped knocking the table and looked up.

“Because it’s too simple. It’s just — sticky. Sticks in your brain like gum on a shoe.”

Baek Si-on very nearly laughed at the description. He held it. Ed wasn’t joking — he genuinely loathed the rhythm that would soon conquer the world. Which was normal. Musicians with taste mostly put themselves through this exact torture: they write things with real craft and want the listener to hear the structure, the intent, the technique. And the public remembers only the most direct, the catchiest, the part their body reacts to. That was roughly the whole story of ‘Li Bai’ too. Ed was the same. He didn’t lack awareness that the rhythm had a hook — he just hadn’t yet convinced himself that a hook was itself a value.

“You think it’s too simple because you’re the one who wrote it. Listeners don’t care whether it took you ten minutes or three months. They only care whether it got into their heads.”

“That sounds brutal.”

Ed laughed a little and drank.

Baek Si-on glanced at the barely touched plate.

“The market has always been brutal. Look at British food — centuries of survival without being good. Proof the world doesn’t run entirely on quality.”

Ed nearly sprayed the beer he’d just sipped. He turned his head and laughed for a good while, and by the time he turned back, most of the conflict on his face had dissolved.

“You comfort a man and invade Britain in the same breath.”

“I’m citing examples.”

“Terrible example.”

“But you’ll remember it.”

Ed pointed at him, opened his mouth to rebut — and found nothing. That was the most annoying part. He looked down and knocked the rhythm again. This time, no frown.

“So your point is, I should finish it?”

“Obviously.”

“Even if it might not be, you know — elevated?”

“‘Elevated’ is the verdict your peers give. ‘Remembered’ is the verdict listeners give.”

Ed was quiet for a few seconds. Party noise pressed in from all directions — someone laughing, glasses clinking, someone else stuffing a scone dry as masonry into their mouth and instantly regretting it. Then, suddenly:

“And if I finish it, and still think it isn’t for me?”

Baek Si-on had been waiting for exactly this sentence.

“Then play it for me.”

Ed looked at him.

“So everything you’ve been saying since we met was building to that one line?”

“No.”

Baek Si-on’s face was perfectly serious.

“Everything before it was also true.”

Ed studied him for two seconds, shook his head, and pulled out his phone. Baek Si-on assumed a demo transfer. Instead, Ed opened his contacts.

“Number.”

Baek Si-on took the phone, typed it in, handed it back. Ed looked at the saved entry — and in the memo field typed two words: Billboard Thief. Baek Si-on glimpsed it.

“Thief?”

“You stole my dead draft and sang it to number one. What else would I call you?”

“Recycling.”

“…Fine. Station Master of Dead Draft Recycling. Priority supply rights are yours from now on.”

Ed stood.

“This was fun. Next time you’re in London, warn me first — I’ll take you to eat one of the few British foods still worth serving.”

“What food?”

“Afternoon tea.”

“Tea?”

Ed shrugged.

“Which is, at its core, stolen from India and the East.”

“So: still colonialism.”

“Right. British cuisine’s core competitive advantage is colonialism.”

He threw off a salute.

“Welcome to Great Britain.”

Then he turned with his empty glass, a fish indifferent to the current, and shouldered his way through a crowd of tailored record executives toward the beer table. Baek Si-on watched him vanish into the crowd, then looked down at his own phone. One new contact: Ed Sheeran. He thought for a moment, and added a memo of his own.

the exchange of memos · two phones, one deal, zero paperwork

ED’S PHONE — new contact: Baek Si-on · memo: Billboard Thief

BAEK SI-ON’S PHONE — new contact: Ed Sheeran · memo: King of the Dead Drafts

CONSIDERATION — priority supply of all future dead drafts, in perpetuity

Executed with a beer and a bottle of water. The lawyers were not consulted and would not have understood anyway.

❧ ❧ ❧

Back at the hotel, Baek Si-on didn’t sleep right away. Not from award-night adrenaline, and not because he needed to sit by a window processing his feelings. He was hungry. The after party had been a social triumph and a culinary catastrophe — the kind of spread that retroactively explains an empire’s entire maritime strategy. A nation facing fries like those was always going to want ships.

Room service arrived in twenty minutes: a spaghetti bolognese, a grilled chicken breast, a basket of bread, a bottle of sparkling water. He sat, took the first bite — tomato acid, rendered fat, hot carbohydrate landing together — and confirmed that human civilization still existed. Only then did he open his phone. Messages had piled up. He worked through them in order — Choi Jin-ri, Son Nam-won, Scooter, Adam — and arrived last at Lee Ji-eun’s thread. He thought for a moment, then photographed the table. In the frame: trophy left, pasta right. The angle made the trophy look like the pasta’s garnish. He sent it.

She inhaled slowly. Put the phone down, drank some water, picked it up again.

KakaoTalk · Baek Si-on · continued

Lee Ji-eun

Why did you have me take it back to Korea?

This time he didn’t reply at once. He set down the fork and drank. Outside was London, past midnight — street lights smeared into rain-damp glass, the view clammy and cold. He looked at the question on the screen. Why have her take it back to Korea? The official reasons were numerous, but they weren’t the whole of it. He typed, head down.

KakaoTalk · Baek Si-on · continued

Baek Si-on

Because you’ll treat it like a guitar.

Milan went quiet again. Lee Ji-eun read the sentence with her finger suspended above the screen. She could understand it. Precisely because she could understand it, it was trouble. After a few seconds she muttered, barely aloud:

“Who told you to be this good with words.”

And then decided the sentence was wrong. Baek Si-on was not good with words. He simply had a gift for producing the hardest-to-refuse answer precisely when a nice one was not allowed.

Because you’ll treat it like a guitar.

— the whole safekeeping arrangement, explained in six words, from a London midnight

Which was far more trouble than being good with words.

Chapter Twenty-Five

How an Underground Musician Says Hello

June 8:00 a.m. Yeonnam-dong, the half-basement A doorbell, and a manager

Eight in the morning.

The doorbell rang twice. Yoon Hye-ja set down her coffee cup and went from the dining table to answer it. Outside stood Baek Eun-ah. Canvas bag slung, hair tied back in a ponytail, a fresh, well-slept flush on her face.

“Auntie, good morning!”

Hye-ja looked at her for two seconds.

“Eun-ah? What are you doing here this early?”

Eun-ah compressed the past few days into a summary in about forty seconds — left SM, paid the penalty, contract dissolved, no longer a trainee, and now her cousin's manager.

She'd left out the World Cup betting part. Some things, if the aunt learned of them, would determine whether her cousin survived the day at all.

Hye-ja took it in with no particular reaction. A nod.

“Alright. Come in. Breakfast's in the pot. I'm off to class.”

She turned back for her bag and keys, changed her shoes, and picked up the parasol hanging by the entrance.

The door closed.

Eun-ah stood in the entrance and caught the smell drifting in from the kitchen.

✿ ✿ ✿

Morning The half-basement Doenjang-jjigae

Si-on was woken by the smell.

Doenjang-jjigae.

More precisely, Hye-ja's version of doenjang-jjigae. Someone else's used tofu and zucchini; hers used potato and minced beef, the broth a proper bean paste. To Si-on's brain that smell held top administrator privileges — the thing an alarm clock couldn't do, it did.

He opened his eyes.

The ceiling.

White.

June light squeezed through the curtain seam, drawing a bright line on the wall. He rolled over, got up, and pushed his room door open. Baek Eun-ah was sitting in the living room.

He looked at her for two seconds. She looked at him for two.

“…Morning, cousin.”

Si-on didn't answer. He turned around, went back in, and emerged ten seconds later carrying the caramel leather bag. He set it down in front of Eun-ah.

“Your welcome-to-the-job present.”

Eun-ah flipped it over, flipped it back, ran her hand over the grain, tugged the strap, opened the magnet clasp to peek inside.

“Handmade?”

“Mm.”

“Where'd you get it?”

“Someone I know made it.”

Eun-ah slung the bag over her shoulder, ran to the mirror in the entrance and turned this way and that, the grin on her face just about spilling over.

“Does it look good?”

She struck a pose at Si-on.

Si-on was already in the bathroom, door half-shut; the sound of running water and a single, indistinct syllable came through.

“Mm.”

Eun-ah spun one more turn in front of the mirror.

“I'll take that as a yes.”

✿ ✿ ✿

June The half-basement Breakfast, and a bag

Ten minutes later, when Si-on came out washed up, Eun-ah had laid out the breakfast. Doenjang-jjigae, a fried egg, a dish of pickled radish, two bowls of rice.

After a couple of bites, Eun-ah couldn't hold it in anymore.

“Cousin, who made this bag?”

“Kim Jae-kyung.”

Eun-ah's chopsticks froze.

“Kim Jae-kyung? Rainbow's Kim Jae-kyung sunbae-nim?”

“You know her?”

“Of course. She posts her crafts on Instagram all the time.”

Si-on chewed the fried egg and nodded.

“What's the plan for today?”

“Meeting someone, two this afternoon, Hapjeong-dong.”

“Who?”

“A music producer. Jung Jae-joon.”

Eun-ah pulled out her phone, opened her memo, and started writing.

“Hapjeong-dong. Two o'clock. Jung Jae-joon.”

She looked up.

“Anything else?”

“One thing. He might be a little… direct. Don't hold it against him.”

Eun-ah's pen stopped. But before she could ask what that meant, Si-on had already pulled out his own phone and was dialling. He'd only got the name and the neighborhood out of Goo Hara the day before; the floor and the room number had come from a follow-up she'd messaged late last night, and he wanted to be sure the man was even expecting him.

It rang three times.

Baek Si-on → Jung Jae-joon · morning · Hapjeong-dong

“Yeah.”

“Baek Si-on. Goo Hara sunbae gave me your number. We're meant to meet at two.”

“Right. Fourth floor, room 401. I don't do punctuality, I do songs. Be on time and don't bring anyone who's going to ask questions.”

“Understood.”

“Mm.”

The line went dead. No goodbye.

Si-on lowered the phone. Eun-ah was watching him with an expression caught halfway between bracing and fascinated.

“Fourth floor, room 401. And he clearly hates people who ask questions.”

“You'll be fine, then.”

✿ ✿ ✿

June Si-on's room The search for a song

After the meal, Eun-ah collected the dishes. Si-on went back to his room and shut the door. He didn't open the curtain, just left that slit. June light cut through, painting a white line on the floor that crept slowly across.

He sat cross-legged on the bed, and began going through the playlist in his head.

The rule was simple. Don't touch anything released in the past three years. Too recent — if the original writer was already working on it, crossing paths would be a case of plagiarism you couldn't explain. Only things from 2017 onwards could even be considered.

But “considered” didn't mean “usable.”

He wasn't in the music business in the other life. What he remembered of a song wasn't a score — it was repetition. Some songs he'd heard hundreds of times, the melody carved into the bone, could hum start to finish with his eyes closed.

Some he'd only caught in short video clips, knew the first two lines of the chorus, everything after a blur. He needed the former.

One by one, eyes shut.

Song scout · memory log · eyes closed

01 — chorus remembered, verse gone. PASS

02 — melody full, lyric zero. KEEP

03 — only the piano intro, rest gone. PASS

04 — Way Back Home. melody 80%+, chorus near-perfect.

The rule: nothing from the last three years; only 2017 onward. And only the songs burned into the bone.

He got to number four.

And stopped.

A melody surfaced out of the memory — clearer than all the others. Not the whole thing, but that hook. That chorus was too catchy to lose. One whole summer in the other life, it seemed to play in every convenience store he walked into, loop over every short-video app he opened, even the fried chicken shop downstairs had it on repeat.

Impossible to forget even if you tried.

Way Back Home.

He hummed a few notes under his breath, eyes closed. He had eighty per cent or more of it. The chorus nearly note-perfect; the verse had two or three spots he'd have to fill by feel, but the whole shape and emotional arc was there.

The lyrics were a different matter. The Korean words were almost entirely gone. Of the English version, a few lines surfaced, but they didn't assemble into a whole verse.

Didn't matter. The melody was the skeleton. Words could be written after, arrangement after. Once the melody locked, the song was alive.

The song that plays in every store · summer, other life

“Way Back Home” — the hook he can't outrun.

[ blank verse ] — the melody is the bone, the words can wait.

He reached for the phone on the nightstand, opened the recorder, and hummed the whole thing into the microphone. He played it back. Two places felt uncertain; he marked them.

Then he set the phone down, lay back, and stared at the ceiling.

Good.

This one.

June 1:20 p.m. Hapjeong-dong The old five-storey building

One twenty in the afternoon.

Si-on came out of the bedroom in a clean white tee. The flower-print shirt from yesterday was already hanging on the balcony, drying.

Eun-ah was on the sofa, the canvas bag swapped for the caramel leather one, slung crosswise, phone held up for a few selfies. When she saw Si-on emerge, she locked the screen in a flash.

“Let's go.”

Si-on grabbed his keys. The two stepped out, went down, and hailed a cab at the kerb.

“Hapjeong-dong, by Hongdae entrance.”

Eun-ah jotted the address in the back seat. Si-on leaned against the window and ran the melody through his head one more time. From Yeonnam-dong to Hapjeong-dong wasn't far — a twelve-minute cab ride.

The cab pulled up in front of an old five-storey mixed-use building. The outer walls painted a grey-green, a strip of bare concrete showing at the base. The ground floor was a tailor shop already closed, rolling shutter pulled down, two lines of graffiti sprayed on it that meant nothing.

Eun-ah tilted her head up at the building.

“…This is it?”

“Fourth floor.”

The two climbed up.

Room 401. Si-on knocked three times. From inside came the sound of a chair rolling, then footsteps. The door opened.

Jung Jae-joon. Late twenties, thin, a pointed chin, hair dyed an only-half-successful flax, a couple centimetres of black at the roots. A band T-shirt, the logo on the collar one Si-on didn't recognise.

He looked at Si-on, then at Eun-ah behind him.

“Who's this?”

“Hello, I'm Baek Si-on. Goo Hara recommended you.”

Si-on put out his right hand.

Jae-joon looked down at the hand.

Didn't take it. Instead he balled his own right hand into a fist and offered it sideways.

“We don't do handshakes in this crowd.”

Si-on's hand hung in the air.

His social database had no entry for this. In the other life he'd acted for over a decade; directors, producers, actors, managers — all of them you either shook hands with or bowed. A fist bump was something he'd only ever seen in American hip-hop documentaries.

He froze for about a second.

Then he quietly balled the open palm into a fist and knocked it against the other man's.

Behind him, Eun-ah pressed her lips into a line, her shoulders trembling faintly.

“Come in.”

Jae-joon stepped aside to let them through.

Chapter Seven

A Midnight Talk in Mapo, and the Unexpected Arrival of Sulli

That night · 9:40 p.m. A studio in Yeonnam-dong, Mapo The corridor

Having pulled off her mask, she bowed and introduced herself, posture flawless.

“Hello. I’m Choi Jin-ri.”

“Hello.”

Si-on gave a small nod.

Choi Jin-ri.

Not Choi Sulli.

He didn’t stare. He stepped aside, putting Baek Jeong-hun in view. The director had recovered enough by now to sit up straight and try to look like a professional.

Baek Eun-ah hurried forward.

“Jin-ri unnie, this is my dad, Director Baek Jeong-hun. The director of that film I told you about.”

Choi Jin-ri bowed again.

“Director, nice to meet you. Please, take care of me.”

Baek Jeong-hun stood and made a gesture of welcome.

“Sit, sit, make yourself at home.”

Baek Eun-ah turned to Si-on and tipped her chin toward the door.

Si-on shrugged and turned to leave. Han-teuk looked from the director turning pages, to the girl bowing, to Si-on already at the door.

He chose to go with Si-on.

Not because he knew Si-on better.

Because what was happening in that room had already exceeded today’s information-processing limit, and staying any longer felt like he might blue-screen on the spot.

✿ ✿ ✿

In the corridor. The motion-sensor light flickered on, then died.

Si-on leaned against the wall and checked his phone. 9:40 p.m.

Baek Eun-ah came out, closing the door softly behind her, then crossed her arms and looked him up and down.

“Cousin.”

“Mm?”

“You’re discharged.”

“Mm.”

“And then?”

“And then what?”

“And then how did you end up like this?”

Eun-ah raised one finger and trailed it down the length of the floral shirt.

“What’s this? A grease stain? Or blood?”

“From the barbecue shop.”

“And the smell on you?”

Si-on grabbed the front of his shirt and gave it a sniff. Frying smoke. The stale cigarette smell of the loan-shark office. Car leather. And the odour of a man who had not showered in a full day.

Four scents, all in one.

“…normal body odour.”

“Normal, nothing.”

Eun-ah wrinkled her nose and stepped back half a pace, but the corner of her mouth was lifting. She was actually glad. Her cousin was out of the army, alive and kicking, even if he looked like he’d been hauled out of a drain. The old Baek Si-on had been like a squeezed-out rag. Now there was a light in his eyes — even if that light looked a little crooked.

Han-teuk leaned against the wall on the other side of the corridor, quietly listening, his brain still trying to digest the day. Debt collector. No, temporary worker. No, actor. No, the director’s nephew.

He gave up.

Baek Eun-ah talked a bit more, then lowered her voice.

“Cousin, what were you saying to my dad in there? When I came in he had the expression of a man who’d seen a ghost.”

“Talking about the script.”

“You? About the script?”

Eun-ah’s tone was unmistakably sceptical.

“I’m the lead.”

“…Does Aunt know?”

“She knows.”

“What did she say?”

“She said she understood.”

Baek Eun-ah was quiet for two seconds, then nodded. In her family, an aunt saying “I understand” basically meant I don’t object, but I reserve the right to worry; do as you see fit, but don’t come crying to me if it goes wrong — although if it really does go wrong, I’ll still be there.

The whole family was like that. Never said it out loud, but the legs were always more honest than the mouth.

The corridor fell quiet for a moment. Si-on glanced at the closed door.

“What’s the story with her?”

Baek Eun-ah’s expression closed off a little.

“Jin-ri unnie…”

She chose her words carefully.

“Actually, the role — my dad originally offered it to Kim Sae-ron.”

Si-on’s eyebrow rose. Kim Sae-ron. Child-actor turned — one of the strongest actresses of her generation, and her look suited a girl ground down by life. A genuinely good choice.

“And then?”

“Fell through.”

Eun-ah gave a small shrug.

“I don’t know the details. Something about her mother not being keen. Said an indie film had too little exposure, wanted her on something with a better script. Some people say her boyfriend’s side had a hand in it… anyway, it just didn’t work out.”

Si-on didn’t press. That kind of thing was far too common in the industry.

Director’s Note

An indie first feature is a hard sell. The talent agent reads the script, looks at the budget, and quietly pushes the client toward the prime-time drama with the guaranteed slot and the name producer.

An actor’s career is a marathon, not a sprint.

But a film that lasts longer than a season is a ticket to the next decade.

“After the Sae-ron deal fell through, my dad was anxious for a while…”

Eun-ah leaned back against the wall, hands in her pockets.

“He said he’d go back to Chung-Ang University and hold an open audition, pick an unknown from the acting students. I heard that and thought — that’s like fishing for a needle in the sea.”

She paused.

“Then I thought of Jin-ri unnie.”

Si-on didn’t respond, waiting for her to go on. Eun-ah’s voice dropped a little further. The corridor light died again, and the two of them stood in the dim, with only a thread of light leaking out under the door.

“Cousin, do you know what’s been happening to unnie lately?”

“A rough idea.”

Si-on was telling the truth. He knew far more than Eun-ah imagined. In his previous life’s memory, at around this stage, Choi Sulli had been introduced to a man named Choi Ja, and the relationship became the fuse for a storm of public opinion. But in this life, so far, it hadn’t happened.

He wasn’t sure what had changed it. Some tiny variable. Or just that the time hadn’t come yet.

Whatever the reason, her position hadn’t improved by much.

“A while back, unnie had stomach pains — just normal ones. She went to the ER, had tests, nothing serious.”

Eun-ah paused.

“But somehow, the next day, the whole internet knew. And it said she went to the hospital because…”

Fan-cafe thread · surfaced overnight Report: idol admitted to hospital — rumours spiral

“She went to the hospital because … because …” the post left the sentence unfinished, and the replies finished it for it.

Everyone was so sure of the worst that no one asked the person actually in the waiting room.

She didn’t finish, as if letting the words out of her mouth would dirty it.

“Anyway, really awful things.”

“Unnie’s been off this whole time. Not going to the practice room, not going back to the dorm. She rented a place by herself and won’t answer anyone’s calls.”

A little nasal quality crept into Eun-ah’s voice, but she held it together.

“The company… didn’t do much. Or rather, the way they handled it was wrong. Just — watch your image, don’t cause trouble for the group.”

Si-on listened, then asked.

“Does she want to leave?”

“Not leave.”

Eun-ah thought for a moment, hunting for the accurate word.

“It’s that she can’t stay anymore.”

The corridor was quiet a few more seconds. The motion sensor, sensing her shift her weight, flicked the light back on. The pale light fell across both faces, and Eun-ah’s eyes were a little red.

“I’ve always been close to unnie. Back when she was still at the company, she was so good to us younger trainees. Other seniors were busy with dance practice and fighting for the center; she’d bring us snacks in the breaks and teach us how to survive the month-end evaluations.”

She sniffed.

“So when I heard my dad was looking for an actress, she was the first one I thought of. She’s got the acting foundation, so the performing is no worry. And…”

Eun-ah glanced at Si-on.

“And she needs somewhere to be. Somewhere that isn’t SM, somewhere nobody knows her. My dad’s set, that’s just the place.”

“Alright, I get it.”

He patted Eun-ah’s shoulder.

“Go in and stay with her. Han-teuk and I are getting something to eat; we’ll talk later.”

Eun-ah nodded and pushed the door open. Han-teuk asked, quietly:

“Mr. Baek—was that…”

“Mm.”

“Really Choi Sul—”

“Don’t call her that.”

Si-on turned toward the stairwell.

“She said her name is Choi Jin-ri.”

Han-teuk swallowed the rest of the sentence. The two of them went down the stairs and pushed open the building door. The night wind rushed in, carrying the last cold at the tail of March.

Si-on stood at the curb, looked up at the lit window on the second floor, and watched it for two seconds. Then he put his hands in his pockets and walked toward the mouth of the street.

“Come on, barbecue.”

“Huh? You’re really paying?”

“I said I would.”

Han-teuk followed, then hesitated.

“Um… can I order beef?”

“Order whatever.”

“Really?”

“Ask again and it becomes kimchi and white rice.”

Han-teuk shut up immediately and quickened his steps to keep pace. The two shadows stretched long under the streetlights, one behind the other, and vanished into the alleys of Yeonnam-dong.

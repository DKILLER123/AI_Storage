IU, Full Marks on the Fan Service

December 18 · the last two hours, and the drive home from them Guitar and confession songs · one bow that empties a stadium · a van, a phone call, a fortune Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium · the encore stretch → backstage → the Red Velvet van A special guest steals a stage with bunny ears and a borrowed guitar, a headliner sings the last song like a letter’s postscript, sixty thousand light sticks refuse to snow out — and in one van, a rookie’s whole price changes.

After BTS left the stage, the concert’s progress bar crossed the two-thirds mark.

The stage lights died for a few seconds.

When they came back up, Baek Si-on was standing at a standing microphone with a guitar strap across his shoulder.

The first pluck of strings. His voice came mixed into the chord, low, like a man in a midnight room writing a letter to someone very far away.

Sixty thousand light sticks lowered slowly. The light pulled down and in, until all that was left was one pool of warm yellow at the center of everything.

‘Way Back Home’ ended, and Baek Si-on rolled without a seam into ‘Love Yourself’.

‘Love Yourself’ · the chorus the room was waiting for

“Cause if you liked the way you looked that much”

“Oh baby, you should go and love yourself”

The screaming had already started during the intro.

His voice moved right up against the microphone with that faint, gentle coldness of his. Parts of the stadium sang along. Parts just listened.

‘Love Yourself’ isn’t a complicated melody — by the chorus, most of the bowl could keep up.

Two verses in, the screaming spiked for no operational reason at all.

Baek Si-on had done nothing. He was standing there, playing guitar.

But on the big screen, Lee Ji-eun had appeared behind him without a sound.

Pale stage dress, hair tucked behind her ears, expression solemn as a state ceremony — walking up behind him and raising both hands to frame his head with bunny ears.

The director — of course — surrendered instantly. Two-shot, live.

Baek Si-on in front, singing. Lee Ji-eun behind, bunny-ears at full extension. Neither looked at the camera. One sincerely singing. One sincerely earring.

The total non-cooperation of the bit made it worse. The room came apart.

In the seats, Choi Jin-ri watched Lee Ji-eun’s bunny ears bloom four meters tall and felt something go mildly sideways in her chest.

Her tolerance for stage business, to be fair, had been professionally hardened over the past year. As long as the two of them didn’t kiss right there on the stage, she could hypnotize herself through most flavors of ambiguous.

It’s a concert, she told herself. It’s atmosphere. It’s fan service.

She stacked three mental platforms under herself, one after another.

While laying the fourth, the sourness landed anyway.

How is Lee Ji-eun this good at it?

Bunny ears are not a skill! Bunny ears are something I could do!

…

‘Love Yourself’ ran out. Lee Ji-eun took her place obediently at Baek Si-on’s side, both hands on her mic, waiting to be introduced.

Baek Si-on had just raised the microphone when somebody on the floor started it:

“Kiss! Kiss!”

“Kiss! Kiss!”

A small zone first. Then the whole floor. Then the second tier.

Choi Jin-ri: “……”

Wonderful. Her psychological durability was being stress-tested live, on national broadcast, at regular intervals, by sixty thousand training instructors.

The two on stage knew exactly what the room was playing at: the running gag from the MV — the fake-out everybody got tricked by. An actual kiss would detonate precisely the section that was loudest about demanding it.

So the two of them achieved, in that moment, the perfect unison of a longtime deaf duo. Nothing heard. Nothing acknowledged.

“Thank you to IU for coming out tonight as this show’s special guest.”

Lee Ji-eun raised her microphone and picked it up cleanly:

“And it’s an honor to be here as your guest. Hello everyone, I’m IU.”

Applause rolled again.

Baek Si-on raised the mic once more:

“As most of you know — the lyrics of ‘Way Back Home’ were written by IU.”

Someone in the floor:

“We know!”

“So next — a different version of ‘Way Back Home’.”

Baek Si-on unslung the guitar and handed it over. Lee Ji-eun took it into her arms, head down, tuning pegs ticking softly under her fingers. Baek Si-on turned for backstage — and at the lip of the lift platform, stopped, and looked back once.

She was test-strumming.

The lights came down. Just her now, one figure at the center of everything.

She didn’t use the band arrangement. She played a small intro of her own, just strings and room tone.

“……”

Her version was good.

Good enough that people who had only been waiting out the costume change found their light sticks drifting lower, hand by hand, without deciding anything.

‘Way Back Home’ had been written to her own language habits in the first place — the words fit her mouth because her mouth was the mold. Sung by Baek Si-on, it sounded like a letter written to someone far away. Sung by her, it sounded like the reply.

The room arrived at a consensus: tonight had already been worth it.

Free concert. Baek Si-on live. And now IU singing a special version.

This had passed the border of “perks.” This was the year-end settlement, when you open your fan account and find an unexplained deposit the size of a house down payment.

the pale dress, and the guitar that rewrote the room December 18 · solo slot · the reply, not the letter

The dress A pale cream stage dress, knee-length, soft-skirted, moving a half-second behind her like it was listening too. Sleeves light at the elbow. The color of warm milk at the bottom of a late night — deliberately un-decorated, because the costume plan for this slot was: nobody looks at the dress, everybody looks at the hands.
The instrument One acoustic guitar, borrowed from the man who wrote the album version and handed over at the lip of the lift. Strap across the shoulder, hair swept back and tucked behind her ears — nothing allowed to fall between the room and the face doing the apologizing-for-him. A costume change for Baek Si-on became, on this stage, her entire set design.

Written to her language. Sung as his letter. Returned as hers.

The spotlight did the arithmetic: one girl, one guitar, sixty thousand lowered light sticks — the quietest equation a stadium can run.

‘Way Back Home’ ended, and Lee Ji-eun didn’t leave.

She lifted her head from the guitar and looked out at the bowl.

“Well — I’m already standing here. One more.”

The stadium approved enthusiastically.

The intro of ‘Friday’ rolled in.

Different weather entirely. If ‘Way Back Home’ is a cup of warm milk at midnight, ‘Friday’ is coffee at three in the afternoon, when the sun cuts across a windowsill at the exact angle that makes you want to stand up and leave wherever you are.

Not healing music.

Standing-up-and-going music.

…

By the time ‘Friday’ ended, Baek Si-on was back in his last outfit of the night.

Black suit jacket, plain white shirt underneath. No tie, no hardware, nothing extra. It didn’t matter — the sound the room made when he walked out lifted a full register on its own.

Lee Ji-eun glanced over at him.

Baek Si-on reached her side:

“This next song goes out to every single person here.”

Lee Ji-eun picked it up instantly:

“And to everyone who is absolutely not single, but is currently pretending to be.”

The bowl folded over laughing.

The intro of ‘Besides Spring, Love, and Cherry Blossoms’ started up.

Front row look at the calendar: this is a single-people’s song. In April, with the cherry trees doing their annual business, it is already cruel. Sung in December in Jamsil, it approaches being chased down an alley. The two of them sang it lightly anyway — his voice leaning along the underside of her melody, the two lines braiding in the air like currents of one river meeting on a spring flood.

By the chorus the singalong had caught up. Couples laughed. Single fans swore. Someone hoisted a light stick and hollered:

“I don’t need love, I need Baek Si-on!”

Someone next to them, instantly:

“IU would also be acceptable!”

Everything dissolved from there.

Baek Si-on heard it, looked at the stands with a perfectly straight face, and muttered, just audible:

“Quite the shopping list.”

Lee Ji-eun nearly lost a beat laughing.

One song later, she bowed to the bowl.

“Thank you, everyone.”

Baek Si-on turned to her.

“Thank you, IU.”

She waggled her microphone at him and said, quietly, for the two of them:

“Save me a seat at the thank-you dinner.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Mm.”

And that was how IU left the stage.

the last outfit · black suit, white shirt, no argument December 18 · final slot · the closer

The suit Black jacket, black trousers, white shirt worn open at the collar — the evening’s least decorated garment and its most efficient. No tie to apologize for, no sparkle to manage. After a night of gold wire and lighting rigs, the plainness is the point: the last outfit of the year belongs to the songs, not the tailoring.
The timing Worn for the single-people’s duet, for the last dedication, and for one deep bow in front of sixty thousand people. It will finish the night smelling of stage haze and December — and it is, the wardrobe department confirms, the version of him the front row will describe to their group chats in present tense for the next week.

No decoration. The room lifted anyway.

One warm light, one plain suit, sixty thousand phones — the math of the final slot never changes.

When the applause had settled a degree, Baek Si-on spoke:

“The next song — this one is for everyone here tonight.”

He said it simply.

No title. No explanation for the choice.

The saxophone intro came up — and Choi Jin-ri’s heart skipped one full beat.

“Can’t Take My Eyes Off You.”

Venice. The festival’s closing dinner — Baek Si-on had sung this to the whole room, and the whole room had understood that “the whole room” was a fig leaf.

Nobody had ever established who the “you” was that night. Everyone had their own guess, and the guesses had their own group chats.

Now the same melody was standing over Jamsil.

And on the same phrase, he lifted a hand toward the audience — the same gesture, looser this time, wider, a sweep across the whole fan section, a thing that could mean nobody in particular.

Choi Jin-ri watched him on the big screen and knew, with total irrational certainty, that the finger was pointing at her.

No evidence supported this.

She believed it anyway.

She lowered her head, tucked half her face into the collar of Baek Si-on’s down coat — and smiled into the fabric where nobody could bill her for it.

Spineless. She knew.

She was keeping it.

The song didn’t get a mass singalong. Most of the room just listened — listened to Baek Si-on hand the year’s last warmth out over sixty thousand heads, saxophone and drums and low bass braided into the sound of that many people breathing carefully. When the last note landed, the stadium held its silence for exactly one second.

Then the applause and screaming came down like weather.

…

The clock had passed nine.

Baek Si-on stood at the center of it all, microphone up, and looked out at the bowl.

“Tonight’s concert — were you satisfied?”

The answers came up from everywhere at once. Some shouted yes. Some chanted encore. Some had already moved on to demanding an annual event, in perpetuity, starting next year.

He listened for a while, then lifted a hand for quiet.

“This is where tonight ends.”

A groan went through the whole building, genuine and synchronous.

He kept going:

“These past two-plus hours were the happiest I’ve had all year.”

“I hope tonight leaves you with one good evening to keep.”

The year, for Baek Si-on, had been overfull. Films. Awards. Billboard. Stages abroad. Ceremonies at home. He had, more or less, been running forward since January.

Tonight was the first time he stood on a stage that was his — that he had called sixty thousand people to — and said the actual word out loud. Thank you.

“Last few things.”

“For everyone who traveled in from outside — free shuttle buses are staged in the parking area outside the stadium, and they’ll run by district.”

“The cushion under you can go home with you. Consider it a souvenir.”

“The light stick goes home with you too.”

“When you exit, follow the staff and volunteer guidance. Don’t push. Don’t run.”

“Get home safe.”

Then he took one step back.

And bowed, deep, to the bowl.

The big screen put the bow on every wall at once — Baek Si-on folded at the waist, four meters tall.

Applause, screaming, and the sound of a meaningful percentage of sixty thousand people outright crying all ran together.

The light sticks stayed on.

All sixty thousand of them. Refusing, together, to snow out.

No need.

Choi Jin-ri touched the tip of her own nose.

Somehow she had the strong feeling that this “Mm” carried exactly the same destructive yield as Baek Si-on’s “Mm.”

The family, it turned out, shipped a common arsenal.

The three of them carried on like this for a statistically significant amount of time, at a volume that threatened the van’s roof lining.

You couldn’t blame them. For a group barely out of debut, the leader being seen by the public meant the group had a doorway to be seen by the public. As for whether this arrangement might curdle into “Bae Suzy and her backup dancers” or “HyunA and her backing track” — they weren’t terrified of that scenario tonight.

First: Shabby Hat did not permit that kind of unhealthy group structure to survive. Company policy, historically enforced.

Second: yes, Bae Joo-hyun’s face could hold a big screen silent for three seconds, but she carried visible, documented shortcomings of her own. A group is built to work exactly like this — covering for each other, holding each other up.

Bae Joo-hyun listened to the three of them carry on, and the corners of her mouth climbed degree by degree.

And then an old, ridiculous thought surfaced — one of her own, from way back.

Stand near Baek Si-on-sunbaenim, and your luck improves.

When the idea had first wandered through her head, she’d dismissed it as superstition. It had since compiled a winning record.

Tonight: she stood on his concert stage. The big screen gave her face to sixty thousand people. And now the producer of a KBS music show was calling the van.

Had Baek Si-on-sunbaenim’s luck finally, officially, landed on her?

Bae Joo-hyun looked down at her own hands for a few seconds. Then she asked:

“Oppa — so what do I do?”

The manager watched the road.

“Don’t answer yet.”

“Wait for tomorrow’s concert coverage and clips to cook. If other stations call too, we’ll see.”

Bae Joo-hyun nodded. She understood.

The term is: let the bids come in.

It sounds like a cabbage stall at the market, but the industry runs more literal than the market most days. At least cabbage has a weight you can check. An entertainer’s price is whatever today’s temperature says it is.

The manager added:

“And at the thank-you dinner tonight — don’t forget to raise a toast to Baek Si-on-sunbaenim.”

Bae Joo-hyun looked up.

“Okay.”

“Can you handle your drinks?”

Bae Joo-hyun nodded.

“I can.”

Her actual drinking experience remained, at this date, theoretical.

But this was not the sentence in which one says so.

The manager nodded.

“Good.”

The van ran on toward the dinner. Outside the windows, Seoul’s nightlights ticked backward one by one.

Bae Joo-hyun leaned into the seat and let one long breath out.

She thought: after tonight, some things might genuinely be different now.

Chapter Seventy-Six

The Stomach That Gurgled in Venice

September 2 Afternoon · red carpet Venice Lido · Palazzo del Cinema The opening ceremony, and a stomach that would not cooperate

The opening ceremony went exactly as Lee Seung-cheol said it would.

The red carpet was walked.

The flash from several hundred cameras burned Baek Si-on's retina for a good fifteen minutes. By the end he could no longer tell which side was a lens and which was a light.

Choi Jin-ri walked half a pace ahead of him, stopping every few metres to turn with the photographers' direction and change angles.

Baek Si-on stopped when she stopped. Turned when she turned.

The red carpet ended, and they entered the main hall of the film palace.

The opening ceremony.

The festival president's address. The jury president's address. Then the opening film.

This year's opening film was Alejandro González Iñárritu's Birdman.

The Opening Film · the one he had to watch on an empty stomach

An actor who used to play a superhero, alone backstage at a Broadway theatre, facing a mirror.

The whole hall was so quiet you could hear the film spool turning.

And the quietest single moment in the theatre was the one Baek Si-on's stomach chose to make itself known.

Baek Si-on sat in the main-competition section, seventh row, aisle seat.

Choi Jin-ri sat to his right. Baek Jeong-hun sat to his left.

The lights went out.

The screen lit up.

Baek Si-on stared at the screen, forcing his attention onto the story about the washed-up superhero actor.

His stomach refused to cooperate.

From the moment he'd woken up that morning, the only calories he'd taken in were two black coffees.

You couldn't eat before the red carpet.

Eat and your face swells, your suit goes tight, and the photos get picked apart under a magnifying glass by the global fashion press.

He hadn't even had more than two small sips of water.

In the past three hours his stomach had sent out at least four clear protest signals.

Each one louder than the last.

The first three were still restrained, like distant thunder.

The fourth was not so polite.

It came somewhere around the forty-minute mark of Birdman.

On screen, Michael Keaton stood backstage at a Broadway theatre, alone, facing a mirror. The whole auditorium was so quiet you could hear the film spool turning.

Baek Si-on's stomach chose the single quietest moment in the entire theatre to let out a long, low-frequency, cello-open-E-string gurgle.

It lasted about two seconds.

In the front row, an old French man with grey hair slowly turned his head and, peering over the seat back, gave Baek Si-on one long look.

Baek Si-on stared at the screen, expressionless.

The expression management was so good that it looked as though the noise were a piece of ambient sound design Iñárritu had deliberately composed.

The old Frenchman turned back around.

Beside him, Choi Jin-ri dropped her head and covered her mouth with the back of her hand, shoulders shaking twice.

Nobody said anything.

On screen Michael Keaton went on talking to the mirror.

Baek Si-on's stomach went quiet.

Temporarily.

September 2 9 pm Excelsior Hotel · private beach The beach banquet, and a man who chose the corner table

Nine at night.

The private beach at the Excelsior Hotel.

The beach banquet.

Directors, producers, distributors and critics from every country, in evening dress, holding champagne flutes, standing in twos and threes among torches and low tables, talking.

At least seven or eight languages — Italian, French, English, Spanish — mixed in with the sound of waves lapping the shore, making up an elegant, restrained hubbub.

Baek Jeong-hun was pulled away by Lee Seung-cheol the moment he came in.

Choi Jin-ri was taken off by the SM manager too.

Baek Eun-ah and Park Ji-hun were not there.

Invitations to the beach banquet went only to the producing team of the selected films and the festival's official guests.

Managers and stylists were not on the list.

The two of them were probably in the hotel room eating room service.

Baek Si-on looked around the beach.

To the left, the socialising area.

To the right, the buffet table.

He turned right.

And he walked fast.

Fast enough that it looked like he was afraid the buffet would pack up and leave before he got there.

The Italian buffet was several orders of magnitude larger than any industry banquet in Korea.

Appetisers, mains, seafood, cheese, desserts — laid out in five separate stations.

Excelsior beach buffet · the banquet that could not be eaten politely

Five stations · two plates · no cutlery

Parma ham — sliced thin as a cicada's wing

Buffalo mozzarella — thick-cut, with San Daniele tomato and basil

Smoked salmon — lemon-marinated octopus salad

Roast lamb chop — crisp crust, pink within

Sea bass — in cream sauce, a large piece

Pappardelle — with truffle sauce, a full scoop

The first plate was full. The second was too.

No knife, no fork — he had forgotten them, thirty metres back.

Baek Si-on picked up a white porcelain plate.

Parma ham sliced thin as a cicada's wing.

He filled half the plate with the tongs.

Next to it, fresh buffalo mozzarella, cut thick, with San Daniele tomatoes and basil leaves.

Four pieces.

Further on, Italian smoked salmon, lemon-marinated octopus salad, grilled vegetable platter.

One serving of each.

The first plate was full.

He set it down on the edge of the buffet table.

Picked up a second plate.

The mains station.

Roast lamb chops, crisp on the outside, pink within.

Three of them.

Sea bass in a cream sauce, a large piece.

Pappardelle with truffle sauce, a full scoop with the serving spoon.

The second plate was full too.

Baek Si-on carried one plate in each hand and swept his gaze across the beach.

All the low tables in the socialising area were full.

He didn't go over there.

Instead he went to the edge of the beach, to the corner the torchlight could barely reach.

There was a single lonely low table there.

No glasses on it. No name cards. No flowers.

Probably a spare that had been set out extra, or one that had simply been forgotten.

Baek Si-on carried the two plates over.

Set them down on the low table.

Dragged over a beach chair and sat.

The night of the Adriatic spread out in front of him — the ink-blue sea reflecting moonlight and the lights of some distant island.

The waves lapped the sand at a leisurely, unhurried rhythm. One. Two. Three.

Behind him, the banquet clinked glasses and talked on in dozens of languages, with the occasional burst of laughter.

Baek Si-on looked down at the two plates.

Then at the table surface.

No cutlery.

He'd forgotten to get some.

Baek Si-on looked back over his shoulder at the buffet table.

About thirty metres. A minute round trip.

He looked down again at the Parma ham glistening with its sheen of fat in the torchlight.

He made a decision.

Hands.

A man who has been hungry for sixteen hours does not need table manners.

This turned out to be true.

Parma ham eaten with the fingers tastes at least three times better than it does cut with a knife and fork.

That was the first lesson Baek Si-on learned in Venice.

The roast lamb chop went the same way.

Held by the bone, gnawed, teeth tearing through the crisp crust while pink juices ran down between his fingers.

He ate intently.

So intently that he never noticed the footsteps.

Footsteps on sand are light to begin with, and his visitor was wearing flat sandals.

By the time Baek Si-on registered it, a woman was already seated in the beach chair across from him.

Around twenty-seven, twenty-eight.

Blue eyes.

A black spaghetti-strap evening gown.

Legs crossed in the beach chair, a champagne flute in hand she had barely touched, looking at the ruins of his two plates, more than half devoured.

"Two or three hundred people on this whole beach."

Her English carried an American East Coast accent.

"Everyone's socialising, handing out business cards, laughing at things they don't actually mean."

She gestured with the champagne flute toward the bright banquet area behind her.

"Only you."

Her gaze came back to him.

"Carrying two plates of food, walking to the farthest corner, with your back to everyone, eating facing the sea."

"You're either the person on this whole beach who cares least about these people — or the hungriest."

❝

You're either the person who cares least — or the hungriest.

— Alexandra Daddario, to a man eating alone in the dark

Baek Si-on looked at her.

Thought for about half a second.

"Both."

The woman laughed out loud.

Not a hand-over-the-mouth lady's laugh, but the kind where she leaned back in her chair and her shoulders shook twice.

"I like honest people."

She sat up straight and held out her right hand.

"Daddario. Alexandra Daddario."

Alexandra Daddario

American actress · main competition, Venice

Real-name counterpart · Alexandra Daddario

The woman who sits down across from the man eating alone in the dark and tells him he is either the least interested or the hungriest person on the beach. Direct in a way Seoul's politeness never is — and the first on that beach not to come away with a phone number.

Baek Si-on looked at the clean hand she'd extended.

Then at his own hand, the one that glistened in the dim light.

Thought about it.

Picked a slice of Parma ham off the plate and held it out to her.

Daddario's blue eyes blinked twice.

She probably hadn't expected someone to hand her a slice of ham at the self-introduction stage.

"…"

She took it.

Chewed a couple of times.

"Mm. Not bad."

Daddario nodded, wiped the oil from her fingers on the black fabric at the side of her gown, and looked at Baek Si-on again.

"What's your name?"

"Baek Si-on."

"Where are you from?"

"Korea."

"An actor?"

"Also a singer, depending on the day."

"Which film?"

"The Green Fly."

"Oh."

Her eyebrow lifted slightly:

"Main competition. That's a strong film."

Baek Si-on didn't play modest.

And didn't exchange pleasantries.

He just tore off another slice of ham, put it in his mouth, then pushed the plate toward Daddario.

"Eat. It's better than networking."

Daddario's eyes glittered in the torchlight.

"You know, Baek."

She used his surname, the vowel flattened by her American accent — "Baek" came out as a short, clipped "Back."

"You're the first man on this beach who hasn't tried to get my phone number."

"Because my hands are covered in oil."

Daddario laughed again.

This time she said nothing, just raised the champagne flute from the low table in Baek Si-on's direction.

"Cheers, Mr. Baek."

Baek Si-on raised the lamb bone he'd half gnawed.

"Cheers."

Choi Jin-ri had exactly one assignment that night.

Smile.

Smile at the Italian producer, smile at the English critic, smile at the distributor from some country explaining in French and hand gestures that "your film sounds wonderful, I'm very much looking forward to it."

She didn't understand a single word.

The translator stood beside her, compressing whatever the person had said into a single line of Korean every thirty seconds and feeding it into her ear. She caught it, smiled, nodded, said a "thank you" or "please look after me," and the translator threw it back the way it had come.

Round and round.

Like a vending machine set to a program. Insert coin, dispense, smile.

After all those years as an idol at SM, Choi Jin-ri had practised this smile hundreds of times.

But tonight's smile was especially tiring.

Maybe the jet lag. Maybe the humidity. Maybe simply that her shoes had been uncomfortable for too long.

She held her champagne flute and, in the gap while the translator chatted with a Frenchman, let her eyes drift toward the beach.

She saw Baek Si-on.

He was striding from the buffet toward the edge of the beach with two plates piled as high as small mountains.

Two plates.

Walking fast.

Fast enough that it looked like he was afraid someone would take them from him.

She lowered her head and covered her mouth with the champagne flute.

Hasn't eaten all day, has he?

Choi Jin-ri knew his routine. On the day he performed on KBS Music Bank, Baek Eun-ah had complained about him on KakaoTalk. "My cousin hasn't even dared drink water since morning, in case his face swells. In eighteen years of life this is the first time I've seen a man starve himself for the camera like this."

Today was the red carpet.

Camera specifications several orders of magnitude higher than a music-show stage.

So in all likelihood he'd been even harder on himself than that day.

Which was why, now that the event was over and he could finally eat, he'd carried two plates of food to the farthest corner, turned his back on the whole world, and faced the sea to scarf it down.

So Baek Si-on.

Choi Jin-ri pulled her gaze back, put the smile back on, and listened to the translator relay her thoughts on "the presentation of post-colonial narrative in East Asian cinema."

She nodded.

"Mm."

"Indeed."

"You make an excellent point."

Fully automatic replies.

About ten minutes passed.

Choi Jin-ri was pulled into a round with a Japanese distributor; his English came with a heavy Kansai accent, the translator struggled, and the three of them stood torturing one another for nearly ten minutes.

Finally over.

In the gap between the crowds, Choi Jin-ri glanced once more toward that corner of the beach.

Baek Si-on was still there.

But he wasn't alone.

A woman sat in the beach chair opposite him.

Too far to make out her face.

But the figure was clear enough.

The kind of oppressive presence that "god feeds with a spoon."

Choi Jin-ri's fingers tightened a little around the flute.

She looked away.

Just basic networking.

Beach banquet, filmmakers from every country meeting each other, a chat or two was perfectly normal.

Choi Jin-ri returned her gaze to the Italian producer's assistant who was talking, kept nodding, kept smiling.

But the corner of her eye stayed on that direction.

It wouldn't come back.

Another five or six minutes.

When Choi Jin-ri looked over a third time.

She watched Baek Si-on pick something up off a plate and hold it out.

The woman on the other side took it.

Put it in her mouth.

Baek Si-on pushed the plate toward her again.

Choi Jin-ri's smile froze on her face.

She knew what food meant to Baek Si-on.

It was equivalent to oxygen.

Not even his own cousin, the one he'd grown up with, had ever been granted food-sharing rights.

And yet, now.

He'd pushed his plate toward a stranger.

✿ ✿ ✿

The SM manager stood two steps behind her, head down, texting.

He looked up, and on habit gave his artist a once-over.

Choi Jin-ri's profile was beautiful in the warm torchlight, gown and jewellery flawless.

But the smile was wrong.

The difference between a real smile and a fake one isn't in the mouth, it's in the muscle group around the eyes. Whether the orbicularis oculi contracted, whether the crow's-feet creased — those details were readable from three metres away.

The manager followed the line of Choi Jin-ri's gaze to see what she was looking at.

Edge of the beach. The corner the torches couldn't reach.

A grey suit.

Across from him, a woman in a black slip dress.

A low table between them with plates on it.

The manager withdrew his gaze.

Understood.

"Sorry — Mr. Lee Seung-cheol over at Finecut said there are a couple of premiere-related details he needs to confirm with her…"

The excuse was seamless.

Choi Jin-ri looked at him.

Knew he was lying.

But also knew he was helping her.

The person she'd been talking to gave a slight bow.

"My apologies, I'll excuse myself for a moment."

"…"

The manager led her to a relatively quiet corner at the edge of the tent.

"How about we head back to the hotel and rest?"

Choi Jin-ri shook her head.

Her eyes did not turn.

Still fixed on that direction.

The manager followed her gaze and looked again.

Baek Si-on was leaning back in his beach chair, apparently holding something and gnawing on it, while the woman across from him raised her champagne flute, at ease as if she were in her own living room.

He did a little arithmetic in his head.

Actually, the best way to repay Baek Si-on right now would be to take Choi Jin-ri straight back to the hotel.

Leave that corner a complete, unbothered space.

Man and woman. Beach. Moonlight. Champagne.

The classic configuration.

He should do this favour.

After all, Baek Si-on had upgraded him to first class and booked him a luxury suite at the hotel. Every convenience he'd enjoyed on the way from Incheon to Venice that exceeded the company standard was Baek Si-on's money.

But then again.

What was the precondition for him getting to enjoy any of it?

That he was Choi Jin-ri's person.

His value came not from himself, but from Choi Jin-ri.

And Choi Jin-ri's mood ranked higher in Baek Si-on's priority list than the gratitude of an SM manager would ever be worth.

The arithmetic came out.

The manager turned on the spot.

The night of the Adriatic spread out before him, pitch-dark, broken only by the lights of a distant island and the moonlight drawing threads of tarnished silver across the water.

He clasped both hands behind his back and earnestly began studying the frequency of the waves hitting the sand.

One.

Two.

Three.

A cycle roughly every seven seconds.

Mm.

Very regular.

Worth studying.

Soon the sound of high heels on the wooden boardwalk came from behind him.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

The sound drifted toward the edge of the beach and faded.

The pace wasn't fast at all.

But it was clear that not one of those steps carried a moment of hesitation.

Chapter Eighty-One

The Speech Wasn’t Finished Before the Headlines Were

September 6 evening Venice · Sala Grande, the podium A cup, a thousand faces, and a speech that refused to stay polite

Baek Si-on walked to the podium.

He set the Volpi Cup on the table. Adjusted the microphone down a fraction, until it sat at the height of his mouth and not the height of a taller man’s chest.

A thousand-odd people below the stage were looking at him.

He looked at the thousand-odd people below the stage.

Sala Grande · Volpi Cup acceptance

Spotlights. Translators live in English and Italian. Tim Roth three metres off, stage-right.

“Thank you. This is the highest honour I have received as an actor. I will treasure it for the rest of my life.”

Standard opening · polite applause, two beats

The translators moved with him, English and Italian in lockstep.

The hall offered the two polite claps that every winner is owed, and then stopped, waiting for the rest.

Baek Si-on went on.

“The other men nominated in the main competition all gave extraordinary performances. There were scenes I watched in the screening hall knowing I could not have done them…”

As he said it, he felt the muscles in his mouth go stiff.

Not nerves.

Falseness.

These sentences were the ones he had typed into his phone’s notes at two in the morning, flipping on the hotel bed like a pancake. Word by word. Delete, rewrite, delete, rewrite. Two hours of grinding until every line was unimpeachable.

Proper. Modest. Nothing you could pick at.

At the time, they had felt good.

Now he was standing next to the Volpi Cup, a follow-spot nailed to the top of his head, a thousand people sitting under him, and Tim Roth three metres off in the wings, and he understood the problem.

The words were too proper.

Proper to the point of being fake.

Humility is a virtue.

Too much humility is disrespect to the jury.

Tim Roth, Alexandre Desplat, Sandy Powell — those people had sat together, argued, voted.

Then they had put the cup into his hands.

And he was standing here saying the other actors in the field were all better than he was.

Then what was the jury? Blind?

Was he thanking them, or questioning their professional judgment?

❝

The way to respect a judgment is not to stand here pretending to be overwhelmed. It is to take the cup, and go make a better film.

— Baek Si-on, Sala Grande, the 71st Venice

“…But the jury chose me.”

“So I respect their judgment.”

“The way to respect it is not to stand here pretending to be overwhelmed. It is to tell you that I will take this cup, and go shoot a better film than this one.”

The applause that came up then was not polite.

Baek Si-on waited until it had fallen a notch, then continued.

“First I want to thank my uncle, Director Baek Jeong-hun. Thank you for forgetting to mention my name just now, when you collected your prize, because you were too nervous.”

The man just named covered his face with one hand.

Laughter went through the hall in the first real wave of the night.

Baek Si-on’s gaze left Baek Jeong-hun and landed on Choi Jin-ri.

“And I have to thank my partner, Choi Jin-ri. Without the performance you gave in this film, I would not be holding this cup today.”

He did not linger on her.

“I also want to thank my mother, Yoon Hye-ja.”

“She should be asleep by now.”

“But I still want to say — please be proud of your son.”

“He is standing on the stage of the oldest film festival in the world, with the best directors and actors on the planet sitting around him.”

“The man who just handed him the award is the lead of The Legend of 1900.”

“And your son has this cup.”

He looked down at the Volpi Cup.

“It’s a heavy one.”

The hall waited for the translation to catch up.

The applause came again.

He let it pass, then:

“Director Baek Jeong-hun already thanked everyone once in his own speech, but I am going to say it again. Thank you to every member of this crew who walked here with us — camera, sound, lighting, art, the grips. Thank you.”

Then he tipped his head a little to the side.

“And my cousin. Eun-ah. Are you crying?”

September 6 same hour Palazzo del Cinema · side hall, staff waiting area A wall-mounted television, and two people who would not admit they were crying

The side hall of the Palazzo del Cinema.

Staff rest area.

A holding space the venue kept for unbadged companions. A few rows of folding chairs against the wall. In the corner, a wall-mounted LCD, running the closing ceremony live from inside Sala Grande.

Baek Eun-ah sat in the folding chair nearest the screen.

Park Ji-hun sat beside her.

Neither of them moved.

When Baek Jeong-hun had taken the Lion of the Future — the Luigi De Laurentiis — Baek Eun-ah had already cried the first round.

An Italian staffer had glanced at her, probably assuming something had gone wrong at home.

She had just about stopped.

Then her cousin took Best Actor.

The second round upgraded itself into a flood.

She stared at the man on the television who was standing at the podium asking her whether she was crying.

Pressed a tissue over her face.

Shook her head, hard.

Park Ji-hun sat on her left.

His condition was a little better than Baek Eun-ah’s.

A little better meaning: he had not made a sound.

He had not wiped the tears, either.

Because wiping them would have been an admission that he was crying, and as an adult male stylist, crying at work felt, to him, a little humiliating.

He also could not stop.

Venice Best Actor.

His boss.

The person he dressed was, at this second, wearing the full look he had helped design, standing in front of every camera on the planet, holding the Volpi Cup.

From this second on.

Park Ji-hun’s résumé could carry a line:

Exclusive stylist — Baek Si-on, Best Actor, 71st Venice International Film Festival.

Exclusive.

Stylist.

He sniffed.

Turned his head and looked at Baek Eun-ah, who was coming apart in the next chair.

He pulled two tissues from the low table beside the folding chairs and held them out.

Side hall · folding chairs · two tissues

“Wipe.”

She pasted them to her face, mumbled thank you, then saw that the person handing them over was also crying.

“You’re crying?”

“I’m not. Allergies.”

“Your tears are on your trousers.”

“…The pollen count in Italian air is high.”

She looked at him once and did not expose him. Pulled two more tissues off the table and handed them back.

“Here. Pollen allergies still have to be wiped.”

He was silent for a second. Took them. Wiped.

September 7 3:10 am (KST) Seoul · Gangnam, Dispatch headquarters A live stream still talking, and a push alert that had already finished the story

Seoul, Gangnam-gu, Dispatch headquarters.

Three ten in the morning.

The newsroom was lit like noon.

The reporter named Kang Soo-hyuk was tipped sideways in a swivel chair, eyes on the left half of his monitor — the official Venice YouTube live stream — both hands throwing afterimages across a mechanical keyboard, logging every sentence Baek Si-on said, in real time.

Kang Soo-hyuk

Entertainment reporter · Dispatch

OC · night shift · the man who lost a news war by ten seconds

A dirt-digger by trade who, on this particular night, found a drop of patriotic heat still burning under the gossip. He would have had the flash. He did not. Someone else had written the ending before the speech was over.

On ordinary days, Dispatch did the kind of work that lived in other people’s bathrooms.

Tonight, watching a countryman take the roof off the most prestigious film festival in Europe, the little bit of hot blood still left in him caught fire anyway.

He needed the flash written in two minutes, screenshots dropped in, first over the line before every other outlet in the country.

A news war is a time war.

Every desk in Korea was watching the same live picture.

Everyone on the same starting block. The only variable was whose keyboard was faster.

The speech was still going.

Kang Soo-hyuk listened and, in his head, built the lede.

First paragraph: the two or three best lines from the speech. Second: the THR and Variety day-of reviews. Third…

His phone buzzed.

The screen lit.

A push alert.

Insight · flash · 03:10 KST Venice closes! Baek Si-on takes the Volpi Cup for Best Actor! First Korean Best Actor at any of the three major festivals!

Kang Soo-hyuk stared at the push for three seconds, then looked at the YouTube live picture.

What the hell.

On the screen, Baek Si-on was still standing in front of the microphone. The speech was not finished.

And you had already published?

What was this?

Precognition?

He tapped the push.

He wanted to see which lunatic outlet had the nerve to file before the award was even over.

The page loaded.

Site mark: Insight.

Insight · exclusive

byline · Son Nam-won

A complete article. Headline, lede, body, stills, keyword tags — nothing missing. Even a rundown of previous Volpi Cup Best Actors, laid out in a neat sidebar.

Obviously.

This was not “fast hands.”

Unless —

He had known the man would win. The copy had been written in advance. All that was left was the order to publish.

Kang Soo-hyuk scrolled to the bottom.

Lead byline: Son Nam-won.

He knew the name.

Out of OSEN. Later blacklisted by the big three. Had gone off and started a new-media shop called Insight.

The company had been standing for a little over a month.

Rumor was the whole shop, boss included, was four people.

A circus tent like that had beaten every desk on earth to the Venice Best Actor exclusive.

Kang Soo-hyuk thought for a second, then sent Lim Geun-ho a KakaoTalk.

KakaoTalk · Lim Geun-ho

Kang Soo-hyuk

Director! Insight’s Son Nam-won just flash-published Baek Si-on winning Best Actor, faster than the live stream. He has an inside line!

3:10 a.m. · read? · no reply

Three ten in the morning.

Lim Geun-ho did not answer.

Probably asleep.

Kang Soo-hyuk put the phone down and looked back at the YouTube live picture.

Baek Si-on had already walked off the podium.

He looked at the half-typed speech log in his Word document, then at the Insight piece on Naver, which the other outlets had already begun to syndicate in a frenzy.

He let out a breath.

September 7 the same minutes Seoul · Insight, a forty-square-metre live-work flat Fifty thousand reads, two iced Americanos, and a draft folder full of every possible prize

At the same moment.

Son Nam-won was tipped back in a swivel chair in Insight’s live-work apartment of less than forty square metres, watching the article’s live read-count skip past fifty thousand, the corner of his mouth bending up.

The reason he had beaten every outlet on the planet was not supernatural typing speed, and it was not psychic intuition.

It was an information gap.

This morning, in the small hours.

To be precise: this morning, Venice time.

After Baek Jeong-hun had dragged Baek Si-on out of bed with a “you’re not sleeping either, we’ll be insomniacs together,” Baek Si-on, in the middle of tossing, had sent Son Nam-won a message.

KakaoTalk · the inside line

Baek Si-on

The committee called. Stay for the closing tomorrow. There’s probably a prize. Don’t know which.

Venice, the small hours · one line · enough

Armed with that absolute inside line, Son Nam-won had drunk two iced Americanos in a row and turned himself, in front of the computer, into a merciless copy machine.

He had written finished drafts for every prize the committee might open.

Whatever blind box they finally unsealed, the second the live broadcast hammered it down he would only have to open the matching draft, drop in one live screenshot, and hit Publish with extreme cleanliness.

Ten seconds, total.

Fastest on earth.

September 6 still the ceremony Sala Grande · the walk back to the seat A cup on a pair of knees, and a camera with a red lamp that would not turn off

Baek Si-on walked back down the centre aisle with the Volpi Cup in his hand.

The follow-spot had already come off him. The house lights had returned to a normal warm white.

Even so, walking the two sides of the aisle, more than a few people leaned out to nod at him. Several were producers from other competing crews; they smiled and put out their hands, and Baek Si-on shook each one.

Back at the seat.

Choi Jin-ri was sitting there, chin tipped up a little, looking at him.

“Congratulations.”

Baek Si-on sat down beside her and set the Volpi Cup on his knee.

“Want to look?”

“Yes.”

Choi Jin-ri put out both hands. Baek Si-on passed the cup over.

She took it, both palms around it, and looked down.

The body of the Volpi Cup carried a relief of the Venetian lion. On the base plaque: the winner’s name, the year, the film.

Her thumb brushed lightly across the letters of “Baek Si-on.”

Then she felt something.

Turned her head.

About five metres away, at the edge of the aisle.

The festival’s official photographer was half-crouched on the floor.

A black professional body at his eye. A long lens pointed in their direction. The red working lamp above the lens was on, and staying on.

Which meant, at this exact second.

The picture of the two of them was being fed, live, onto tens of millions of screens watching the closing.

Choi Jin-ri hurriedly reached out and tapped Baek Si-on, who was in the middle of hanging the simultaneous-translation earpiece back over his ear.

He turned.

She pointed toward the aisle.

He took his hand off the earpiece at once, leaned half his body toward the camera in the aisle, and waved, smiling.

Choi Jin-ri smiled at the lens too, then turned the cup so the lettering on the base plaque faced the camera.

The photographer shot for five or six seconds, nodded, and, satisfied, swung the camera somewhere else.

Baek Si-on sat back, and tipped his head toward Baek Jeong-hun on the other side.

“Uncle.”

“Mm?”

“Can there still be any more surprises after this?”

Lion of the Future plus the Volpi Cup — uncle and nephew one apiece — was already a harvest that would fill the papers of the world.

But what if there was more? Grand Jury Prize? Golden Lion?

Baek Jeong-hun shook his head.

“No.”

“How do you figure?”

“Exclusivity rules.”

He dropped his voice very low.

“If the same film has already taken a major individual prize, the remaining core awards will not go to that film. The point is to spread the honours around, so more films get seen.”

He indicated the stage with his chin.

“This result, right now, is already the ceiling this film’s configuration can touch.”

What came after was exactly as Baek Jeong-hun had judged.

Envelope after envelope was opened on the stage. The spots all landed on names that had already established themselves in European cinema.

✦ the 71st Venice International Film Festival ✦

Roy Andersson — A Pigeon Sat on a Branch Reflecting on Existence

Swede. A living fossil of European art cinema.

The name The Green Fly was not read out by anyone on that stage again.

No one felt it as a loss.

Baek Jeong-hun did not.

A film of two hundred million won — a hundred and thirty thousand euros — taking the Lion of the Future and the Volpi Cup at Venice was already the absolute ceiling this film’s configuration could touch.

Baek Si-on did not.

The Volpi Cup was sitting on his knee.

Choi Jin-ri did not, either.

In fact, she had had a chance.

The Marcello Mastroianni Award for an Emerging Actor or Actress, given specifically to newcomers.

Choi Jin-ri’s work in The Green Fly had drawn more than a few positive notes from the press-screening critics. THR’s day-of capsule had used a subordinate clause on her specifically:

The Hollywood Reporter · capsule The female lead, Choi Jin-ri, completes a convincing character arc inside an extremely compressed performance space.

In the end that prize went to a young male actor in another French film.

Perhaps the competition really had been too strong.

Perhaps the committee had been doing regional balance.

For Choi Jin-ri, none of that mattered much.

She was sitting, at this moment, in the VIP of Sala Grande.

The seventy-first Venice International Film Festival, main competition, official selection, female lead.

Worldwide screening.

A standing ovation.

On one side of her, the man who had just taken the Volpi Cup. On the other, the director who had just taken the Lion of the Future.

That résumé, dropped into the Korean idol circuit — what grade of existence was it?

Nuclear-weapon grade.

If, someday, the fans went to war on the internet.

Sulli’s fans would only have to keep repeating this résumé, and it would be enough to break the other side.

The last item of the awards ceremony ended.

Luisa Ranieri delivered the closing remarks in Italian and then in English. The house lights all came up. The thousand-odd seats of Sala Grande began, in ones and twos, to stand.

A buzz of conversation filled the hall again.

Baek Si-on had just gotten to his feet when a staffer with a headset walked up quickly.

71st Venice International Film Festival · official call

Mr. Baek Si-on. Mr. Baek Jeong-hun. Congratulations. The committee invites all winners to the shooting area behind the stage for this year’s official group photograph.

white backdrop · softboxes · one frame for the archive

Baek Si-on looked at Baek Jeong-hun.

Baek Jeong-hun nodded, Lion of the Future in one hand, the other straightening his lapel.

“Let’s go.”

Baek Si-on took the Volpi Cup in his hand and followed the staffer toward the stage.

Two steps in, he looked back.

Choi Jin-ri was standing beside the seat. She saw him looking, and waved once.

He turned, and followed Baek Jeong-hun and the staffer through the emptying crowd toward the stage of Sala Grande.

The white backdrop was already up.

Softboxes from both sides and from directly above. The photographer in front of the tripod was still dialling in the camera.

The winners arrived in ones and twos.

Staff began arranging the stand.

Golden Lion in the centre.

Everyone else fanned out from the middle according to the weight of the prize.

Baek Si-on was placed second to Roy Andersson’s left. Baek Jeong-hun one person further along from him.

Everyone held their own trophy.

Roy Andersson

Director · Golden Lion, 71st Venice

Real-name counterpart · Sweden · A Pigeon Sat on a Branch Reflecting on Existence

A living fossil of European art cinema, and the man who would, later that night, walk over with a glass of red and tell a twenty-two-year-old that the young man should drink. On the official photograph he only turned his head and said, You are very young.

The photographer put his eye to the viewfinder, then lifted his head.

“Everybody ready?”

Everyone faced the lens.

Baek Si-on held the Volpi Cup at his chest.

Roy Andersson turned his head and looked at him.

“You are very young.”

You are very young.

Baek Si-on turned his head and looked at him.

“Thank you.”

The corner of Roy Andersson’s mouth moved. It probably counted as a smile.

The shutter went.

Click.

The shutter went twice more.

The photographer came out from behind the viewfinder and put up a thumb.

“Perfect.”

The stand broke.

The winners began talking in twos and threes. Some shook hands. Some exchanged contacts with the person beside them.

Baek Si-on shifted the Volpi Cup from his left hand to his right, and was about to walk back, when the staffer with the headset appeared again.

He had two extra cards in his hand. Thick ivory paper, the festival’s gold logo hot-stamped on the front.

Official closing dinner

Tonight at nine, in the open garden beside the Palazzo del Cinema. The committee invites all winners to attend.

ivory card · gold logo · a map of the garden on the back

He handed one card to Baek Si-on and one to Baek Jeong-hun.

Baek Jeong-hun took his, glanced at it, flipped it. Address of the garden, and a simple map.

Baek Si-on took his too, slid the invitation into the inside pocket of his suit, and looked at Baek Jeong-hun.

“Uncle. Are we going?”

Baek Jeong-hun put his invitation away as well, and patted the breast of his suit.

“We’re going.”

He paused.

“I heard there’s wine.”

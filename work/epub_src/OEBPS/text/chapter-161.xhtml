Chapter One Hundred Sixty One

To Avoid Being a Sack, She Confessed

December 7 · then December 13 Nomination morning · a wrap at 11 a.m. · five days out from the concert Seoul · the Blue Dragon list — Hwaseong Haenggung — Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium One award list opens a door, one phone call pays for the doorway, and one crown prince is finally allowed out of the palace to go be a singer again.

December 7.

The 35th Blue Dragon Film Awards published its nomination list.

the 35th Blue Dragon Film Awards · nominations · ‘Green Bottle Fly’

BEST FILM — ‘Green Bottle Fly’

BEST DIRECTOR — ‘Green Bottle Fly’

BEST ACTOR — ‘Green Bottle Fly’

BEST ACTRESS — ‘Green Bottle Fly’

Four nominations. Three of them arrived without a single dissenting voice in the industry. The fourth had the word “encouragement” filed politely under it.

Compared with the three nominations nobody could find fault with — Best Film, Best Director, Best Actor — Choi Jin-ri’s Best Actress nod carried a heavier load of well-meaning.

Not because she had acted badly.

Quite the opposite. A young actress who came out of an idol group — one who, not long ago, had been harassed off all activity by the internet — clawing her way onto a Blue Dragon Best Actress list through an independent film, said on its own that the industry was willing to look at her again.

But to claim she could go head-to-head for the trophy against queens of the caliber of Jeon Do-yeon, Son Ye-jin, and Kim Hye-soo — that was, frankly, bullying the newcomer.

Blue Dragon obviously knew this too.

They had to do it anyway. Korean cinema spent every year watching the same handful of queens duel each other; if young actors never got a ticket into the room, the awards themselves would slowly go stale. Blue Dragon needed to keep a film award’s dignity intact — and needed to hand the industry a signal at the same time.

Choi Jin-ri happened to be that signal.

…

Hwaseong Haenggung. The Sado set.

Song Kang-ho was drinking hot tea in the rest area when the nomination news reached him.

He was in the Best Actor race this year too — for The Attorney.

Which meant the person standing on the road to Baek Si-on’s Best Actor trophy was, of all people on earth, his own screen father.

Yeongjo.

Song Kang-ho read the list once on his phone, got up, and found Baek Si-on in the makeup chair.

“Si-on-yah.”

“Mm?”

“Congratulations in advance.”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

“For what?”

“Blue Dragon Best Actor.”

With Baek Si-on’s current momentum, the Blue Dragon organizers could hardly pretend not to see him. The Volpi Cup sat on the shelf; the first-in-Korean-film-history title sat beside it. However fierce Choi Min-sik’s Roaring Currents, however solid Song Kang-ho’s The Attorney, this year’s table had exactly one seat facing the meat, and it was Baek Si-on’s.

Originally, Baek Si-on had planned to be modest about it. The Attorney was one of Song Kang-ho’s most important films — box office, reputation, social weight, all of it load-bearing.

But with the words already at his lips, he decided modesty had no flavor this season.

Besides: his Crown Prince Sado spent every shooting day being pointed at and berated by Song Kang-ho’s Yeongjo, spirit compressed to the breaking point. And now, at the Blue Dragon table, Baek Si-on winning over Song Kang-ho —

how was that not revenge on Sado’s behalf?

“Then I’ll do my best to win it.”

Song Kang-ho froze for a beat.

Then he laughed out loud.

“Ya, you kid — you can’t even be bothered to fake modesty now?”

“You said it was certain.”

“I said it’s likely. You saying it yourself is called no modesty.”

“Then I take it back.”

“Too late.”

“……”

Song Kang-ho left, and the makeup artist went back to work with the powder puff. Hwaseong’s winter ran drier than Seoul’s, and the moment the heating came on, base makeup started to float.

Baek Si-on picked up his phone from the vanity, scanned the news alerts, and opened one particular chat thread.

He typed.

Not so long ago he had stood at the Green Bottle Fly party declaring that Ten-Million Leading Lady sounded better — he hadn’t expected the sentence to develop an echo this fast.

Waiting for notice, in audition language, was usually a polite dialect of “we’ll see if anything better walks in.”

Baek Si-on thought for a moment, then looked up at Baek Eun-ah, who was working through paperwork at the back of the room.

“Eun-ah.”

“Here.”

“Get me director Yeon Sang-ho’s contact number.”

“Who’s Yeon Sang-ho?”

“An animation director.”

“Okay — hold on.”

She looked down and started digging through her contacts — a contacts list that had stopped being a trainee’s phone book about six months ago. A few minutes and two calls later, she waited another two minutes, and a number arrived. She handed the phone over.

“Got it.”

Baek Si-on dialed. It rang several times before connecting. The voice on the other end was careful.

Baek Si-on → Yeon Sang-ho · December 7 · from a makeup chair to an animation director’s office

Hello?

Director Yeon Sang-ho?

Speaking.

Hello. This is Baek Si-on.

A pause on the line. Then the voice came back less certain of itself.

Baek Si-on… the actor?

Yes.

Ah — hello! Hello.

The caution was gone; the voice had doubled its pulse. Nobody in Korean film could be blamed for the reaction. The Volpi Cup. ‘Green Bottle Fly’ past a million admissions. The Blue Dragon Best Actor favorite. Currently shooting Lee Joon-ik’s ‘Sado’ across from Song Kang-ho. When a man like that called out of nowhere, a director’s first surviving assumption was: he wants to be in my film. Yeon Sang-ho’s heart rate did the arithmetic — a commercial genre picture with Baek Si-on attached meant investment, distribution, and overseas sales jumping a whole classification. Then Baek Si-on spoke.

Does your film need investment right now?

Yeon Sang-ho: “?” He nearly asked the man to repeat himself. Not the role? Not the script? Not the schedule? He opens with — does it need money? What species of self-introduction was this. He steadied himself.

Financing is already in progress. Of course — it’s a commercial picture, a budget never suffers from being larger.

Smoothly said. Plainly meant: not lacking. But capable of lacking. Money, for a film crew, had never once met the word “enough.”

Baek Si-on acknowledged it and moved on.

This morning you auditioned an actress called Choi Jin-ri.

And there it was — the reason for the call, arriving at last. Choi Jin-ri. Sulli. ‘Green Bottle Fly’’s leading lady. The newly minted Blue Dragon Best Actress nominee. Yeon Sang-ho had indeed seen her today: strong image, strong camera sense, and more restraint in her performance than he had budgeted for.

Name a figure. I’d like her in your film.

Beside the makeup chair, Baek Eun-ah had already closed her eyes. Of course. The makeup artist’s hand stopped mid-powder, then decided it had heard nothing, and resumed.

Yeon Sang-ho let several seconds go by.

Actor Baek — casting isn’t something you can settle with investment alone.

Careful. A man that size could not be afforded as an enemy — but a film was not charity, and not a favors ledger either. He couldn’t hand out a role on one phone call.

I know.

Yeon Sang-ho: “……” You know, and you asked me to name a figure?

What I mean is: if you already think she fits, and the only worry is her market risk — I can absorb part of that risk. And if you think she doesn’t fit, then treat this call as never having happened.

The expression on the other end of the line could be heard relaxing several degrees. Now THAT could be listened to. This was not a man buying a role. This was a man handing a director a staircase: if Choi Jin-ri had passed the audition and the only hesitation was the idol label, the commentary risk, the commercial stability — then he would underwrite exactly that part, with money and resources. But if the director judged she couldn’t play it, he wouldn’t touch the film. A different animal entirely.

Yeon Sang-ho considered.

Actress Choi Jin-ri did well today.

He did not praise her all the way. Directors never did — keep three-tenths in reserve, and the heart has room to turn around.

But we’ll need an internal discussion.

Understood. If the discussion puts her on the shortlist, I’d like to be notified. Investment amount and terms — have the production side contact my studio.

That settled it. He was actually investing. And investing cleanly.

Actor Baek — may I ask one thing?

Mm.

Why does she matter this much to you?

Baek Si-on looked into the makeup mirror: a man in Crown Prince Sado’s costume, wearing Baek Si-on’s own composure on top of it.

Because she wants to be an actress.

…I understand.

I’ll wait for your message.

Right.

The call ended. Baek Si-on handed the phone back to Baek Eun-ah, picked up his own, and typed.

Lunch break. The temporary rest room received a visitor: the head of SK Telecom.

The man had come quietly — no entourage, just one lawyer and one business lead.

Baek Si-on’s current SK Telecom deal was a short quarterly one, due to expire in January. The two-plus months of it had gone unusually well for both sides: his heat had climbed in a straight line from Venice to the Billboard charts, the AMAs, and the MAMA Artist of the Year — SK’s ad placements had effectively been riding a tailwind. And Baek Si-on’s side genuinely needed SK’s network standing at the free concert: sixty thousand people in one stadium, and the nightmare scenarios — fans unable to send a photo, press unable to file, ticket authentication lagging — any one of them could raise Baek Eun-ah’s blood pressure on the spot.

So the renewal was relaxed from the first minute. The head pushed the folder across.

“Two years. Four billion won.”

“Brand endorsement, ad shoots, offline events — plus priority rights on the communications support for this free concert.”

Seo Eun-ju had sent the redlined version to Baek Eun-ah the night before; the key clauses were already confirmed. Today’s meeting was, in substance, paper discipline: writing down what had already been agreed.

Baek Si-on picked up the pen and signed on the spot.

the SK Telecom renewal · one page, no drama

01 — Term: two years, effective January. 2 YRS

02 — Value: four billion won, brand endorsement + ad shoots + offline events. ₩4B

03 — Priority communications partnership for the December 18 free concert: fan traffic, press filing, ticket authentication. SIGNED

Redlines closed the night before by Seo Eun-ju; executed at a lunch table in nineteen minutes. Both sides shook hands before the food got cold.

“A pleasure working with you.”

Baek Si-on shook the hand too.

“A pleasure.”

Watching the contract close, Baek Eun-ah felt one more brick of calm settle into place. She had come to love this species of deal: fair price, clear clauses, clean responsibilities — and no half hour at a conference table of “we deeply believe in Mr. Baek’s potential” ending in an offer priced like discount radishes at a market stall.

After the SK Telecom party left, the rest room went quiet. Baek Eun-ah closed the contract folder, and then, abruptly:

“Cousin.”

“Mm?”

“Something happened on the SK duty-free side.”

Baek Si-on looked up.

“What thing?”

“The Blue House’s VIP side pulled their license.”

“Don’t tell me it went to Hyundai Department Store.”

Baek Eun-ah nodded.

Baek Si-on: “……”

A duty-free license looked like a commercial permit. In practice it was heavy enough to make two chaebols reweigh each other on the scales. Reel one in, let one go. Give one a sweet, give the other a knock. The people over at the Blue House, it turned out, were not entirely unskilled at cutting meat with a small knife.

As for whose idea it had been —

the shadow of that “teacher” crossed through Baek Si-on’s mind.

It seemed Baek Jeong-hun’s new film concept was growing real soil by the day.

…

December 13. Hwaseong Haenggung.

Over these days, Baek Si-on’s scenes had been closing out one by one. The ink painting in the study. The archery in the courtyard. The brief joy after the child’s birth. The stretches of what could still be called daily life — in the narrow window before father and son tore the whole house apart.

Eleven in the morning.

Lee Joon-ik sat behind the monitor. In the frame, Baek Si-on set down his brush and looked up toward the window. Hwaseong’s winter light fell across the paper. An ink painting that would never be finished. A life that had never been allowed to be.

A few seconds passed.

Lee Joon-ik finally spoke:

“Pass.”

the wrap call · Hwaseong Haenggung, 11:04 a.m.

The assistant director grabbed the megaphone at once.

“Baek Si-on-ssi — that is a WRAP on all Crown Prince Sado scenes for ‘Sado’!”

Applause broke out immediately. Staff brought a bouquet up to director Lee Joon-ik, and he carried it over himself.

“You worked hard.”

Baek Si-on took the flowers with both hands and bowed.

“Thank you, director.”

Song Kang-ho came over and clapped him on the shoulder.

“Go sing, Crown Prince.”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

“Has Royal Father given permission?”

Song Kang-ho laughed once.

“For today, you are permitted to leave the palace.”

Baek Si-on looked down at the crown prince robes on his body.

From Green Bottle Fly to Sado — he seemed to keep dying inside films. Fortunately, outside the set, sixty thousand people were waiting for him to walk out alive.

the group photo · evidence for the record

The script supervisor herded everyone in. Baek Si-on stood center with the bouquet, Lee Joon-ik and Song Kang-ho flanking him; camera, wardrobe, props, makeup, and floor crews — dozens of people — packed in tight. Someone crouched in the front row. Someone stretched onto tiptoe at the back. Somebody held two fingers high, determined to leave evidence of themselves in the frame.

“Eyes on the camera!”

“Three — two — one!”

Click.

The frame froze. Baek Si-on’s part in Sado was officially over. The remaining shoot would follow the young heir’s succession and the political settling of accounts — Crown Prince Sado’s death would stay in the film.

But Baek Si-on himself could now walk out through the palace gates.

He handed the bouquet to Baek Eun-ah and got into the car to change. No wrap party. No rest. The van pointed straight from Hwaseong Haenggung at Jamsil, Seoul — because the free concert was five days out.

…

Two thirty in the afternoon. Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium.

A venue that had been bare steel and cable a few days ago now had the outline of a real concert.

Somewhere in stack three sat a pallet of cushions priced at three thousand won, smelling of nothing at all, audited by the industry’s most serious nose.

Baek Si-on came in through the staff corridor into the infield. Music was playing over the stage speakers.

“Do it do it Chu~” — “Do it do it Chu~”

The four girls of Red Velvet were rehearsing on the stage.

Baek Si-on stopped to watch for a moment from the infield, then turned toward the broadcast work area.

Rows of monitors glowed: main-stage wide, thrust-stage cameras, floor section, stand wide shots, the crane, the big-screen output. The broadcast director — sent by tvN, a man with grey in his resume — wore headphones and walked the rehearsal feeds through with his camera operators.

Baek Si-on walked over.

“Hello.”

The man stood up at once.

“Baek Si-on-ssi, hello.”

They shook hands briefly. Baek Si-on looked at the screens.

“In your view — how do they get the most exposure?”

The tvN director was an experienced animal, and he answered on pure industry instinct.

“This group doesn’t have a sharp identity yet, so they walk the early Girls’ Generation road: push the most striking child out front to catch the passersby, then let her popularity feed the whole group.”

Baek Si-on turned his head.

“I mean at this concert.”

The director: “……” Understood. What concept Red Velvet grew into next year, when they changed styles, who stood at center — that was Lee Soo-man’s ulcer, and the planning department’s. What Baek Si-on cared about was that Red Velvet’s trip to this sixty-thousand-person stage and tvN’s live broadcast would not be a wasted journey.

The director was a smart man too. He turned, pulled up the console fader, switched both main-screen inputs over — and held the talkback key.

“Camera seven, stand by.”

“Follow the girl center, white top.”

“Face close-up.”

The monitor cut over — and Bae Joo-hyun’s face arrived on the main screen, several dozen square meters of it. In rehearsal mode her expression ran at half power, but the moment the lens pushed in, the smile rose on instinct.

CAM 07 — member singles, in order

The frame moved: Kang Seul-gi. Son Seung-wan. Park Soo-young. Then back to Bae Joo-hyun.

RESULT: the room went quiet.

The director turned to Baek Si-on and spread his hands.

“You see.”

“……”

It had to be admitted: this was, in fact, the simplest way for this group to catch a passerby’s eye. The entertainment industry was sometimes very complicated. And sometimes very simple. A sufficiently stunning face, projected onto a few dozen square meters of screen, solved a great many problems.

Bae Joo-hyun, out on the stage, knew nothing of what had just happened in the director’s area. She was still walking her marks with the music.

Chorus ended. The four shifted to the side. Bae Joo-hyun turned —

and the next second, the girl on the screen vanished.

Baek Si-on looked at the wide monitor. She had fallen. The three beside her froze mid-move and dropped down around her at once.

The broadcast director grabbed the talkback.

“Music stop.”

Cut. The backing track died mid-phrase.

Baek Si-on watched for two seconds. Bae Joo-hyun still hadn’t stood up. He took out his phone and dialed Baek Eun-ah.

“Which company built the stage?”

She answered fast.

“Cousin, the stage is fine. Staff saw it — her heel went over sideways on the landing.”

“The shoe?”

“Performance heels. Too high.”

“Got it.”

He hung up, rounded the broadcast console, and headed for the stage.

the seven-centimeter decision · rehearsal floor, Jamsil December 13 · 2:47 p.m. · gravity: unsympathetic

The top and leggings A clean white long-sleeve top and dark practice leggings — rehearsal-issue Red Velvet, the uniform of four girls grinding marks in an empty stadium while the real costumes hang in a wardrobe truck somewhere. Nothing about it is styled. Everything about it is honest.
The heels Seven centimeters of stiletto, selected — officially — for line and silhouette, and — privately — because three centimeters of stubbornness had once failed to survive one group photo. Chosen by nobody’s company but her own non-refusal. On flat studio floors, survivable. On a December stage still wearing its construction dust, a risk assessment was owed; nobody had filed one.
The landing The choreography survived; the shoe did not. One heel tipped over on touchdown and took the whole concept of “seven centimeters of stubbornness” down with it. The ankle now held the entire negotiating position.

White top, dark leggings, seven centimeters of stubbornness — and a heel that lost the argument.

The stage crew checked their own work twice that afternoon and found it innocent. The shoe was charged with everything.

On the stage, Park Soo-young saw him first.

“Baek Si-on-sunbaenim.”

Kang Seul-gi and Son Seung-wan scrambled up too, and the three bowed together.

“Hello, sunbaenim!”

Baek Si-on nodded in reply, crouched beside Bae Joo-hyun, and looked — once — at the ankle she was holding, and once at the absurdly tall performance shoe still hanging off her other foot.

“Can you stand?”

Bae Joo-hyun tried to push herself up, hissed in a breath at the pain — and still went stubborn.

“…I can.”

“Then you can’t.”

Bae Joo-hyun: “……”

Before the sentence finished landing, the world rotated. The next second she was hanging upside down over Baek Si-on’s shoulder — a textbook fireman’s carry, so textbook it contained not one milliliter of shoujo manga.

the three-girl reaction line · world, temporarily silent

Kang Seul-gi: “……”

Son Seung-wan: “……”

Park Soo-young: “……”

The world went quiet. Even Baek Eun-ah, hurrying over from the far side, stopped dead when she saw it. Three different expressions, one event: worry from Seulgi with a little bewilderment folded in; Son Seung-wan fighting for rational thought; and Park Soo-young biting her lip hard enough to leave a mark — she wanted to laugh. She did not dare.

Slung over his back, hair hanging down, Bae Joo-hyun spoke in a muffled voice.

“…Sunbaenim.”

“Mm.”

“This position is… a little strange.”

Baek Si-on kept walking off the stage.

“More normal than watching you hop on one foot.”

Bae Joo-hyun: “……”

The logic wasn’t wrong. But her world was currently upside down.

A few staff members in the distance rotated their heads away with tremendous professionalism. See nothing you shouldn’t see. Ask nothing you shouldn’t ask. The live-event field manual achieved total unanimity in that moment.

“Could we use a more… dignified position?” she asked, small.

Baek Si-on asked:

“What position?”

Bae Joo-hyun went quiet. Then quieter.

“…Princess carry.”

Baek Si-on’s steps didn’t stop.

“Are you a princess?”

Bae Joo-hyun: “……”

Her face went hot all at once. Fortunately her hair was hanging down over half of it.

“…No.”

“Then endure it for now.”

He kept walking toward the medical room. On his back, Bae Joo-hyun went still — and accepted, for the time being, her fate as a sack.

“Someone’s hurt an ankle,” Baek Eun-ah was saying into her phone behind them, to the medical team. “No — you don’t need to come over.”

She looked up at the figure being carried away ahead of her.

“The… the patient has already been transported.”

…

They had gone some distance when Bae Joo-hyun spoke up again.

“Sunbaenim.”

“What now?”

“Could you…”

She stopped for a good while.

“Ask me that question from before? One more time?”

Baek Si-on’s steps slowed slightly.

“Which one?”

“The princess one.”

Baek Si-on stopped. He apparently found the request completely baffling — but he asked it.

“Are you a princess?”

“At home…”

She paused.

“Mom and Dad call me that.”

Baek Si-on was quiet for two seconds.

Then he set her down. Her toes had barely touched the ground when his arm collected her back — a re-rigged hold, and the next second she was lifted horizontally.

This time, it was finally a princess carry.

Bae Joo-hyun’s arms went around his neck on pure reflex. Her view of the world righted itself from ground-level to standing. She looked up — far too close, close enough to see the faint stubble coming in on Baek Si-on’s chin — and immediately looked away.

A few seconds later, she peeked again.

The truth was she had always considered being near Baek Si-on something vaguely supernatural — as if luck simply improved the closer you stood. But only now did she discover that so-called luck could be this specific.

Specific enough to walk out from behind a broadcast console.

Specific enough to judge, correctly, that she couldn’t stand.

Specific enough to actually carry her off the stage.

Even if the first posture had resembled a sack.

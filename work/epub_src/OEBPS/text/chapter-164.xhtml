Chapter One Hundred Sixty Four

Meeting the Parents, From the Audience Seats, Without Warning

December 18 · 3:00 p.m. — until the first drumbeat Gates, giveaways, and one cushion discovery · a seatmate from a film set · countdown to light Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium · the thank-you concert, day of A small vlogger arrives to film freebies and finds a mother in the next seat; a mother arrives to be one face in sixty thousand and finds a girl wearing her son’s coat; and a stadium arrives at one shared word.

December 18. Mid-afternoon.

The area outside Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium had heated up early. The sky wasn’t dark yet, but the winter wind had zero manners — it came sideways across the open ground and set the support banners on the fences snapping like sails.

A young guy in his early twenties came jogging out of the subway station. His name was Kim Min-jun. Small-time YouTuber: modest subscriber count, content history mostly K-idol merch unboxings and new-food first tastes.

His fans’ reviews of him were remarkably stable.

Shoots average. Eats deliciously.

Today he had a compact camera on a neck strap, a phone in hand, and the lens up every ten steps.

“Hey everyone, hello!”

He waved at the lens, nose tip already wind-burned pink.

“Today is a VERY special day, as you can see behind me—”

He swung the camera around. A long queue already stretched away from the stadium gates — staff holding zone signs, security keeping the entrances orderly, and in the distance, enormous, the concert’s key visual poster.

the key visual · main gate poster · black on white on black

BAEK SI-ON THANK-YOU CONCERT

Black background. White type. Baek Si-on’s face printed dead center, looking at sixty thousand people who hadn’t paid a won to be looked at back.

Kim Min-jun swung the lens back to his own face, unable to keep the smugness in.

“Yes — I got a ticket.”

He produced a printed entry confirmation from his inside pocket and waved it at the camera.

“Don’t ask how I got it.”

“If you ask — I spent this lifetime’s entire luck budget on today.”

Two girls in the queue overheard and laughed.

“You’d better treasure it. The people who didn’t get one today will genuinely be sour at you.”

Kim Min-jun stuffed the confirmation straight back into his pocket.

“Hear that, everyone? The threats have already started.”

The queue moved. Entry was tighter than he’d imagined: ticket verification first, then ID, then security — and then a staff member reminding every single person that filming the crowd atmosphere was fine, but no flash once the show started, and no parking a lens on other audience members’ faces.

When his turn came, the security guard looked at the camera.

“You’re shooting video?”

“Yep — YouTube.”

The guard nodded.

“Outside and entry are fine. But when you upload — if any bystander’s face is identifiable, blur it out.”

Kim Min-jun threw a peace sign at his own lens.

“You heard it, everyone — this event briefs its security about BYSTANDER PRIVACY.”

“Everywhere else I’ve ever shot, security only tells me to stop blocking traffic. This is better service than my university course-registration system had.”

He laughed at his own joke before the audience could.

Past the ticket gates, the first distribution point sat right along the approach. Volunteers in matching Under Armour down coats stood behind tables stacked with boxes of light sticks.

“Light stick — please take one.”

“One per person.”

“Please check the battery works before seven tonight.”

Kim Min-jun took his and inspected it on camera: white handle, Baek Si-on name mark printed on the grip, translucent round cap on top, and when switched on — a very clean, cold white light.

“Free.”

“Everyone — this is also free.”

Another staffer handed him a small pouch.

“Hand warmer — please take one.”

Kim Min-jun stared at the hand warmer in silence for a good three seconds, and moved on.

The outer concourse was worse — in the best possible way. A row of food trucks stood along the side. Not the decorative sponsor kind that only exists for photographs; these were actively serving. Fish cake soup. Tteokbokki. Gimbap. Hot dogs. And a hot drinks section: coffee, yuja tea, hot chocolate.

the concourse signage · laminated, honest

FREE WITH ENTRY WRISTBAND

ONE SERVING PER PERSON PER ZONE — PLEASE DON’T WASTE

The queues were long and the lines were polite. Volunteers steered people through and reminded them, item by item, to keep warm.

A few fans stood to one side, cupping steaming fish cake soup and talking in low voices.

“He’s actually insane, right? A free concert and he’s FEEDING people?”

“It’s more than that — the notice said the subway’s running two hours extra tonight.”

“Daebak…”

Kim Min-jun collected a hot yuja tea, and then a fish cake soup, on camera and then in person. His plan had been to film the freebies. Somewhere between the steam and the first sip, the fish cake soup captured him personally.

Inside, the floor-entrance flow had already begun. He followed his zone line in — and the moment he cleared the tunnel, the stadium simply opened. Empty stands climbing away on both sides; the main stage lit with test rigs in the distance; the giant screens cycling the key visual while the broadcast team flipped test feeds; and the thrust stage running from the main stage deep into the floor, its edge light strip already glowing a faint white ring.

“Guys — are you seeing this?”

“THIS is tonight’s stage.”

“I’m already regretting that second yuja tea, because if I get emotional later my bladder and I are going to have a problem—”

A fan nearby laughed and pointed.

“They’ve got crowd-flow signs up by the restrooms too.”

Kim Min-jun turned, and the respect was instant and total.

“Baek Si-on has considered even my bladder.”

“……”

He found his seat, checked the ticket number against the zone sign, looked up — correct, this was it. And then he noticed.

Every single seat had a thick cushion on it. Gray shell, piped edges, a tiny Baek Si-on concert mark printed dead center. He pressed it with a palm. Soft. And underneath — an anti-slip layer.

He sat down on it, and his entire face reorganized.

“Everyone.”

“I am currently sitting on a free cushion.”

“I am holding a free light stick, I have a free hand warmer in my pocket, and there is a free yuja tea in my stomach.”

He paused for effect.

“This concert charges no admission, but Baek Si-on has already spent the money on our butts, our hands, our stomachs, and our feelings.”

The fans around him lost it.

Someone shouted:

“And the EYES! Looking at that face later is free too!”

Kim Min-jun swung the camera to the stage.

“Correct.”

“And the eyes.”

Choi Jin-ri reached Jamsil after six.

She was late — and the main cause wasn’t traffic.

It was the closet. From mid-afternoon she had stood in front of it, deliberating. Wear her usual coat? Dress it down a little, be invisible? In the end she had pulled out Baek Si-on’s Under Armour down coat and put it on. In the mirror: a kid who had stolen an adult’s clothes.

She liked it.

Not because it was warm.

Well — partly because it was warm.

But mostly: the coat was his.

She zipped it halfway, looked down at the front of it, and informed herself, with great seriousness: only because it’s cold. Mm. Completely reasonable.

the coat, worn to be invisible · apartment mirror, before leaving December 18 · 4:50 p.m. · logic: airtight, officially

The coat, again The long black Under Armour down coat, several sizes too generous, zipped to half mast, sleeves consuming her hands to the fingertips. On the hanger it is outerwear. On her it is a decision — the kind you make twice: once with your arms, and once, later, in front of a mirror, while assuring the reflection that this is a temperature matter and nothing else.
The reasoning Presented for the record: it was cold. Counter-evidence, also on the record: her own perfectly adequate coat, hanging exactly one meter away, unworn. The court rules in favor of the cold. The court is not impartial. The court sleeps in this apartment.

Only because it’s cold. Mm. Completely reasonable.

The mirror, like every mirror before it, declined to comment and recorded everything.

The car stopped at the staff entrance. The moment she stepped out, she spotted Baek Eun-ah waiting at the channel mouth.

Before a show like tonight’s, the busiest person on the property was not Baek Si-on. It was Baek Eun-ah. As de facto head of reception she had to greet his entire industry on his behalf: who arrived, and where they sat; who could not be seated adjacent; who needed a manager pass into the backstage; who must on no account appear next to whom in a press frame. All of it, her desk.

She had just delivered Yoo In-na backstage to go hover adoringly around Lee Ji-eun — and had come straight over to collect Choi Jin-ri.

“Eonni.”

Baek Eun-ah waved. Choi Jin-ri walked over, smiling.

“Eun-ah, you’re working so hard.”

“Not hard.”

Baek Eun-ah’s eyes passed over the down coat, and she said nothing at all.

They went through the staff channel. The stadium already held a good number of spectators; the cold-white light sticks were coming on in scattered points, like stars that had dropped into the venue early. Outside noise stayed outside; inside there was only the low hum of talk and the occasional sub-bass thump from stage checks.

Baek Eun-ah led her toward the floor front.

“Your seat is here.”

She checked the seat number against her sheet.

“This one.”

Then she looked at the time.

“Eonni, I have to go collect more people.”

“Go, go — I’m fine.”

“Message me if anything.”

“Mm.”

And she was off at a jog. Choi Jin-ri watched her go and felt a genuine pang on her behalf. This concert looked like a gift Baek Si-on was handing sixty thousand people. In practice, every single screw on the site had a human being tightening it — and Baek Eun-ah was the human being holding the most screws.

Choi Jin-ri had barely settled into the seat when she realized the woman beside her looked familiar.

She looked again. Then down, thinking. Where? A set? A ceremony? An interview? She went through her memory in circles — and then it landed.

Green Bottle Fly, the set — she had brought Baek Si-on a meal once, and director Baek Jeong-hun had greeted the visitor warmly.

This was Baek Si-on’s mother.

Professor Yoon Hye-ja.

Oh no.

Choi Jin-ri shot to her feet and bowed.

“Good evening, ma’am.”

Yoon Hye-ja looked up. Her gaze landed on the girl’s face first. Then traveled, entirely without ceremony, over the enormous down coat. Then she nodded.

“Hello.”

Choi Jin-ri was nervous.

“I’m Choi Jin-ri.”

“I know.”

Yoon Hye-ja folded the handout sheet she was holding.

“Sit. You’re blocking the row behind you.”

Choi Jin-ri: “……”

She sat at once, both hands parked on her knees like a student at inspection, brain sprinting. Say something. Anything. You can’t just sit here in silence. She ransacked her mind for a topic.

“Ma’am — is your work usually very busy?”

The words left her mouth and she wanted to bite her own tongue off. What a question! The woman was a professor at Ewha. Whether her job was busy was nobody’s business, least of all a fidgeting idol’s. She pressed on anyway.

“I meant — coming out for a concert today, does it cut into your rest?”

Yoon Hye-ja looked at her.

“No.”

“Oh.”

The topic died on the table. Choi Jin-ri mentally struck herself. Who ENDS a conversation with an elder on the word “oh”? She looked down at the down coat — obviously several sizes too big — and tried to reroute.

“Ma’am, when Si-on-oppa is at home, he usually—”

The sentence jammed in the gate. Because she realized, with dawning horror, that she had no idea what Baek Si-on was like at home. She had no evidence he possessed “home” as a concept. He seemed to be permanently at work — on sets, in studios, shuttling between ceremonies and schedules, forever.

For the first time in her life, Choi Jin-ri discovered that a mouth which could survive variety-show interviews could fail her completely at the worst possible moment.

Yoon Hye-ja set the handout aside.

“You don’t need to be nervous because I’m Baek Si-on’s mother.”

Choi Jin-ri looked up. The tone was, to the decibel, the same one her son used — the one where you cannot tell comfort from a weather report.

“Today I’m just one audience member.”

Choi Jin-ri blinked — and then, slowly, came down out of the panic.

“Mm.”

She suddenly wanted to laugh. The two of them really were mother and son.

She tucked her hands into the coat’s sleeves and said, softly:

“Then I’ll be an audience member too.”

Yoon Hye-ja’s eyes went once more to the oversized coat.

“You already look the part.”

Choi Jin-ri: “……”

She was no longer entirely certain whether “the part” meant an audience member — or something else.

The voices came back one over another: lighting normal, audio normal, floor normal, stands normal. Then:

“Broadcast confirms — standing by.”

the countdown · the house lights dying tier by tier

“Ten.”

“Nine.”

“Eight.”

The stadium’s main lights went out bank by bank. The crowd’s murmur fell off a cliff.

“Seven.”

“Six.”

“Five.”

The big screen went black. Sixty thousand people held one shared breath, as if the same giant hand had pressed down on all of them at once.

“Four.”

“Three.”

“Two.”

“One.”

“Go.”

The big screen detonated into light.

Baek Si-on’s face filled dozens of square meters.

The venue exploded in a single second — the scream rolling up from the floor into the second tier, shredding what little quiet remained before the intro.

On screen, the filmed Baek Si-on looked into the lens. Said nothing. Only tilted his head a few degrees, in the screams, like a man hearing something at the door. Then he turned, and the camera followed him down a dim backstage corridor — to a door at its end. His hand settled on the handle.

In the same second, the live stage went fully black, and the intro of ‘Legend’ began.

On the first drumbeat, the man on screen pushed the door open. White light burst through the screen — and the same white light cracked open dead center on the real stage, simultaneously.

Baek Si-on walked out of the light.

Sixty thousand people detonated at once.

In the control room the director cut to the wide: sixty thousand light sticks snapping on together, a cold-white sea turning over in the bowl of Jamsil Stadium.

Baek Si-on raised the microphone. Before he could sing a note, the crowd got there first — scattered at first, then joined, then total.

sixty thousand voices · one word, three escalations

“BAEK SI-ON!”

“BAEK SI-ON!!”

“BAEK SI-ON!!!”

The big screen cut from the filmed door to the live close-up. On the floor, the chant kept going, one name, sixty thousand mouths, no oxygen reserved.

For this one moment, all of Jamsil held exactly one sound.

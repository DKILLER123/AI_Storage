Chapter One Hundred Three

A Political Line You Don’t Have to Stand On

September 29 10:00 am PDT Los Angeles · KIIS-FM, then Spotify, then CBS Scooter’s speed, under forty-eight hours from a signature, filling a day until there was no white left on it

Scooter’s execution was a kind of terror. The ink was not yet two days old and the calendar had already been stuffed.

Ten in the morning was KIIS-FM, a glass booth that smelled of coffee and the kind of carpet radio stations never replace. The host said Venice as if Venice were a brand of gum, then played forty seconds of Legend and asked whether a Best Actor was allowed to want a chorus that loud. Si-on said the chorus had not asked permission. The host laughed the way American radio laughs, which is a laugh with a clock on it, and they were out before the laugh had sat down.

One o’clock was Spotify. A room that wanted to be a studio and was, mostly, a logo. He sat on a stool with an acoustic that was not Jae-joon’s and sang Legend without the stadium. The song, unplugged, still refused to be furniture. The head of content listened with the face of a man who had been paid to be hard to impress, and then, before the last guitar had cooled, put the track into New Music Friday’s global editorial as if the playlist were a door he had decided to hold.

Three was the last block. CBS. The Late Late Show, taped early, because evenings in this city were already sold.

The host was James Corden. A British man with a British man’s volume.

James Corden

Host · The Late Late Show · CBS

Real-name counterpart · a Range Rover, four cameras, a hug that lands harder than it looks

He hears Legend in rehearsal and immediately wants a car. He sings harmony in a body that should not be able to, and then howls a chorus until the note breaks and does not stop. He tells a Best Actor to smile more. He means it as a note, not a kindness.

In rehearsal Corden listened to Legend once and offered an improvisation: put a Carpool Karaoke in the taped hour. Si-on in the passenger seat. Corden driving. Si-on looked at Scooter. Scooter shrugged, which in Scooter meant your call.

A black Range Rover rolled into the CBS lot.

TAPE · Carpool Karaoke · CBS lot

Four cameras. Corden at the wheel. Si-on in the passenger seat. First: Way Back Home, two voices, the British one thicker than it has a right to be, the blend accidentally kind. Then Legend. The chorus. Corden in full British variety-show, beating the wheel, howling until the note splits, not stopping when it splits.

The howl is not a joke at the song. It is a man who has decided the song can take a beating and still be a song.

Si-on, beside him, is infected enough to smile once. The smile is small. Corden files it.

Seven-forty. They called cut. Corden climbed out of the Rover and put a hug on Si-on that used more force than Kobe’s water and more frequency than Kobe’s blink.

“You should smile more.”

Two pats on the back. Lighter than a Mamba. Faster.

“When you smile, the audience loses its mind.”

“I’ll think about it.”

“Don’t think. Do.”

He waved at Scooter and was walked off to punch in a few on-camera reads. Si-on came out of the tape zone. At the corner of the corridor Baek Eun-ah was already coming at a half-run, an opened water in one hand, a tablet in the other, a black tote on the shoulder that had decided to be a filing cabinet.

“Oppa.”

He took the water. A swallow. The ice went down the throat and put out the heat of howling Legend in a car with a British man.

She kept pace.

“Busan called at noon. Official invite. Opening night, October second.”

The festival sat up in his head. Diving Bell. A sunken ferry. Protest. A committee and a city hall at each other’s throats. Cultural freedom. Political pressure. And later, a list.

“I looked at the grid.” She turned the tablet. “We’ve still got two of Scooter’s press hits and a Spotify follow-up shoot. If we change the ticket, earliest is tomorrow night, and then transfer, landing, look, carpet, all stacked.”

“In theory it isn’t impossible, but —”

“I’m not going.”

He did not hate politics. He knew, too clearly, what happened when a political story got its teeth into an entertainer: every work after that was made to wear a colour. He could have a position. He did not want to be someone else’s flag. Not now.

Eun-ah blinked. She had expected a weigh. Busan was the country’s most important film festival; The Green Fly had just taken the Lion of the Future and a Volpi; a carpet at home would have given the Korean press another week to cook. The no had not paused to cook.

“All right. I reply scheduling conflict?”

“Mm.”

She started the mail on the iPad. He walked two steps. Stopped. A person had stood up in his head.

Baek Jeong-hun. Samchon. Freshly pulled into the Korean film core by a Mapo table of Park Chan-wook, Bong Joon-ho, Song Kang-ho. This Busan, he would go. Not only go. If a joint denunciation were put in front of him, he would sign. The thing itself was not wrong. Cultural freedom should be defended. A government using admin to filter, press, punish creators for the wrong politics was wrong. Wrong was one column. Cost was another. The people at the front had international prizes, capital pipes, an industry gold body; if they were hit they could live on overseas and on their own names. The ones who got crushed were always the names that did not ring. Jeong-hun was not yet allowed to think of himself as a ship that would not sink.

Si-on took out the phone. Found the number. Called.

Baek Si-on → Baek Jeong-hun · CBS corridor, to a Showbox hallway

“Si-on-ah?”

Noise. A corridor. Staff talk, paper, a distant lift chime.

“Samchon. You busy?”

“Not busy. Just out of a meeting at Showbox. What’s wrong?”

“Has The Green Fly got a domestic date?”

“Just now. Showbox knocked November first. Not a bad week. No giants that week, nothing heavy in the two around it. We won’t get commercial volume, but it’s better than I thought.”

“Mm.”

November first. Between the October music line and a November call time on Sado. Not bad.

“Has anyone asked you to Busan?”

“They have. I already said yes.”

He exhaled. Of course.

“Samchon.”

“Mm.”

“Do you know what walking into this year’s Busan means?”

“I know.”

“And you still said yes.”

“Si-on-ah… some things, someone has to stand up.”

“Yes. Some things, someone does. But samchon — you don’t have Park Chan-wook’s international capital. You don’t have their box office. You don’t have the kind of skin the world’s festivals have stamped so many times it counts as armour.”

“You walk out with them, the applause is the same. The bill is not.”

The line went quiet. A few seconds. When Jeong-hun’s voice came back it had dropped.

“Since when do you talk like a distributor. Open your mouth, capital. Close it, box-office record. What, you take a Venice cup, sign a few ads, go to America, and now you teach me how to be a filmmaker?”

Eun-ah, beside him, could not hear the words. She could see his face cooling.

“You know what The Green Fly is. It’s these rotten things. Now it’s time to say it in the real, and you want me not to go?”

At the end of the corridor someone called Si-on’s name. Eun-ah put a hand up: wait. Si-on listened to the anger on the other end. He let a silence sit, then:

Baek Si-on → Baek Jeong-hun · continued

“Samchon. You think if you stand up and talk, VIP wakes up?”

“If she were going to wake up, she wouldn’t have pushed Vice Chair Lee Mi-kyung to America, and she wouldn’t have Bong Joon-ho abroad reading foreigners’ faces every day.”

“You stand with Park Chan-wook. What’s the first result. Showbox can’t hold VIP’s pressure and drops The Green Fly. Your film doesn’t get screens. Box office zero. Your next one doesn’t get a Film Council production grant.”

“Unless the person VIP puts in is the left, you don’t see daylight in this industry again.”

“Then your only road is Bong Joon-ho’s road, exile. Bong-suk lives overseas because he has results walking in front of him.”

“What do you have?”

A longer quiet.

“Si-on. Do you know something?”

He knew. He knew the list. He knew thousands of names in the cultural field going into a box nobody would admit was a box. He knew whose film funds would vanish, whose projects would stall, whose careers would be held by the throat by a paper that had never been published. He did not say the list.

“Samchon. Reality isn’t a betrayal of an ideal.”

“Reality is making sure you still get to shoot the next film.”

“Having VIP congratulate your film against her own conscience — isn’t that a higher class of thing than a name on a joint statement?”

The other end went fully quiet. Si-on did not push. For a man like Jeong-hun, admitting a step back was harder than a long take in mud. Especially after the country’s top table had just let him sit down.

A long time. Then a lighter, on the line.

“…I’ll think.”

“Mm.”

That, he knew, was the largest concession he was going to get.

“When are you back?”

“A few more days.”

“Come eat.”

“All right.”

“Don’t bring those American sweet things. Your imo doesn’t like them.”

“I know.”

The line died. He took the phone down. The glass went black. Eun-ah watched him the way you watch a corridor that has just had a fight in it.

“Oppa.”

“Mm.”

“Will Appa listen?”

He looked at his own face in the dark screen.

“He’ll think.”

“And after he thinks?”

“I don’t know.”

People were not machines. You did not input logic and get a correct choice. Especially not a left-leaning filmmaker like Jeong-hun. He understood stakes. He was also, at certain hours, able to decide that stakes were not the point.

Eun-ah looked at the Busan reply still unsent on the iPad. She hit send. Thought. Added a line at the bottom of the memo.

Eun-ah · memo, last lines

Remind: back in Korea, watch Appa.

If needed — let Eomma do it.

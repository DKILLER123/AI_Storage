Chapter Two Hundred Twenty

The Lipstick Revolution Yields, Temporarily, to Gunfire

Early June · spring open-hiring

Midday — scrolling job posts on a dorm bed

Ewha Womans University · the dormitories

Every May and June, Korea’s spring open-hiring season began.

Ewha Womans University was a name that sounded glossy, but a major like consumer sciences could not be eaten for dinner.

In big-company hiring, women were already quietly discounted as a class.

And if the major wasn’t hard enough on top of that —

The destinations narrowed to ad agencies, PR firms, planning desks at small and midsize companies, or contract work at public institutions.

Which of those buckets Choi Na-young belonged to, she couldn’t yet say.

At the family dinner the weekend before, her mother had asked — in the tone that translates as I’m not pressuring you, though I am absolutely pressuring you:

“Na-young-ah. Shouldn’t you widen the range a little?”

She had said “I know.”

And understood perfectly what “widen the range” meant.

The phone rang while she was lying on her dorm bed scrolling job postings.

The caller ID popped up: Professor Yoon Hye-ja.

She froze.

Yoon Hye-ja was the professor of one of her electives.

Professors did not call students for nothing.

Unless grades were in trouble, or some academic project needed free labor.

Choi Na-young sat up, cleared her throat, answered.

Yoon Hye-ja ↔ Choi Na-young · voice · two o’clock · bring a résumé

“Professor-nim, hello!”

“Na-young-ah. Do you have a confirmed interview this afternoon?”

“…No.”

“Then come by at two. Small conference room, third floor, Social Sciences Building.”

“Yes — may I ask what—”

“Bring a résumé.”

Click.

Choi Na-young

Employee No. 1 · Ewha Womans University, consumer sciences

White-shirt interview armor · a salary advanced on the spot · seniority: four whole days

Came expecting a hidden-camera show, left as the brand’s first formal hire. Jumped exactly once in the corridor, kept the jump out of the minutes, and now presides over the everything-department — population: her.

Choi Na-young sat on the bed holding the phone while her brain accelerated.

Was the professor recommending her for some research-assistant post?

Or did some academic conference need an errand-runner?

Three minutes of thinking produced no answer.

She elected to stop thinking.

She hopped off the bed and pulled the wardrobe open.

A spring-hiring senior kept at least two interview outfits on permanent standby.

Choi Na-young picked the more formal of the two: white shirt, deep-blue slacks, black heels.

One forty-five.

She was out the door.

❧ ❧ ❧

Social Sciences Building, third floor.

The small conference room’s door stood half open.

Choi Na-young inhaled, knocked three times.

“Come in.”

She pushed the door and stepped through.

Three people sat inside.

Farthest left: Professor Yoon Hye-ja.

Choi Na-young’s gaze panned right — and stopped on the middle man’s face.

Long legs, crossed.

Body tipped slightly back into the chair.

Turning over something in his hands.

Choi Na-young’s first thought was unbearably lowly: I should have worn lipstick.

Her second thought instantly strangled the first: Are you insane, Choi Na-young — this is a job interview, not a fansign. Fix your face.

It took her roughly three seconds to tune every facial muscle into a state of no non-professional thoughts whatsoever have been generated by your face.

“Hello — please, sit.” Baek Eun-ah spoke first.

Choi Na-young nodded, handed her résumé across, and took the chair opposite.

Baek Si-on leafed through the résumé.

English: solid. A few social-practice stints. A few research projects.

Overall: a résumé that wasn’t bad, and also wouldn’t hold any big-company HR officer’s eyes for three extra seconds.

“How tall are you?”

“One… one meter sixty-five.”

Choi Na-young assumed this was an image assessment, hesitated half a second, stood, and turned a full circle on the spot to demonstrate her bearing.

Baek Eun-ah studied her face for a few seconds from the side.

Then, abruptly:

“Weren’t you a volunteer at the free concert?”

Baek Eun-ah could not possibly have memorized every volunteer’s face, but a few faces had genuinely stayed with her.

Choi Na-young went still.

“I was.”

“East-side entrance, crowd guidance?”

Now Choi Na-young was thoroughly stunned.

“You remember that?”

Baek Si-on glanced at Baek Eun-ah, the look asking: sure?

Baek Eun-ah nodded.

“Any requirements?”

Baek Si-on’s gaze came back to Choi Na-young.

“…Sorry?”

Choi Na-young hadn’t caught up.

“I said — do you have any requirements for the position.”

Choi Na-young shook her head on reflex.

“No.”

“Nothing wanted, nothing needed? Then what am I supposed to motivate you with? Affection?”

Choi Na-young’s brain, at a wildly inappropriate moment, flashed the reply that would not be nothing.

She looked down and pretended to think it over seriously.

In reality she was moderating the expansion rate of the blood vessels in her face.

Three seconds, give or take.

She raised her head.

“Well… I’d want the company to pay the four national insurances, properly. And then — if I’m converted to full-time… I’d hope for 2.5 million won a month.”

Even she could hear how sheepish it sounded.

2.5 million was, honestly, not a low ask.

“Accepted.”

Baek Si-on nodded:

“We’ll skip the probation. Four million won a month, pre-tax.”

“…I’m sorry — what?”

Four million a month put her level with the chaebol-tier entry packages — Samsung, LG, Hyundai, SK — and above some of them.

“Also, draw one month in advance and buy yourself a few work outfits.”

“……”

Choi Na-young’s precise state at that moment: soul out of body, flesh holding the seat.

❝

Ten a.m.: flat on a dorm bed, scrolling hiring notices, worrying she’d end up at some small company that didn’t change its water-cooler filter for a quarter.

Two p.m.: first formal employee of Baek Si-on’s new brand. One month’s salary, advanced.

— the fastest career arc of the spring-hiring season

What Korean company pays on the spot, straight out of the interview?

She had a powerful, unmistakable sensation of being taped for a hidden-camera variety show.

❧ ❧ ❧

Baek Eun-ah walked her out of the conference room.

The corridor was quiet.

Sunlight came through the windows and lay across the floor tiles.

A few steps in, Choi Na-young stopped and turned to Baek Eun-ah.

“This isn’t a hidden-camera show, right?”

“It’s not,” Baek Eun-ah said.

“Then… at work, going forward — what should I call him?”

She expected “Director Baek” or “Mr. Baek,” something in that family.

What she heard was:

“Boss. Husband. Either works.”

Choi Na-young’s brain froze solid for two seconds.

“…Husband?”

“Isn’t that what his female fans call him?”

Choi Na-young’s face went red all at once.

“Th-that’s only when everyone’s chanting it together…”

“And privately it’s embarrassing?”

“Of COURSE it’s embarrassing!”

Baek Eun-ah nodded and said:

“Then ‘boss.’”

With that she turned and went back into the conference room.

The summer breeze came in through the window. Choi Na-young looked at the direction Baek Eun-ah had vanished in.

Then she performed an action highly unbecoming of an Ewha student —

She jumped, once, on the spot.

Small amplitude. Entirely sufficient to express a graduate receiving an offer in spring-hiring season.

❧ ❧ ❧

Inside the dorm.

When Choi Na-young pushed the door open, her three roommates were each absorbed in their own business.

She set her bag on the desk and cleared her throat.

All heads turned at once.

“Did you win the lottery?”

“Yes.”

Choi Na-young drew herself up straight:

“I am now a direct subordinate of Baek Si-on.”

“……”

The dorm detonated.

“Which Baek Si-on?”

“Is there a second Baek Si-on in this country?”

“As his assistant?”

“Are you going to see him in person now?”

“Can you get me an autograph?”

“……”

The world was, occasionally, genuinely absurd.

But when the absurdity landed on your own head, Choi Na-young decided the reasonable thing was to defer the philosophical questions.

Spend the money first; audit reality later.

❧ ❧ ❧

Over the following days, the Seongsu-dong office began to change.

The source of the change was Professor Yoon Hye-ja.

She had evidently classified her son’s founding of a company as a special form of university-employment support program.

Conveniently, students of consumer sciences, advertising, and cultural industries could all be put to use in a brand’s infancy.

And Baek Si-on’s interview process had settled into a template so efficient it bordered on negligent.

The hiring template, as perfected in week one

Read the résumé.

One glance: articulation and posture.

Two questions.

Baek Eun-ah sets the pay.

Sign the temp contract.

Advance the wardrobe-and-commute subsidy.

Total elapsed: under twenty minutes, end to end.

Inside one week.

The Seongsu-dong office went from Choi Na-young suspects she has been hired into a shell company to something one could, charitably, call a “team.”

Several new tables pushed together.

On them: sample tubes from the contract manufacturer, folders plastered with shade stickers, office supplies still in their shipping wrap.

Keyboard sounds had started up.

Phone sounds had started up.

So had the sound of young people arguing about a brand name.

Choi Na-young, as the company’s longest-tenured employee, possessed seniority so absurd it bordered on the criminal.

She had joined four days before everyone else.

But those four days of first-mover advantage were already enough to build, in front of the new hires, an aura of I am your senior.

Somebody’s first day: Choi Na-young went to meet her personally.

“Welcome. I’ll walk you through the company first.”

The newcomer nodded, very earnest.

“What’s the org structure?”

Choi Na-young scanned the office.

Baek Si-on: absent. Baek Eun-ah: slumped over a laptop, frowning at it. Everyone else: unpacking boxes.

“Boss Baek, Manager Baek, and us.”

The newcomer tried again.

“And the department?”

Choi Na-young considered.

“There’s currently one department.”

“Which department?”

“The does-everything department.”

Two seconds of silence from the newcomer.

“…Then which team am I on?”

“You’re the team.”

A thread of bewilderment appeared in the newcomer’s eyes — precisely identical, Choi Na-young noted, to her own on day one.

Choi Na-young patted her shoulder.

Very seasoned. Very senior.

“You get used to it.”

❧ ❧ ❧

Days later

Mid-afternoon

Seongsu-dong · the office, twelve tubes on the table

That afternoon, Baek Si-on was at the office.

Spread across the table: the first lip-gloss trial batch from Cosmax.

Twelve small tubes, lined up from nude pink to berry red.

Each tube carried a formula number and a shade label.

Choi Na-young and another recent hire were logging swatch results.

Baek Eun-ah sat beside them with a draft of the OEM contract, the page gashed red with a pen.

Baek Si-on uncapped the tube marked “BSR-07” and drew one stroke onto a swatch card.

Warm-leaning color, water-shine acceptable, set speed still short.

He was about to write the remark on the log sheet.

The phone rang.

Caller ID: Baek Jeong-hoon.

Baek Si-on picked up.

Baek Jeong-hoon ↔ Baek Si-on · voice · eight words, whole new priority

“What are you working on?”

“Lip-gloss samples.”

“Quit screwing around with that lipstick nonsense of yours.”

“It’s called a color-cosmetics brand.”

“Same thing.”

Baek Jeong-hoon possessed zero self-awareness regarding his disrespect for the consumer-goods industry.

“Anyway — come over.”

Baek Si-on still had the swatch card in his hand.

“What for?”

Baek Jeong-hoon said one line.

“The script is done.”

Baek Si-on’s hand stopped.

The warm-pink stroke on the swatch card wasn’t dry yet.

But this sentence’s priority was, in truth, higher than any tube of lip gloss in the room.

He turned to Baek Eun-ah:

“You all carry on with the swatches this afternoon. Fill the log — send it to me tonight.”

Baek Eun-ah didn’t raise her head.

“Got it.”

Choi Na-young asked on reflex:

“And you, boss — where are you going?”

Baek Si-on was already standing.

“To kill someone.”

The office went abruptly silent.

The other new hire’s coffee cup hung in midair.

Baek Si-on swept them with a look.

“In a movie.”

Three exhales came out in unison.

Clearly, none of them had yet adapted to a boss who switched between lip-gloss shade numbers and homicide without touching the gear.

❧ ❧ ❧

Same afternoon

Ten-odd minutes by car

Baek Jeong-hoon’s new house · the murder wall

Baek Jeong-hoon’s new house wasn’t far from Baek Si-on’s studio — ten-odd minutes behind a wheel.

The house was not small.

The furnishing, on the other hand, was austere.

Gray floor tile, white walls, a long wooden table — and, beyond a single “Lion of the Future” trophy, nothing that counted as ornament.

The guest room, as dressed

Two twenty-seven-inch monitors, side by side on a long table against the wall.

One enormous relationship board filling the wall above: red thread, blue thread, black thread running crisscross through a dozen-plus photos and index cards.

Around it, taped thick: printed script pages, clippings on Korean political-business entanglement, photocopied papers on cult organizations, and several interior floor plans of a church, sourced who-knew-where.

Less a director’s workroom than a serial killer’s bedroom, as television would dress it.

“If I’m remembering right — this used to be Eun-ah’s room?”

Baek Jeong-hoon turned at the voice.

“Right.”

“Where does she stay when she comes home?”

“Doesn’t she stay at your hotel?”

Baek Si-on went quiet for a moment.

He suddenly suspected that Baek Eun-ah’s old line — coming back to this home, she was surplus — might not have been an emotional flourish at all.

It was an objective statement of fact.

Her bed had, physically, ceased to exist.

“Here.”

Baek Jeong-hoon handed across the bound latest draft.

Baek Si-on took the script and sat down.

Page one: character table and synopsis. He didn’t linger; straight to the body.

The first pages matched the version he’d seen before.

But the kill chain and the logic line had been rebuilt.

The politician had been first in the old version; the nightclub owner was first now — and the trail led upward from there, to the man behind him: a politician.

The press called him the “national father who climbed up from nothing,” guardian of the clubs’ gray businesses — and, behind the paint, a man who ran drugs, hidden cameras, and a hostess network that held a row of political and business figures by the collar.

Both evils down, the male lead tasted enormous satisfaction, left his name at the scene, and fled to a church in the south of the country.

The church was run by a middle-aged woman the faithful called the Holy Mother.

Her public face was compassion itself: she healed the sick, sheltered the homeless, prayed over the families of the dead, claimed she could bear sin on a sinner’s behalf — and commanded a vast flock across television and the internet.

The male lead meant only to spend his remaining time there quietly.

Instead, he stumbled through her true face.

On the day of the church’s grand atonement ceremony, the male lead walked in from outside, covered in blood.

Hymns and gunfire, one sound.

White vestments going red.

Religious murals shot to pieces.

The Holy Mother kept smiling.

Right up until he stood in front of her.

She told him: every killing of yours was, itself, one move on someone else’s board.

He pulled the trigger.

When it was done, he chose to surrender himself to the police.

The dead politician and the dead Holy Mother both ran deep into the Blue House’s interest web.

Out of spite.

The sitting president broke Korea’s long-standing, unspoken moratorium on executions, and signed — rarely, swiftly — the order that put the male lead to death.

At the end, from the prison doctor, he learned one thing: the original terminal diagnosis might itself have been a lie.

After his death.

The three dead villains’ files were leaked, systematically.

Public opinion detonated.

An opposition leader seized the moment: a National Assembly inquiry, an impeachment drive, the press and a chaebol defecting to his side — ascending to power on the slogan clean out the rot, rebuild the country.

The former president was investigated to the bone, and jailed.

The broadcast, as aired

The new president takes the oath. Citizens wave the Taegukgi; the young fill the streets celebrating; the anchor announces that “a new era has formally begun.”

Everything, on its surface, flourishing.

Baek Si-on turned to the last page.

The new president stands at the floor-to-ceiling window of the Blue House office.

The door opens.

A man walks in.

The broker — the man who, from the film’s earliest act, had kept feeding the male lead his leads, his guns, his altered medical report.

He takes his place behind the new president and bows, low and correct.

The new president turns around.

Across the mild, upright, scholarly face, a smile surfaces — slow, and impossible to read to the bottom.

The script ends.

❧ ❧ ❧

Baek Si-on finished, and let several seconds pass before he set the script back on the table.

The concept he had handed Baek Jeong-hoon was a genre picture — greed, anger, delusion; villains scraping at redemption on their way out; one indirect swipe at a certain someone.

The result…

“Uncle.”

“What?”

Baek Jeong-hoon leaned back in the opposite chair, head tilted slightly, eyes aimed at the window — pointedly not at him.

“This film can make you immortal.”

“Don’t exaggerate.”

The mouth was humble.

But when he lifted his water glass, the corner of the mouth visible past the rim was clearly, measurably, on its way up.

He had received the line extremely well, evidently.

“Is the cast set?”

“Mm.”

“All of it?”

Baek Jeong-hoon pulled over a handwritten cast sheet and read aloud:

“The nightclub owner — Yoo Ah-in.”

Fair. The role rhymed with Cho Tae-oh, his bully in Veteran.

“The politician — Park Sung-woong.”

Also fair: the man who played the imperious Lee Joong-goo could do a smiling tiger in his sleep.

“The Holy Mother — Kim Hye-soo. And the president who rises at the end — Lee Sung-min, special appearance.”

Baek Si-on was quiet a moment.

He’d assumed that with material this dark, his uncle would run true to type: no superstition about star power, the whole budget poured into shooting and post.

Then the list came out, and every single name on it could carry a film’s box office alone.

“Uncle. You’re playing the all-star game now?”

Baek Jeong-hoon answered with a completely clear conscience.

“You’re a Venice Best Actor yourself. If I don’t use good actors, what do you want — someone along to drag you down?”

Baek Si-on genuinely had no counter.

“When do cameras roll?”

“Four days.”

“Four?”

“Mm.”

“And you’re only handing me the script NOW?”

“I’m handing it to you four days early. Isn’t that what I just did?”

Baek Si-on stared into the old face, hunting for the smallest trace of I’m joking.

Trace not found.

Baek Jeong-hoon patted the script on the table.

“Stop brooding over that lipstick side-business of yours. Get into character.”

“It’s a color-cosmetics brand,” Baek Si-on corrected, again.

“Fine. A color-cosmetics brand.”

Baek Si-on picked the script up.

Over in Seongsu-dong, Choi Na-young and the other new girls were probably still debating which reds made the first lip-gloss lineup.

Four days from now, his job would stop being deciding which red suited a woman’s lips best — and become deciding which red suited the white walls of a church.

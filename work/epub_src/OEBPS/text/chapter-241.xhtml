Chapter Two Hundred Forty-One

Faker’s Deadly Temptation

Late July · ‘Sado’ press week

One styling chair · one tablet full of English

Gangnam · the VIP room of a high-end salon

Gangnam. The VIP room of a high-end salon.

Baek Si-on sat in the styling chair. Park Ji-hoon was still in Paris for further training, so today’s styling fell to the salon’s owner. She made one full orbit of him, concluded that this particular head offered a stylist very little artistic room, and shifted her main energy to the state of his skin.

Baek Eun-ah sat on the sofa beside him, a tablet on her knees, one English email filling the whole screen.

“Cousin — Riot replied.”

“What do they say?”

Her eyes tracked the English line by line. A year ago, handling Scooter-related business had meant running translation software beside the laptop the entire time; after the North American land-grab and the gauntlet of global luxury endorsements, all that stumbling was a distant country now.

The Riot reply, as read aloud

“They’re very satisfied with the current melody, theme, and chorus,” Eun-ah read from the first paragraph. “They especially like the core concept of ‘legends never die’ — it maps onto past world champions, and fits players climbing back onto the stage after failure.”

Baek Si-on rested with his eyes closed while the artist’s brush crossed the bridge of his nose. “Read the turning point.” Eun-ah pursed her lips — eyes still shut, and he had already called it: a client’s praise is always stapled to revision notes.

Her fingers scrolled down two paragraphs and she read:

“But — considering S5 is held this year in Berlin, and Europe is electronic music’s heartland, they hope to hear ‘more diverse elements’ in the song, to match the finals’ lighting and stage design.”

The brush migrated from nose bridge to brow bone.

“Specifics?”

“Official release version, player-entrance version, instrumental version, electronic version...”

She counted on her fingers and couldn’t help the commentary:

“Thousands of words of email. The core demand adds up to one sentence: the money’s paid — please do the whole menu.”

The artist’s hand paused at Baek Si-on’s cheek, uncertain whether laughing was allowed.

Baek Si-on wasn’t surprised. Three million dollars sat far above the ordinary event-song rate, and trading it for more event-adapted versions wasn’t entirely unreasonable. Besides, the electronic element they wanted collided head-on with something he had been turning over for days.

He opened his eyes, looked at himself in the mirror — and his thoughts drifted back to the North American chart war that had just ended. OMI and ‘Cheerleader’ had held the Billboard crown only a short while before ‘What Do You Mean?’ kicked the door down. Before that, OMI had been a nobody, and Felix Jaehn, the German DJ who had remixed the track, an even bigger nobody — two unknowns combined, overturning a world-conquering song that had ruled for twelve straight weeks. Objective factors abounded. But factors explain why it fell; they don’t explain how a remixed old track by a young DJ pulled off the coup at all.

The answer sat inside a structural change running through the music industry:

The era, as forecast

The formula: DJ producer + pop vocal = chart-devouring hit. Within two or three years it would sweep every major chart on the planet like a virus.

The Chainsmokers.

Kygo.

Zedd.

DJ Snake.

Names that lived behind the decks and inside project files, about to strut to the front of the stage and enjoy treatment equal to — or beyond — top-tier singers. Only later would a strange-talented girl called Billie Eilish arrive out of nowhere and end the loud electronic age with minimalist bedroom whispers, kicking the whole lot of them back to the clubs. But this was 2015. The wave had just lifted its first crest. Better than grinding the arrangement alone: ride the era, and be the first to eat the crab at the mouth of the wind.

If there was going to be an electronic Drop, then his DJ — ah, no: the DJ belonging to ‘Legends Never Die’ — should enter the stage too.

“Eun-ah.”

“Mm?”

“Contact a young Norwegian DJ on the international web.”

She picked the tablet off her knees, finger hovering over the input field.

“Name?”

“DJ Walkzz.”

“Walk... what?”

He spelled it out.

“W, A, L, K, Z, Z. Signature track: ‘Fade’.”

She searched SoundCloud. The page loaded. She rose, carried the screen over beside the mirror, and pointed at a conspicuously bare account page:

“This one?”

The SoundCloud page, as inspected

DJ Walkzz — a homepage so spare it was nearly anonymous. A handful of pure-electronic uploads. ‘Fade’ among them.

Baek Si-on swept it once, the plainest profile in the room, and nodded. “That’s him.”

She drew the tablet back.

“What’s the pitch?”

“Verify his identity first. Then send the NDA and part of the ‘Legends Never Die’ vocal stems.”

“How do we word the terms?”

“Tell him I have a world-championship theme that needs an electronic version. If his audition meets the bar, official credit, production fee, and backend splits get negotiated separately.”

Eun-ah asked:

“Should we copy Riot directly? A world-stage production invite with no letterhead reads like a scam email...”

“Yes. And: three days to deliver a demo with an electronic Drop.”

Her finger hovered over the screen.

“Three days? Isn’t that too tight?”

Baek Si-on rose from the styling chair.

“People with real ideas don’t need three days. The three days are for deciding.”

She finished editing the email.

“Send it?”

“Send.”

Baek Si-on stepped in front of the full-length mirror. Suit, fitted. Tie, knotted. Shoes, polished clean. The man in the glass had switched, mid-morning, from the set’s fugitive to the promotional face of Korean cinema.

❧ ❧ ❧

The same days · North Atlantic drizzle

Two screens · one inbox · twelve hours of deadline beaten

Bergen, Norway · Alan Walker’s room

Bergen, Norway. Outside the window fell the kind of thin rain that Nordic summer can’t be bothered to stop. A gray-white sky pressed down on the harbor; the ridgeline beyond showed only a blurred outline.

Alan Walker’s room wasn’t large. One bed, one chair, one dual-monitor computer. The left screen glowed with an FL Studio project; the right displayed a European-server ranked result screen for ‘League of Legends’. The match record wasn’t pretty. He leaned back in the chair, mouse still in hand, studying the KDA and debating whether that team fight had really deserved his Teleport.

That was when the mail chime rang. Mail at this hour was usually a SoundCloud system notice or some small label’s copy-paste collaboration template — the paste marks more obvious than his ranked stats. The mouse wandered over the LoL client twice, and then habit won: he switched to the inbox.

The subject line arrived first.

The inbox, as it detonated

Subject: Riot Games — S5 World Championship Theme · Electronic Reconstruction Test Invitation

Opening signature, brutally short: Project Executive Producer — Baek Si-on.

Alan’s hand froze on the mouse. To ordinary Europeans, that name might be the new Venice Best Actor, the Brit Awards’ Best International Male, an international superstar with three Billboard chart-toppers. But inside the cognition of a hardcore LoL player, Baek Si-on held exactly one title: the true god of esports.

Replay · S4 Worlds opening · Seoul, Sangam Stadium — as still worshipped on the forums

The man on the stage, one song — ‘Legend’ — and BTS’s war-dance beside him, and sixty thousand in the stands detonated to a boil. The clip circulates in every gaming forum to this day.

And now that god’s team had crossed half the planet to find a nobody who had only ever posted a few pure-electronic tracks online — to build the electronic climax of the S5 theme.

Alan pinched his own face. The pain was real. Not a hallucination born of a ranked losing streak. He read the email again from the top: the project wanted him to build a Drop-direction demo over a set of encrypted stems. The requirements were unambiguous.

The test brief, as specified

Do not alter the main melody.

No leaking the project or the collaboration.

Delivery: within three days.

If officially adopted: co-producer credit, production fee, and royalties — negotiated separately.

He finished reading and typed into the reply box. Deleted it. Typed again. Deleted it again. In the end, one line remained.

The reply, as sent

I’m in. I’ll take the test.

Send.

❧ ❧ ❧

The other side’s efficiency was as if daytime in some other timezone simply refused to go off duty. A few minutes later, an e-signature NDA arrived. Alan read every clause. Most of it he could parse; the paragraphs on governing law and dispute resolution sent him to fetch his father. His father knew Baek Si-on too — the Billboard champion, the Venice Best Actor, the Time Asia cover. Precisely because the counterparty was that famous, he was all the more careful. Father and son sat side by side at the computer and turned from the first page to the last; then his father completed a second confirmation by email with the Riot project contact, and the esports department wrote back verifying the invitation was genuine. No trap. Only then did the guardian confirmation and the electronic signature get done.

After the signed agreement went back, they asked for his bank account and international wire details. He filled them in and sent them off — and before long, the bank’s cross-border credit notice popped up. The amount field read: ten thousand U.S. dollars.

Alan blinked at the digits. Ten thousand dollars would not change his life — ‘Fade’’s YouTube plays already paid him a steady passive income. What made his heart slam was the attitude travelling behind the money. With Baek Si-on’s fame and resources, countless young producers worldwide would pay to participate. No fee, fine. No credit, fine. One resume line — participated in Baek Si-on’s world-championship theme — would already be a heavyweight credential for entering the industry. Baek Si-on had declined to exploit any of that gap. An audition is labor. Even if it isn’t used, it gets paid.

❝

The ten thousand dollars wasn’t payment. It was respect — wired in advance, to a nobody, on the principle that trying is working.

— the deposit, as Alan understood it

His blood ran hotter than it did after a Flash into a wall. Once payment cleared, the project sent the encrypted files. Alan keyed the password, extracted, imported, headphones on — and Baek Si-on’s voice rose out of the unreleased stems. He sat up without deciding to. The song already carried a fully articulated epicness: strings advancing from far off, drums thickening stepwise, the melody surging at the chorus — and the core line, “Legends never die”, landing at the peak as strings and voice detonated together. Alan looped it three times, opened FL Studio, built a new project, and dragged the stems in one by one.

Work.

❧ ❧ ❧

The kitchen door pushed open. His mother came down the hallway with a plate of sandwiches and called once. No answer. She pushed the door and found her son in headphones, eyes locked to the screen, fingers flying between keyboard and mouse. The sandwiches sat at the desk’s edge — and three hours later still lay untouched on the plate. The LoL client on the right screen never opened again. For a boy who treated ranked queue as a daily obligation, that was possibly the bigger sacrifice — bigger than skipping a promotion series.

Baek Si-on had given him three days. Alan used twelve hours. He exported the first test demo, checked the audio format and sample rate twice, and then revised the email body many, many times. Draft one was a long outpouring — how excited he was, how much he had worshipped the S4 opening performance, how grateful for the chance. Re-read, it resembled a fan letter. Deleted entire. Draft two still ran too hot. Deleted. What survived was a few lines.

The submission, as sent

Hello — attached is the first electronic Drop test, built from the stems provided.

If there are any notes, I can adjust immediately.

Attachment upload complete. He pressed send. The status flipped to “Sent”. Then came the hardest stretch of the entire process: the waiting.

❧ ❧ ❧

The first meal after sending, Alan tasted nothing — though possibly Norwegian food tastes of nothing on its own. Back in his room: refresh. No reply. He bit his nails and hammered F5. The inbox held its shape. He stood, poured water, came back, refreshed. Nothing. Washed his face in the bathroom, came back, refreshed. Still nothing. Anxiety climbed up from his stomach. Maybe Baek Si-on had just heard ‘Fade’ in passing and thought the melody mildly interesting — and now that the demo had actually arrived, the gap between SoundCloud play counts and professional production chops had announced itself at once. He reopened his own project and the problems multiplied the longer he listened.

Was the low end too heavy?

Had the Drop entered too early?

Would the vocal-chop rhythm read as green?

His father came in from the living room and found him knotted with worry.

“South Korea and here have a time difference.”

“I know.”

“A project like that isn’t heard by one person, either.”

“I know.”

“Then why do you keep refreshing?”

Alan took his hands off the keyboard.

“Confirming the network is fine.”

His father nodded and did not expose so fragile a technical excuse.

❧ ❧ ❧

Day two. The new-mail chime finally rang, and Alan came off the bed like a spring. He opened the inbox, fingers trembling faintly from just having woken. It opened with praise.

The reply, as received

Mr. Baek Si-on and the production team have completed the listen; the team is very satisfied with the melodic direction and the sense of space in the electronic Drop.

On the Drop’s low-end treatment and the fine detail of the vocal chops, email is an inefficient medium.

To guarantee the quality of the S5 theme, we cordially invite you to Seoul, South Korea, to advance the remaining work face-to-face with the production team.

Your first-class flights, five-star hotel, and all lodging and meals in Korea will be borne in full by our side.

Alan read “to Seoul, South Korea” over and over. The excitement was promptly replaced by a different emotion: nerves.

He had been born in England and moved to Norway as a small child — but moving with your parents at six, and flying alone to East Asia at seventeen as a music producer, were different species of event. He spoke no Korean. He had never taken a long-haul international flight alone. He had barely left Bergen. And to give up a music collaboration with his favorite game over that — ten thousand times, no.

Deeply conflicted, he walked out to the living room. His sister Camilla sat on the sofa with her phone; his parents were making lunch in the kitchen. He told the whole story — the invitation email, the first-class and five-star arrangements, and his fear of going to Korea alone. Camilla put the phone down and examined her brother the way one examines an alien:

“Alan, is your brain broken? That’s Baek Si-on!”

“But I don’t know them.”

“That’s why humanity invented meeting.”

“I’ve never been to Korea.”

“Planes know the way.”

“I don’t speak Korean.”

“Baek Si-on speaks English.”

“But what if—”

“What if what? What if — they fly you first-class to Seoul and then sell you?”

Alan: “......”

Every argument was stopped at the source. His parents leaned out of the kitchen too. “Son, this is the most important step of your music career,” his mother said, gentle. “You should go out and see the world.” His father added: “At least the part of it outside your bedroom.” That one cut harder than Camilla’s.

Mid-family-summit, the computer chimed from the bedroom. Alan ran back. New mail. The sender, once more, Baek Eun-ah.

The second email, as received

We noticed your profile mentions that you are a ‘League of Legends’ player.

Should you accept, the project team can — without touching the work schedule — arrange a visit to a Korean professional team for you.

If the timing works, you may name one to three Korean pro players you’d like to meet, and we will do our best to arrange it.

No guarantees on everyone’s calendars — but the team of the player Faker will be our first contact.

Alan’s eyes stopped dead on the name “Faker”.

Social anxiety?

What social anxiety?

To hell with social anxiety!

Camilla followed him in and peered at the screen.

“Who’s Faker?”

Alan turned around. With exactly the gaze she had just spent on him, he now examined his sister.

“You don’t know who Faker is?”

“Why would I need to?”

Alan opened the browser at once. The first photo the search returned: Lee Sang-hyeok in an SKT jersey, hoisting the S3 championship trophy.

And the lecture began. S3 world champion. The best mid-laner alive. The shadow-duel of two Zeds. The un-petrifiable Lee Sang-hyeok. He spoke faster and faster, pulling up match VODs, attempting to explain to the entire family who this man was. Camilla listened in silence to the end and asked exactly one thing:

“So — have you decided to go?”

Alan stopped. The first email still hung in his inbox. The second had landed right behind it.

“I’m going.”

❧ ❧ ❧

He redrafted the reply several times. What finally went out:

The acceptance, as sent

Thank you for the invitation.

I will come to Korea and join the remaining production of ‘Legends Never Die’.

Please send me the itinerary.

If at all possible, I would like to meet the player Faker.

He leaned back in the chair and let out a long breath. The nerves hadn’t left. Having decided, they had only grown more specific.

Specific enough to start thinking: what should he say first to Baek Si-on? How big was the gap, really, between a Korean studio and his bedroom? And the most important question of all —

When he met Faker, what should they talk about?

He thought for a long time. Then he opened the ‘League of Legends’ client. The profile page came up, and his current rank appeared in the center of the screen. Alan studied his record in silence — and quietly closed the client.

❝

Some things, the idol is simply better off not knowing.

— the rank, sealed away until further notice

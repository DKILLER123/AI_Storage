Chapter One Hundred Fifty One

The Opening of a Shoujo Manga

November 26 · 8:47 a.m. Woken by her own phone · forty-seven minutes of stolen grace Seoul · Gangnam · Baek Eun-ah’s apartment She had budgeted for exactly the amount of sleep she got minus forty-seven minutes. Fate, this morning, was feeling generous. It would not last past breakfast.

Baek Eun-ah was woken by her own phone vibrating.

She extended one arm out of the blankets, patted the nightstand — once, twice, a third time — and only on the third sweep actually found the phone.

8:47 a.m.

Good. Forty-seven more minutes than she had predicted for herself.

The newest message in her pinned chat was from her cousin. The timestamp read 1:59 a.m.

KakaoTalk · pinned · Baek Eun-ah → Baek Si-on · 1:59 a.m.

SI-ON: Contact SM. Ask about Red Velvet’s availability. Concert guests.

Sent fifty-two minutes after a certain 1:07 a.m. phone call had ended, and composed with the exact cadence of a man delegating a chore he had already, personally, finished the hard part of.

Baek Eun-ah narrowed her eyes at the line for five full seconds.

Then she silently pushed the phone under the pillow.

She rolled over. Closed her eyes. Opened them.

No. It refused to work.

She excavated the phone from under the pillow and read the message a second time, to make sure she hadn’t hallucinated it.

Fate, as a substance, is remarkably impolite.

Six months ago she had been the optimized-away margin case of SM’s trainee system — the line item you strike through. Now she was going to contact SM as Baek Si-on’s manager, to invite the girl group she had come within one evaluation of debuting alongside.

She had expected a twinge. At minimum a small, dignified ache behind the sternum. Failing that, one ceremonial sigh of ah, life.

Instead, when she actually opened the laptop and started drafting the email, she discovered it didn’t hurt at all.

There was an amount of absurdity she couldn’t quite name. And underneath it — she hated to admit it — a small, unauthorized triumph.

…

Seoul. Gangnam. SM Entertainment headquarters.

Kim Tae-su, an ordinary employee of the PR team, had just peeled the wrapper off a convenience-store triangle gimbap when a new mail notification slid out of the corner of his screen.

Sender: S.W Studio.

His hand stopped. The tuna inside the gimbap had not yet been dispatched to his empty stomach; the sender’s name got there first and woke him instead.

He opened it. The subject line was impeccably polite:

S.W Studio · guest invitation: Red Velvet · performance-intent confirmation · reply requested by 3:00 p.m.

01 — Whether Red Velvet holds performance intent for the engagement. CONFIRM

02 — Whole-group availability across the coming two weeks for rehearsal, recording, and the performance itself. CONFIRM

03 — Performable set list, annotated with live-vocal ratio, dance version, and stage duration. CONFIRM

04 — Expected headcount: management, styling, hair and make-up, and accompanying staff. CONFIRM

05 — Licensing boundaries: broadcast, press photography, overseas re-upload, and official behind-the-scenes material. CONFIRM

06 — Failure to confirm intent within the stated window will be read as unavailability, and the waitlist advances automatically. NOTE

07 — This concert is a free fan-reciprocity event; all guest announcements are made solely by S.W Studio. No advance disclosure. BINDING

Signature: Baek Eun-ah, S.W Studio — Artist Management, General Coordinator. Between the lines, in letters of fire: I am giving you hours, not days. The queue behind you has shoes on.

Kim Tae-su read it twice, then set the triangle gimbap down, uneaten.

An invitation at this level, written in a tone that spelled out your window is measured in hours, was not something an ordinary PR employee could resolve by clicking “read.”

The printout reached the artist strategy planning room within the hour. The room was already in session — and the agenda, at that exact moment, was how to get Red Velvet installed as the guest in place of EXO.

On the projector, frozen mid-refresh, was the poll page from the S.W Studio official account.

SM Entertainment · strategy room projector · the S.W Studio guest poll, live

1. IU

2. Taylor

3. EXO

Below third place: the general population of the industry, fighting in a pile.

Under any normal year, EXO’s vote count should have been oppressive. Even now — with the returning-members situation in full uproar and the fandom fighting a daily transnational proxy war on its own forums — the more turbulent the times, the harder the loyalists fight. A vote like this was never a vote to them. It was a proof of life. Proof the brothers were still here. Proof the group had not fallen.

And that was precisely the problem. Send EXO to a free festival crowd at this exact temperature, and the first fan friction — the first cheering-section dispute, the first perceived camera-slight — would flip the next morning’s headline from “Baek Si-on Thanks His Fans” to “EXO Fans Unrest at Free Concert.” Upstage the host at his own party, and you have made an enemy for a decade.

Which was when the door knocked, and Kim Tae-su came in with the printout and handed it to the PR director.

The director read. His eyebrows climbed with the page.

He passed it to the planning director beside him, who gave it a few lines and produced a whistle.

“Well.”

He handed it onward down the table.

“What do they call this? You search high and low, and it walks in the door?”

“I wouldn’t put it that way.”

The PR director tapped the travelling document.

“We play by their rules.”

“Which rules?”

“Vote-rigging.”

“Ahem. Let’s not be crude. It’s called fan mobilization.”

…

Seoul outskirts. The grand opening of a large-chain supermarket.

November 26 · 11:20 a.m. Second commercial gig of the day · wind at full commitment A temporary stage, a new supermarket, somewhere past the city edge On television, being an idol is lights, cheers, and pretty faces. From the stage, it is November wind, a grand opening, and an uncle with a shopping bag who takes one photo on his way past to the eggs.

The four of Red Velvet stood on a hastily assembled stage. Below it: the supermarket’s main entrance, two rows of congratulatory flower baskets, and several staff members in plush mascot costumes waving heroically at passing children.

The late-November wind had no loyalty at all. It declined to blow on the audience. It blew exclusively on idols.

the commercial-gig uniform · four sets, zero insulation grand opening · chair dance

The blazers Thin single-breasted suit jackets in debut-season navy, worn over white stage shirts with the collars buttoned to the throat. Cut for lighting rigs and camera flashes; tested, today, against a wind that files in from the parking lot without an appointment. The four silhouettes stay crisp anyway — shoulder lines sharp, hems disciplined — because the one thing four years of monthly evaluations can install in a body is posture that survives cold.
The shoes Practice-room heels, worn to the exact millimeter where choreography stops arguing with gravity. On a wooden temporary stage with a suspect seam down the middle, they are less footwear than a trust exercise.

Navy suits, white shirts, November wind — and the ending pose held anyway.

Idols, in the television version, are light and cheering. In the field version, the job description adds: hold the final pose while the wind audits the group’s thermal reserves, and smile like the eggs next door aren’t winning.

The performance ended. The four bowed.

“Thank you, everyone — we are Red Velvet!”

Scattered applause. The host took the microphone and delivered several sentences of opening-ceremony gratitude with genuine enthusiasm. The crowd dispersed at impressive speed, the supermarket’s eggs outranking the new girl group without breaking a sweat.

The four hunched their shoulders, shivering in a tight formation, and crammed themselves into the van. The door slid open; warm air hit them like a physical blessing.

Park Soo-young jammed her hands directly in front of the heater vent.

“I’m alive.”

Son Seung-wan muttered something in her native language.

Their manager sat in the driver’s seat, pressed the heated-seat button, and looked back at them.

“Good news.”

The back row went silent at once. Four heads visibly switched modes, from frozen small animal to survival-mode rookie.

Park Soo-young spoke first.

“Is today a short day?”

“Dream on.”

The manager’s mercy was instantaneous and total in its absence. Park Soo-young withdrew the hope, re-collapsed into the seat, and went back to being a puddle.

“Then what’s the good news?”

Kang Seul-gi asked carefully, rubbing her hands together.

They were all half-delirious with fatigue. Before debut, they had assumed being an idol meant glamour and adoration. The reality: thin suits, outdoor commercial gigs in the wind, appearance fees docked at source to repay trainee-era costs, year-end stage rehearsals at night, three hours of sleep in the dorm, and then the whole loop again.

And Bae Joo-hyun, as leader, carried an extra shift none of the others could see in full: psychological triage for the younger ones — keeping four smiles camera-ready under pressure from the top floors, resistance from fandoms that had never accepted their debut, and comment sections that treated cruelty as a hobby. On her own worst nights, the anxious ones, the ones where she could feel the company’s patience becoming a consumable resource, she had sat leaning against the practice room’s cold mirror with a notebook, copying out the lyrics of “Legend” line after line — never stop until we become legend — writing it until the sentence held her up and the next day arrived.

“Here’s the thing.”

The manager adjusted the rear-view mirror and said it slowly.

“The company just received an official invitation from S.W Studio.”

Four heads leaned forward simultaneously.

The manager paused, then threw the answer out.

“You will be appearing as guests at senior Baek Si-on’s concert.”

The van stayed quiet for half a second.

Then it detonated.

Son Seung-wan went straight to her native tongue:

“Oh my god! Oh my god! Are you serious?!”

Park Soo-young covered her mouth, eyes at the exact diameter they reach when someone steals her tower mid-game.

“Really?! Really really?!”

Kang Seul-gi, half a beat behind everyone as usual, froze — and then broke into a helpless, glowing grin.

Bae Joo-hyun, deepest in the back seat, exhaled slowly. The breath fogged a small circle on the window glass and faded. She pressed her cheek with her hand, confirming she wasn’t hallucinating from the cold.

Then the unnameable thing rose slowly inside her.

Strange. Not the fireworks kind of joy. Not the bragging kind either. More like — standing in a very cold night, and someone laying a coat over your shoulders without a word.

She had vaguely felt it before: being near senior Baek Si-on seemed to make things luckier.

Only now was she beginning to understand it correctly. It was not that getting close brought luck along. It was that luck itself had started walking toward her.

The feeling was odd. A little like the opening panel of a shoujo manga. Although, to be fair, in shoujo manga the heroine is generally not required to shiver through a supermarket parking lot first.

“Then…”

Bae Joo-hyun finally found her voice again and cleared her throat.

“Which song are we performing?”

The manager looked at her through the mirror.

“Right — I should brief you on that. The word I got is that this invitation happened because of Sulli-sunbaenim’s recommendation.”

“So the stage the company has chosen is ‘Chu~?’.”

The other three exchanged a glance. The logic wasn’t hard to decode. If Sulli-sunbaenim had made the introduction, then performing her old group’s signature song at the concert repays the favor and manufactures a warm same-agency-family storyline in one move. Very clean.

Bae Joo-hyun caught a different detail.

“Are Sulli-sunbaenim and senior Baek Si-on close?”

The moment it left her mouth she heard how unnecessary the question was. If they weren’t close, why would Sulli-sunbaenim have made the introduction? If they weren’t close, why would that side have agreed?

And yet she still wanted it confirmed.

The manager searched back through memory.

“To what degree exactly, I honestly don’t know.”

“But I heard it from Sulli’s manager — a while back, Sulli-sunbaenim went to Baek Si-on-sunbaenim’s filming set in person. Sent over a support food truck.”

The words had no sooner landed than the other three ignited.

“Whoa!”

“She went to the set HERSELF?!”

“Are they actually — is there something?!”

“There were rumors before, right?”

“Wasn’t it officially denied, though?”

“And denials are always true?”

Bae Joo-hyun turned her head and looked at them. Slowly.

The interior temperature of the van dropped by two degrees. Three heads went silent mid-syllable.

She turned back to the window. Her own expressionless face looked back from the glass.

She couldn’t have said why. The little floating thing that had lifted off inside her at “Chu~?” — the shoujo-manga opening, the coat on the shoulders — something pressed it gently back down.

The dance was still hers to do. She would do it properly, better than properly.

But the feather stopped floating. That was all.

Park Soo-young leaned over and whispered:

“Eonni. Are you upset?”

Bae Joo-hyun reassembled her face at professional speed.

“No.”

“But your face is really cold.”

“I was cold already.”

“Oh.”

Park Soo-young nodded, accepting this completely.

“That tracks.”

Bae Joo-hyun: “……”

She experienced a brief, vivid urge to put this maknae out of the van and let the wind have her for ten minutes.

“When we get back, straight to the practice room.”

The manager had already started the car. A wail went up from the back.

“More practice? Now?”

“Of course practice.”

“But we just danced. In the cold.”

“At senior Baek Si-on’s concert — do you want to perform the supermarket-grand-opening version in thin suits and a freezing wind?”

“……”

A few seconds passed. Then Kang Seul-gi said, very quietly:

“…Then let’s practice.”

Son Seung-wan raised her hand.

“Will we sing live? Full mics?”

The manager met her eyes in the mirror.

“Naturally.”

The van drove toward Apgujeong. Baek Si-on’s name sat in the car like a lamp switched on suddenly in a dark room — the kind of light that makes people nervous, and makes them want to walk toward it.

Chapter One Hundred Eighty Nine

Pulling a Big Job with Kobe

January 21 → February 20 · the eastern swing, one hospital-adjacent detour, and the Gulf A push alert that stopped a man mid-recovery · a thermos of samgyetang argued into a patient · a fist tapped once, a partnership concluded · fifteen thousand seven hundred quiet coins · an oud out of the dark · a wall of vinyl called “a little” · one of seven cars · and the guitar that actually wrote music history Seattle / Boston → Newport Coast → Dubai → Doha A machine that cannot stop receives news it cannot act on; two men who each own too much of two different worlds conclude a deal with one knuckle; the desert opens its eyes on schedule, twice; and the Middle East finishes its bidding war in the only currency that outlasts wheels.

The tour kept moving east. Seattle rained without pause, and the queue outside the arena still reached the street corner by mid-afternoon. Boston made Baek Eun-ah briefly suspect the entire audience had completed a group training course in Harvard library etiquette.

Every city had its own temperament. Baek Si-on’s schedule did not. It had only a dense weave of airports, hotels, rehearsals, interviews, shows, post-mortems, and the next flight out.

January 21. The ESPN push alert lit up his phone the moment Baek Si-on walked off a rehearsal stage.

ESPN · push alert · one line, read four times anyway

KOBE BRYANT — torn rotator cuff. Season likely over.

Delivered 9:41 a.m. local. Opened instantly. Closed without a reply, because push alerts do not take replies.

He stared at the line for a few seconds. Said nothing.

Baek Eun-ah, standing beside him, read his expression and leaned in for the screen herself.

“Kobe?”

“Mm.”

“Are you going to go see him?”

Baek Si-on pocketed the phone.

“Can’t. Not now.”

He had a show that night. A flight to the next city the day after. The tour machine had started, and every gear of it was locked into the timetable — not something that stops because he says so.

But Baek Eun-ah knew him very well.

“I’ll ask Scooter where we can squeeze out a day.”

“Mm.”

Squeezing out a day, translated, meant prying one more crack into an itinerary already compressed to its structural limit. It landed on January 26 — the recovery window after Kobe’s surgery.

❧ ❧ ❧

Newport Coast was wearing generous sunshine. Baek Si-on got out of the car carrying an insulated pot. Inside it: samgyetang.

Kobe sat on the living-room sofa, right shoulder braced and strapped, color in his face not too bad. He saw the pot first, and his brow creased before anything else could.

“What’s that?”

“Samgyetang.”

“Chicken soup?”

“Korean patient food.”

Kobe looked down at his braced right shoulder.

“Baek. My shoulder’s blown. I’m not pregnant.”

Baek Si-on set the pot on the table.

“Koreans believe most problems in life can at least be warmed over with hot soup first.”

Kobe stared at him for two seconds.

“Including a torn rotator cuff?”

“Not that one.”

“Then why’d you bring it?”

“Moral support.”

“……”

Kobe laughed once. Took the bowl anyway, and drank. The hot soup went down easy; the chicken was stewed soft; the ginseng was faint — stranger in his imagination than on his tongue.

“Not bad.”

“Thank you for the praise.”

“I said not bad.”

“For a patient, that’s a rave.”

Kobe laughed again. And when the laugh was done, his gaze slid back to the television. The Lakers replay was on. The Kobe on screen was still on the floor — turn, drive, force the shot.

This season’s Lakers had, in practice, stopped having standings goals. The roster was aging, the injuries kept stacking, and both management and fans understood that “rebuild” was a when, not an if. Tanking. Draft picks. Future. In the basketball world these were level-headed words.

Kobe did not accept them. Since coming back he had played the way he had always played. The problem was that the body was no longer the old body — so the right shoulder had surprised no one, least of all him.

He looked into the soup bowl, and his voice came out lower than before.

“Doctor says nine months. Can’t touch a ball.”

Baek Si-on didn’t answer. At a moment like this, “you’ll be fine” is cheap, and Kobe was not a man who needed cheap words smeared over anything.

Kobe took another sip, watched the man on the television force up another shot, and sat with it for a while.

“Baek.”

“Mm?”

“My body is sending me warnings.”

He pulled at one corner of his mouth. It resembled a smile. It wasn’t one.

“Maybe… the time I’ve got left really isn’t that much.”

Baek Si-on looked at the braced shoulder and suddenly remembered the emotion that had ambushed him while recording ‘See You Again’ — a song written for someone already gone. Some farewells, it turned out, started hurting well before they happened.

“If you really do stop playing — what then? Supervise the BodyArmor assembly line every day?”

This time Kobe laughed for real.

“God, no. That’s too boring. Maybe I’d do some investing.”

“Mm.”

“Make a few films.”

“You’d want to write the scripts yourself.”

“Obviously. Nobody else writes my story.”

Very Kobe. Baek Si-on registered zero surprise. Kobe paused, then added:

“Or — if the money’s ever enough — I want to buy an NBA team.”

“You know how I am.”

Kobe said it evenly.

“I can’t leave the game.”

“But teams are too expensive now.”

His voice snapped back into its realistic register almost immediately.

“Last year Steve Ballmer paid two billion for the Clippers. One check, and the whole league’s valuations doubled behind it.”

Kobe shook his head.

“On what a player saves from playing? Nowhere near the big chair.”

That wasn’t humility. It was arithmetic. NBA stars are rich — but set beside the people who actually buy teams, most of them are just the best-paid employees in the building. Ballmer’s hammer, in particular, had rewritten the price of admission to the owners’ box in one swing.

Baek Si-on took a sip of water and began, quietly, to calculate. The fifteen thousand seven hundred–odd bitcoins he had put in last year were still lying there, silent. To any observer they still looked like a pile of unstable electronic chips. But Baek Si-on knew: two, three more years, and that pile stops being chips and becomes an absurd cash flow.

Notes · a mental ledger, Newport Coast

15,700-odd bitcoins. Acquired last year. Current status: sleeping — reads to the room as unstable electronic chips.

Two to three years out: the kind of cash flow that buys a voice at the table.

Disclosure status: filed under “digital assets.” Pending the listener’s heart rate.

“I’m holding a digital asset.”

Baek Si-on said it abruptly. Kobe turned his head.

“A digital asset?”

“Mm.”

“What kind of thing?”

“The explanation is a little inconvenient right now.”

“Sounds like a scam.”

“……”

Kobe laughed once.

“How much?”

“Not much yet.”

“Then why are you telling me?”

Baek Si-on looked at him.

“In two or three years, that digital asset might turn into a considerable cash flow.”

Kobe’s eyes narrowed.

“How considerable?”

Baek Si-on offered no number.

“Considerable enough to cover your voice, once you’ve bought into a team.”

The living room went quiet. Kobe studied him — openly weighing whether he was serious, or whether three hundred shows in two months had finally knocked something loose.

“Baek. Do you know what an NBA team costs?”

“Yes.”

“No. You don’t.”

“I know about the Clippers.”

“And you still dare say it?”

“I didn’t say buy the whole team. I said voice.”

The smile on Kobe’s face receded by a degree. This was no longer crazy talk. Buying a franchise outright was one category of thing. Holding enough of it to move decisions was another category entirely.

He looked at Baek Si-on for a few seconds. Then he put out the uninjured left fist.

“Baek. If what you’re saying is true —”

“The day I retire, you and me, we pull one big job.”

“I run the basketball and the management.”

“You run sitting in the VIP box, watching the games.”

Baek Si-on looked at the extended fist. Reached out and tapped it, lightly.

“Deal.”

A beat.

“Heal well.”

“Future team owner.”

Kobe looked down at the two fists that had touched, and smiled — and this time the smile finally didn’t carry so much shadow in it.

The day I retire, we pull one big job. I run the basketball. You run the VIP box.

— Newport Coast, one fist tapped once, contract concluded

“You’d better not be lying to me.”

“I only ever lie fans into going home.”

Kobe blinked.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

Baek Si-on stood to leave. Kobe leaned back into the sofa and said, suddenly:

“Baek.”

“Mm?”

“The soup wasn’t bad.”

Baek Si-on nodded.

“Next time I’ll bring kimchi.”

The smile on Kobe’s face vanished on the spot.

“You don’t have to terminate the partnership this fast.”

“Then after we buy the team.”

“……”

Kobe concluded, privately, that if he really did end up sharing an owners’ box with this Korean, it might do more damage to his blood pressure than any game he’d ever watched from the bench.

❧ ❧ ❧

Baek Si-on had honestly stopped keeping count of his own flights. Thirty-odd shows deep into a run, a person’s life compresses into a mechanical loop — extremely efficient, profoundly anti-human.

February 20. The Middle East dates arrived. Dubai.

From the moment the plane broke through the clouds, the word “low-key” ceased to be an available concept. Even on a second viewing, the place still landed like a slam.

❧ ❧ ❧

When the Dubai show properly began, the atmosphere inside the venue ran at a completely different temperature from North America. North American audiences scream, jump, switch on their phone lights, and sing along until their voices split. Dubai’s front rows were far more restrained. Royals, magnates, the politically adjacent — seated in the best areas, flanked by security, aides, and equally expensive perfume.

It wasn’t that they weren’t enthusiastic. It was that their manner of expressing enthusiasm resembled waiting — for one moment worth admitting that the evening had been worth it.

The moment came mid-set. Every light in the venue died at once. The big screen went black with them. A few seconds later, a thin oud line rose from somewhere deep in the building. Then low drums, one beat at a time. On the screen, black dune ridgelines were drawn slowly in edges of gold — and far away, a city lit itself up out of the night.

Baek Si-on walked out of the dark at exactly that moment. There was no clumsy costume-regionalism about the stage clothes. He was still Baek Si-on. It was only that, in this minute, the light chose to make him look like a traveler who had walked in from the far end of the sand sea and into the city.

The song title appeared on the screen: — Desert Rose.

‘Desert Rose’ · Dubai · first performance, mid-set blackout

“No one planted me. The storm did”

“I never learned to bloom in gardens — I survive in sand”

“The night is long, and the wind knows my name”

“Every thorn I keep was a rain that never came”

— and then the chorus, as the dunes on screen turned to moving gold —

“When the last light drowns the dunes”

“You’ll find the rose the desert chose”

The lyrics weren’t complicated. They were about a desert. About the night. About a rose that was never grown in any garden — it was found, out in the wind-blown sand. Before the chorus, the strings opened slowly; on the screen the dunes became a flowing sea of gold, and the silhouette of the Burj Khalifa surfaced out of the night.

When the chorus landed, the Crown Prince in the front row finally smiled. By the time the song ended, the applause bursting through the venue had stopped being restrained. Several young royals in white robes stood straight up. Baek Si-on stood at center stage and bowed, slightly. The light came down on him like the desert lending him its gold.

❧ ❧ ❧

After the show, the Dubai Crown Prince fought his way into the backstage corridor with his people.

“Baek!”

He opened both arms and delivered an embrace enthusiastic to the point of exaggeration.

“Tonight, you are not a singer.”

“Then what am I?”

“You are the Sultan of the desert!”

Baek Si-on: “……Thank you.”

The Prince was in far too good a mood to consider releasing him back to his hotel.

“Come. I’ll show you something.”

And so, half an hour later, Baek Si-on stood inside a private residence in Dubai. The word “residence” honestly undersold it — the place was closer to a hotel, a museum, a car gallery, and a small palace pressed forcibly into one body, every remaining seam caulked shut with money.

The Prince walked him down a long corridor. Art on the walls. Limited watches in lit cases. And then a dedicated music collection room.

The door swung open. Baek Si-on stopped.

An entire wall — floor to ceiling — was his Human. On vinyl.

The Prince was entirely candid about it.

“I bought a little.”

Baek Si-on looked at the wall.

“This is ‘a little’?”

The Prince considered the question.

“For me.”

“……”

Excellent. The scale of language, it turns out, adjusts itself to the scale of the bank account behind it.

❝

Lexical scale is a function of bank balance. The same word — “little” — measures a few hundred won in Seoul and several hundred kilograms of vinyl on the Gulf.

— the music room, on language inflation

Baek Si-on felt a brief, sincere apology toward the Korean fans who had lost the vinyl lottery months ago. The market hadn’t been unfair. A Middle Eastern prince had simply launched an annihilation campaign with purchasing power, early, and against civilians.

The Prince was visibly proud of the wall.

“I like physical collections.”

“Digital music is too light.”

“Vinyl has weight.”

Baek Si-on nodded. It did, in fact, have weight. A respectable fraction of fifty thousand copies was probably pressing against that wall at this very moment.

Next, the Prince took him to the garage. A row of hypercars sat in the light, silent. Red. Black. Silver. Each one looked like the phrase “not practical in any sense” carved into physical matter by an industrial designer with a grudge.

The Prince walked to one of them. Red body, lines ground sharp — a ruby cut into the shape of a blade. Baek Si-on knew this car. The Lykan from Furious 7 — a vehicle which, by certain measures, had played a bigger lead in that film than half its human cast.

the garage · the gift, itemized

Vehicle: Lykan HyperSport · red · ruby, blade profile

Screen credit: Furious 7 — by some measures, more of a lead actor than half the human cast

Production run: seven. Worldwide. This one: yours.

Handover conducted with the warmth of a man buying a friend coffee — except this coffee is called “one of seven.”

The Prince looked at him, smiling.

“Baek — I hear you sang the theme for Furious 7.”

“This is the same model they used in the film.”

“It’s yours.”

Baek Si-on: “……”

His first reaction was not joy. It was a vivid mental image of the Hyundai Motor PR department suffering a synchronized cardiac event. If he drove a Lykan through Los Angeles tomorrow, the day after next Hyundai’s marketing director would fly in from Seoul and threaten to open a vein with somebody else’s luxury car key. Their global ambassador’s private car outpricing the entire Hyundai lineup combined was, for brand PR, less an incident than an entire genre of incident.

But declining was not available. The Prince wasn’t handing over a normal gift. This was how a nation-level tycoon expressed affection — the way an ordinary person treats a friend to coffee, except their coffee is called “one of seven in the world.”

Baek Si-on was silent a moment. Then he nodded.

“Thank you.”

The Prince laughed, satisfied.

“You deserve it.”

Baek Si-on regarded the supercar he was now permanently forbidden from driving casually, and his feelings refused to file themselves under any single heading. The most awkward gift in the world is the one where you know perfectly well how obscene the price tag is — and your very first thought is still:

Where do I put this thing?

❧ ❧ ❧

The Doha date was scheduled after Dubai. The flight was short. The temperament was not the same. Dubai was a golden hallucination forcibly erected out of desert. Doha was quieter. The bay. White buildings. A crescent. The city’s night lights lay reflected on the water like a silver ribbon a wind had wrinkled.

Qatar, plainly, already knew what Dubai had done. From the moment Baek Si-on landed, every smile on the reception team carried the same unmistakable subtitle: we are ready too.

The first second of the ‘Starboy’ intro, the Qatari royals in the front rows were already sitting up straight. This was not a rose in a desert night. This was a golden city under a black one. The drums hit hard. The bass was dirty. The synths carried a finish that was cold and luxurious at once. On the screen: the bay, a lighthouse, white buildings, yachts, glass curtain walls — and the abstract sweep of black cards sliding past.

Baek Si-on stood at center stage with the light splitting open behind him. In that moment he was no longer the Sultan of the desert. He was something closer to a tyrant enshrined by money.

‘Starboy’ · Doha · the chorus that read the front row like a statement

“Look what you’ve done”

“I’m a motherfuckin’ starboy”

— two lines in, several young royals stopped taking instruction from their own faces —

“Look what you’ve done”

“I’m a motherfuckin’ starboy”

The lyrics never once said Qatar out loud. Every line still managed to speak directly to this exact section of seats. By the chorus, several of the young royals had entirely lost control of their expressions. And when the last note of ‘Starboy’ ended, the sound that detonated through the venue was more direct than Dubai’s. Younger. And carrying the very specific high of people who have just had their own secret read back to them from a stage.

After the show, the Qatari royal representative met him backstage, and his opening sentence carried undisguised satisfaction:

“Baek — that Dubai crowd always thinks the faster the wheels, the better the gift.”

Baek Si-on did not take the bait. Intra-regional rivalry: outsiders are advised not to pick up a side.

Behind the representative, an attendant stepped forward bearing a long, rectangular instrument case. When the lid opened, Baek Si-on’s expression changed — genuinely changed — for the first time that night.

An old Gibson acoustic guitar. It was not ornate. Set against a Lykan currently being crated for Korea, it looked plain as something found in the back room of an antique shop.

But Baek Si-on knew whose it had been.

— John Lennon’s.

The Gibson that had been lost for half a century — the acoustic guitar that wrote countless early Beatles melodies. Recently rediscovered, and taken at auction for 2.4 million dollars. And now it sat, opened, in front of Baek Si-on.

The representative watched his face and was, evidently, very satisfied.

“Dubai gave you the car from the movie.”

“We are giving you the thing that actually wrote music history.”

Baek Si-on looked down at the guitar. The Lykan was expensive — materially expensive. But this carried time, music, and a provenance that could not be re-manufactured by any amount of money. To a musician, that is harder to refuse than any supercar ever built.

He looked at it for a few seconds, then reached out and touched the body, lightly.

“This gift is too heavy.”

The representative smiled.

“That is what makes it worthy of tonight.”

Baek Si-on looked up at him.

“Thank you.”

He said it more earnestly than he had said it in the Dubai garage — and the representative plainly heard the difference. He nodded, satisfied.

“We are told you are a man who does not like being defined as only a singer.”

“Whatever you become —”

“this guitar will remind you:”

“some things only music gets to leave behind.”

“……”

Dubai had given Baek Si-on a car of which seven exist in the world. Qatar gave him a guitar of which one exists.

❝

One of seven against one of one. The Gulf bids in the only currency that appreciates.

— the tour office, on the Middle East double-header

And so the Middle East double-header concluded — in a manner that was very Middle Eastern, and completely absurd.
